### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:09:00
claim: A business can change its market in five directions: up-market, down-market, adjacent, broader or narrower.
detail: Gym Launch examples: up-market is multi-location owners and franchisers; down-market is trainers (many more of them, less buying power, forming a pyramid); adjacent is chiropractors; broader is health and wellness in general (med spas, weight-loss clinics); narrower is a single type such as spin or dance studios. Small owners rarely define this and accept any customer with a card, which confuses buyers about what the firm is for.
applies_to: Owners deciding where to grow after a niche is won.

### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: More Better New | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:31
claim: A competitor entering the market does not cut your share the way people imagine, because you use one slice of one channel of one platform.
detail: Visual argument: draw the whole attention market as many circles (each social platform, radio, TV, search, direct mail, email, communities), then outreach as a second set (DMs on each platform, calls, mail), then format variants within a platform (posts, stories, carousels, shorts, long video). Most owners occupy a sliver. When leads are short, ask how to produce content in more places, more of it, better, then add ads and outreach.

### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:33
claim: "My market is saturated" usually means the advertiser cannot reach people at a higher level of awareness.
detail: Example: a statement that ads cannot scale past $1,000 a day. His reading: ads that only speak to people who already understand the solution cap spend early, while a larger market sits above that awareness level. A market is truly too small only in extreme cases (140 people in a remote area). In a city of 400,000 to a million, needing 200 customers is fine and something else is the constraint.

### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#05 | TACTIC | topic: verticals | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:09
claim: Local businesses have three ways to grow beyond a single site: expand the existing location, add channels, then open another location.
detail: Expanding the location: his first gym was crowded, so he cut classes from 45 to 30 minutes (1.5x as many classes), removed gaps between classes and opened longer hours. Channels: in an A or B market one channel can reach about $1M; in a C or D market (under about 50,000 people within 10 miles) one channel is not enough. Third, replicate in new markets; he argues $10M a year is reachable in nearly any model this way.
numbers: Class length 45 to 30 minutes = 1.5x classes; small-market threshold under ~50,000 people within 10 miles; $1M per year via one channel in larger local markets.
applies_to: Single-location local services (gyms, studios).

### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Depth-then-Width | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:16:13
claim: Switching to a new opportunity resets your growth clock, so you stay behind the one you left even if the new one grows faster at first.
detail: Comparison: at year three in the current business versus year one in a new one. Even if the new one grows faster, when it is in year two the old one is in year four, and growth is easier when larger. Stay unless the core assumption is wrong or the shortfall is not skill. His "one avatar, one product, one channel until $1M a year" rule is restated, with exceptions for small local markets.
numbers: Rule: one avatar, one product, one channel until ~$1M a year.

### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#07 | FRAMEWORK | topic: verticals | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:17:57
claim: Each business model has a characteristic growth shape and a built-in hard part, so hitting it does not mean the business is broken.
detail: Info and coaching: fast start, hard to scale, almost no revenue retention (learners graduate). Software: slow and unprofitable at first, hard to hire engineers, then scales fast. E-commerce: scales fast but strained by inventory cash, supply chain and logistics changes. Services: slowest but steady, dependent on hiring, onboarding and training. Weight-loss services find staff easily but customers cancel; accounting firms find staff hard but customers stay. Check whether your constraint matches your industry's.

### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#08 | DECISION_RULE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: operator-mindset | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:40
claim: If your constraint is the inverse of your industry's usual one, you are usually doing something either very right or very wrong.
detail: Example: a cleaning company that says it could take 100 times more customers than it has. Either it has solved attracting cleaners and is about to hyperscale, or it has not started. Most of the time the owner matches the industry norm and, being inexperienced, mistakes that for a sign something is wrong, then spends years half-committed.
### YT-hnmBXTyMFKI#09 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: operator-mindset | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:23:45
claim: Rewrite outside-blaming statements as "I do not have the skill to..." to find what you can change.
detail: Replace "no good salespeople in my market" or "the market is crowded" (usually meaning leads are scarce or costly) with "I lack the skill to get more leads" or "to hire and manage a salesperson". Once phrased as a skill you own the outcome and can fix it.
