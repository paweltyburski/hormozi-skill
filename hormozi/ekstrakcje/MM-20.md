### MM-20#01 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:22
claim: Hormozi entered a suit shop with a $500 budget, was shown a $16,000 suit first, and happily left having spent about $2,500–3,000.
detail: Advised to look professional when starting Gym Launch (around 26, after years in gym clothes), he went to a friend-referred shop. The owner, a third-generation tailor, put him in a great suit; the tag read $16,000 and he went pale. Seeing it, the owner asked, "Do you care much about the brand?" — "No, I just want to look cool." A near-identical suit was already prepared: about $2,000–2,200, which felt like relief. He added $300–500 of shirts and socks he'd never normally buy — 5–6× his budget, all of it feeling cheap next to $16,000.
numbers: Budget $500; anchor $16,000; spent ≈ $2,500–3,000.

### MM-20#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Price Anchoring | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:37
claim: Rolls-Royce moved from car shows to plane and yacht shows, where a $400,000 car looks small next to a $50 million jet.
detail: Story attributed to Rory Sutherland: beside other cars the price looks egregious; after passing on a jet it feels like candy. Price is always judged relative to what it sits next to.

### MM-20#03 | DEFINITION | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:25
claim: An anchor upsell shows a premium item first at 5–10× the price; when the customer balks, the main offer — same primary features, cheaper secondary ones — looks like a great deal.
detail: Showing the premium first makes the main offer most people buy look better, so more buy it. Every product has primary and secondary features; vary only the secondary. The cheaper suit looked and felt just as good — only the brand, which he didn't care about, differed — so at about one-eighth the price it felt like a steal. The menu upsell is elegant and suits high-volume transaction businesses; the anchor is brute force for businesses with fewer, bigger spenders and can earn more (00:05:10).

### MM-20#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:10
claim: Anchors pay twice: main-offer buyers spend more than planned, and a minority buy the premium — one in ten buying a 10× offer doubles revenue.
detail: If one in five bought the $16,000 suit, that adds about $3,000 to every average sale, making a $2,000 suit effectively a $5,000 sale. The premium sells the least but can contribute outsized profit (00:12:38).
numbers: 1 in 5 at $16K ≈ +$3,000 per sale; 1 in 10 at 10× = 2× revenue.

### MM-20#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:01
claim: Five steps: show the anchor, let the gasp happen, ride in as the rescuer, present the real offer, ask how they'll pay.
detail: (1) Show the most expensive version. (2) Expect the gasp, and want it. (3) Rescue: ask whether they care about the premium elements — secondary features such as material or lining that most buyers ignore. (4) Present the main offer; they feel relieved and see the bargain. (5) "Which card do you prefer?"

### MM-20#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:01
claim: The anchor must be an order of magnitude higher — 5–10× — and no gasp means it wasn't high enough or wasn't sold earnestly.
detail: 20% more is not an anchor; it can't be within 100% of the main price. It must make the buyer recategorise the purchase, as yachts recategorise a car (00:10:19).
numbers: Anchor 5–10× main; main at roughly 1/5–1/10 of anchor.

### MM-20#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:46
claim: Worse than making a $1,000 offer to someone with $100 is making a $100 offer to someone with $1,000 — so always have premium offers ready.
detail: The first loses $100, the second $900. Hormozi lost many customers who wanted more than he offered. Few buy premium, but they bring big profit; offering it first loses no customers.

### MM-20#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: money-models | also: verticals | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:32
claim: Lawn care: a $1,000/week package with the owner's cell, fancy mulch and natural pest control makes the $200/week standard an easy yes — a price that used to get haggled down.
detail: Main package: crew chief's number, generic mulch, normal pest control, same biweekly maintenance. The yard looks about the same; soil enthusiasts buy the anchor, ~80% take $200. Without the anchor, $200 got haggled to $150 or $99 a week.
numbers: $1,000 vs $200/week; ~80% indifferent.

### MM-20#09 | EXAMPLE | topic: money-models | also: verticals | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:35
claim: Painting and newsletter anchors use premium packaging, insurance or early access to make the standard version feel like a steal.
detail: Painting: protective packaging, 20-year insurance and gift wrap for $1,000 vs normal packaging, one-year insurance and a sticker for $200 — buyers feel they're outsmarting you, but you designed the game. Newsletter: all back issues plus 24-hour early access at ~$199/month (stated "$1.99", unclear in audio) vs new issues for $19/month, in a market previously at $7/month.

### MM-20#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process, sales-team | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:26
claim: Present the anchor in earnest — treat it as fake and so will the customer — because their effort to justify the price makes the main offer feel cheap.
detail: A seller saying "probably out of your range" comes across as a little insulting (Hormozi's own experience as a buyer); a casual "no big deal if it's not in your range" is the example he says would have left him anchored. When they truly consider it, their brain justifies the price; then a version at one-tenth feels obviously worth it. Staff often skim the anchor because they sell from their own wallet ("I'd never pay that") — but the work is easy only for them, which is why it's a business.

### MM-20#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: money-models | also: product-delivery | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:19
claim: Anchor only with a premium you actually want to sell and can deliver — often more than one in ten buy it.
detail: Owners who build a genuine 10× package are regularly surprised by how many take it; be ready to service them.

claim: If the customer doesn't flinch at the anchor, close the whale and keep adding.
detail: "Great — card on file? Want a handkerchief with that?"

### MM-20#13 | DECISION_RULE | topic: money-models | also: offers | fw: Primary vs Secondary Features | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:50
claim: Keep the main offer as close to the anchor as possible, changing only secondary features; changing primary ones forces buyers to recalculate value.
detail: They already judged value at the anchor price; reuse that judgement so the main offer reads as 80–90% off. Remove things they care about and they must re-evaluate, breaking the effect.
