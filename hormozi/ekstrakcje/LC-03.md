### LC-03#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: offers | fw: Lead Magnet Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: A lead magnet is built in seven moves: pick who and what problem, decide how to solve it, decide how to deliver it, name it, make it easy to consume, make it good, and create the next step.
detail: (1) Who and what — the ideal customer and the narrow problem. (2) How to solve — reveal the problem, give a sample, or give one step of many. (3) How to deliver — software, information, service or physical product. (4) Name it — test names; the name drives engagement. (5) Make it easy — every format, every convenient time. (6) Make it good — zero fluff. (7) Next steps — a clear CTA with a reason to act now. Each step ends with an action before the next.

### LC-03#02 | CASE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: content-brand, verticals | fw: Lead Magnet Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Hormozi's webinar for gym owners failed after eight weeks of work, while a short screen-recorded case study ("how we filled this gym") made leads pour in.
detail: On a mentor's advice he spent about eight weeks building a webinar, learning targeting, thank-you pages, carts and payment processing, then spent about $500 on ads: no sales, only opt-ins who never watched (he called and closed some by phone). After seeing another marketer's 13-minute screen-share walking through a campaign, he recorded one: how they filled a San Diego gym with 212 sign-ups in a month — ads, landing page, scheduler, script, sales, photo of the owner — ending "if you want us to do this for you, let us know." Leads poured in. Webinars work for others; the case study suited his avatar and skills. Matching lead magnet to avatar is a major lever.
numbers: ~8 weeks building; ~$500 ad spend, no sales; case-study gym: 212 sign-ups in a month.

### LC-03#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:03
claim: Pitching the core offer cold to a low-trust audience converts a small fraction; a lead magnet first builds trust so the core offer reaches a high-trust group with a much higher take rate.
detail: Illustration with made-up numbers: pitch 25 strangers directly and perhaps one in enough pain buys — it works with volume. With a lead magnet, some take the free thing because it costs only time, which needs less trust than money; some consume it, see you are good, and notice a bigger problem. Having kept your first promise, you pitch a high-trust group. The motive is more money, via trust and value in advance. A straight core offer can still work; he says beginners can sometimes just make one, and this is how he does it.
quote: Less trust is required to give time than money.

### LC-03#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:11
claim: Free salty nuts sell ice-cold water, and Costco's free samples drive purchases — the free thing's cost is recovered many times over.
detail: Costco ran samples for years because they drove sales: you try 20, like the seven-layer bean dip, and buy it — a purchase that would not have happened otherwise. The cost of samples is the cost of delivering the lead magnet. Reciprocity moves people a step closer to buying, not away.

### LC-03#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:13
claim: A person who pays with time now is more likely to pay with money later — so get them to invest time early, and give a high return on it.
detail: That is the whole game of a lead magnet. The time they spend must be rewarded: a bad bean dip sample means no purchase and bad word of mouth. Offer the exceptional kind.

### LC-03#10 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Problem-Solution Cycle | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:58
claim: Every solved problem creates a new one; a lead magnet should solve one problem in a way that makes the next — the one your core offer solves — obvious.
detail: Business cycle: leads → sales problem → delivery → churn → scaling operations → back to needing traffic. Problems never go away; you trade bad problems for better ones (in life too: no girlfriend → girlfriend problems → wife problems). Choose the lead magnet's problem so solving it leads naturally to what you sell.

### LC-03#11 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: verticals | fw: Lead Magnet Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:57
claim: To choose the lead magnet's problem, break your big solution into the micro-problems a customer must solve on the way, and give away the solution to one.
detail: Helping homeowners sell (broad) breaks into: what the house is worth, how to increase its value, photos, cleaning, landscaping, staging. Solve one free; it helps but makes the remaining problem — selling — more obvious. Having kept a promise, you are trusted over competitors and have a foot in the door. Action step: choose who you solve a valuable narrow problem for, and which problem.

### LC-03#12 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: Three Lead Magnet Types | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:19
claim: In Hormozi's review of offers across his past and portfolio companies, the lead magnets basically fell into three types: reveal the problem, give a sample (free trial), or give one step of a multi-step process.
detail: From a review of offers across his past and portfolio companies. (1) Reveal — a diagnosis that makes them aware of a problem without solving it. (2) Sample — they try it; more of the same costs money. (3) One step of many — one step free, the rest paid. Any type can work for any business; ask "how can I make this work here?" not "this won't work for me". Types can combine (a wealth calculator is both first step and diagnosis). Action step: pick one.

### LC-03#13 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: Three Lead Magnet Types | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:54
claim: "Reveal the problem" lead magnets work best for problems that get worse or costlier with time, because revealing them creates built-in urgency.
detail: Examples: a free website audit showing load time versus what it could be; a posture analysis; a termite inspection (delay makes repairs pricier). Once aware of "the snake under the table," they want the next step.

### LC-03#14 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: Three Lead Magnet Types | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:40
claim: A sample is a taste: limit the number of uses, the quantity, the time of access, or a mix, so wanting more requires paying.
detail: Like a toothpick of dip, not the tub. Website speed: host them on your faster server temporarily, show the extra leads, and they pay to keep it. Posture: one adjustment that fixes them temporarily, with lasting results needing repeat sessions. Consumables: fun-size packaging.

### LC-03#15 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: Three Lead Magnet Types | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:43
claim: "One step of many": give away one step of a multi-step solution free so the result makes the prospect want the rest.
detail: Examples: the first of three coats of garage-door sealant; one of ten website-speed fixes ("want to hear about the other nine?"); in a multidisciplinary posture treatment (adjustment, electrical stimulation, massage, stretching), give only the adjustment; first-step templates and calculators; Hormozi's book as a step toward $1M profit.

### LC-03#16 | PRINCIPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:31

### LC-03#17 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: Four Delivery Mechanisms | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:16
claim: Any lead magnet can be delivered as software, information, a service or a physical product — 12 combinations with the three ways of solving.
detail: Software: a tool that does a job (spreadsheet, calculator, dashboard, small app). Information: teach something (courses, lessons, interviews, keynotes, events, mistakes, tips). Services: do the work free. Physical products: something they can hold. Each works with reveal, sample and one-step (3 × 4 = 12). Action step: pick one.

### LC-03#22 | DECISION_RULE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:28
claim: Make the lead magnet as close as possible to what you sell; one that attracts people who want the freebie but not your core offer is wrong for you.
detail: Branded t-shirts are a poor magnet for an IT services firm (wanting shirts is not wanting IT) but a great one for an apparel white-labeler. A lead magnet is right or wrong for your core offer, not good or bad in itself.

detail: The opening changed how viewers framed what they were about to watch. Likewise, a lead magnet's name and positioning matter most for getting leads to engage; how good it is matters more for converting them to buyers.

### LC-03#25 | CASE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:48

### LC-03#26 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: content-brand, paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:28
claim: Test lead magnet names with whatever audience you have: cheap ads, polls on every platform, comment posts, or DMs asking "A or B?"
detail: By audience size: (1) ads with competing names — about $50 gives a usable sample; (2) polls on every platform, summed; (3) posts asking people to comment "1" or "2"; (4) DMs to followers, friends or phone contacts — people asked in earnest answer, giving a directional signal. Best signal: someone picks one and asks when it comes out.
numbers: ~$50 ad spend for a naming test.

So information goes on Kindle, Audible and his podcast (some only use one); software must run on phone and computer, Android and Apple.

### LC-03#28 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:26

### LC-03#29 | PRINCIPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:30:00

### LC-03#30 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:31:04
claim: The market judges you on your free stuff, not your paid stuff — 99.9% of people who see you never buy, but their opinion shapes whether others do.
detail: Make free material so good that non-buyers say everything they got was excellent; that reputation overpowers naysayers. Fluff hurts twice: it discourages would-be buyers, and when friends ask "is this person legit?", those who consumed it say no. Free and paid must both deliver value.

### LC-03#31 | PRINCIPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:33:18
claim: People buy on a prediction of future value, so the easiest way to make them predict you will deliver later is to deliver value before they buy.
detail: At purchase the buyer estimates the probability of getting what they want; giving value before the purchase is, in his words, the easiest way to make them predict it. Make both free and paid offers exceptional.

### LC-03#33 | DEFINITION | topic: offers | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Scarcity and Urgency | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:52
claim: Scarcity limits quantity; urgency limits time — ideally use both, but limiting either element of the offer or CTA lifts demand significantly.
detail: Urgency: an unlimited ebook "no longer available after tomorrow." Scarcity: "only 10 hats," available forever to the first 10.

### LC-03#36 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: unit-economics | fw: Lead Magnet Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:35:42
claim: A great lead magnet passes four tests: ideal customers want it on sight, it gets more people to engage than the core offer alone, it is valuable enough to be consumed, and it makes the right people more likely to buy.
detail: Test 2 is the financial case: more people end up buying for the same effort, lowering cost to acquire a customer. Test 4 means the right people, not everyone — if unqualified people dislike it, fine; repel the bad as much as attract the good (targeting comes later). Result: more interest, more money and a stronger brand at once.
