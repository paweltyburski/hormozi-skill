### SC-03#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: strategy-positioning | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 0 Improvise is one person with no business, whose job is to research and figure out what works; you graduate by getting people to try your stuff for free.
detail: Headcount 1, role researcher (research is how you get clarity), leadership just you, not yet a company. Constraint → graduation step:
- Product: nothing to sell → give something free.
- Marketing: nobody knows → tell people about the free thing.
- Sales: you cannot sell → get people to try it.
- Customer service: no customers → serve the free ones.
- IT: no hardware → computer, internet, microphone, phone, email.
- Recruiting: you do not know how to work → recruit yourself.
- HR: personal assets exposed → form an entity.
- Finance: personal and business money mixed → business bank account, startup write-offs.
Graduating leads to stage 1 Monetize. The roadmap starts at a true zero so nobody is left behind; many viewers will already have done some of these.

### SC-03#02 | DEFINITION | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:31
claim: The roadmap describes each of its eight business functions in terms of what it produces.
detail: - Product: what you deliver — goods, services and outcomes.
- Marketing: how you let people know about your offering.
- Sales: how you persuade people to pay you for it.
- Customer service: how customers are led to refer, retain, review and resell (the four R's).
- IT: how information (contacts, conversations, purchases, feedback) is collected, stored, analyzed and shown.
- Recruiting: how you locate and hire people to work in your company.
- HR: how you lower the odds that employees or regulators take legal action against you (Hormozi's own, intentionally blunt definition).
- Finance: how you report on and manage money.

### SC-03#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: offers | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:31
claim: When you have nothing to sell, start by giving something away free; Hormozi and Leila have done this in every company they have started.
detail: It is a strong recommendation rather than a requirement. Reasons given in the lesson:
- You have no expertise or proof yet, so charging is premature.
- Free work makes reaching out painless, because you are only offering help.
- It brings feedback that improves the product.
- It builds a reputation that pays off long term.
The objective at this stage is to learn more than to earn. For what to give away, see the Lead Magnets chapter of $100M Leads and the offers material.

### SC-03#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: product-delivery | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:31
claim: The free thing can take one of four forms: software, media, a service (knowledge-based or time-based), or a physical product.
detail: - Software.
- Media: books, content, information.
- Service, of two kinds. Knowledge-based means you know how to do something others do not (e.g. coding websites). Time-based means others know how but do not want to do it (e.g. mowing lawns).
- Physical products: things people can hold.
Choose the mechanism first: what will you give away?

### SC-03#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: leads-fundamentals, money-models | fw: Lead Magnets | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:28
claim: A free offer should do one of three jobs: reveal a problem, give a free trial of the solution, or deliver one free step of a multi-step solution.
detail: 1. Reveal a problem, e.g. an assessment. A chiropractor assesses your back for free and shows you your posture is off, which sets up selling longer services.
2. Free trial, for things people need continuously. Example: run a speed test, show you can cut a website's load time (which raises conversions), and give seven days of it. To keep the improvement, they must keep paying.
3. One free step of a multi-step process. Example: a garage-sealant business applies the first of three coats free (sun protection), then points out that termite, wind and color protection still need the other coats.
Each one opens a loop that leads to selling something later.

### SC-03#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:29
claim: Starter services are easy to find: in B2C, do what people hate doing; in B2B, do a part of the business better or faster than the client can.
detail: - B2C: things most people can do but do not want to — cleaning, cooking, laundry, lawn care, fixing things around the house.

### SC-03#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:30
claim: At the start, ignore scalability and give unscalable one-on-one value at your own cost, because the goal is to learn about customers rather than to earn.
detail: With no customers and no employees, scale is irrelevant. Ask what one-on-one time or work you can front someone at your own cost, given you may not be good yet. Then gather feedback from the market. The common mistake is trying to sell immediately with no expertise and no proof. The best founders use this phase to learn their customers' pains so they can create, innovate and improve the product.

### SC-03#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: outreach | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:10
claim: At zero, you probably want the cheapest and fastest of the Core Four, which is warm outreach.
detail: Content is free too but takes much longer to get going. Reaching out to people who already know you gets people trying any kind of product or service fastest.

### SC-03#11 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: - | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:58
claim: Run warm outreach on the platform where you have the most presence, and count every contact you have ever had as a lead.
detail: Channels: email, phone calls, texts and social media DMs. The most common approach today is messaging people who know you and follow you on social media, so start with whichever platform you have the most presence on. Use everyone you have ever emailed, every phone contact and every follower. Even 100 or 200 followers are 100–200 people who know you and may like you a lot. That is high affinity, nothing like 100 cold leads, and enough to jump-start a business. With no money you should have no shame: knock, call, email, text, DM, ask.
numbers: 100–200 warm contacts can be enough to start.
quote: If you have no money, you should have no shame.

### SC-03#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: outreach | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:07
claim: Fear of judgment is what stops most people at stage 0; they hide behind "I should post content" to avoid warm outreach.
detail: People skip warm outreach not for strategic reasons but because they are scared, embarrassed or afraid of rejection, e.g. of what their mother or grandmother will say about a post. You must be willing to be rejected. The choice is between fearing others' judgment and fearing never having a business. Starting with something free also removes much of the fear, because you are only offering help, not asking for money. That gets you one step closer to asking for money later.

### SC-03#14 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:43
claim: At stage 0, "selling" means getting people to try the free thing: ask about their problems and offer to solve them in exchange for feedback, and a review or referral if you were good.
detail: You do not have the selling skill yet, and you do not need it. Ask people what their problems are. Offer to solve them in return for (1) telling you what you could do better, and (2) if you did well, a review or a friend sent your way.

### SC-03#15 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: lead-getters | fw: Four R's | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:43
claim: What you want from every customer long term are the four R's: review, referral, retention and resale (ascension into something more expensive).
detail: - Review: a public endorsement.
- Referral: they send friends.
- Retain: they stay, ideally moving from free to paid later.
- Resell: they ascend to a higher, more expensive level.
At stage 0 these replace money as the payment for the free work. The roadmap defines customer service by these outcomes.

### SC-03#16 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: product-delivery | fw: Perceived Hidden Costs | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:31
claim: Use free users to find the perceived hidden costs of your product (the non-monetary costs from the value equation) by asking why they did not try it or follow through.
detail: If someone will not try it, or says yes and then does not follow through, some cost is in the way. The cost types:
- Something they have to start doing that they do not want to do.
- Something they have to stop doing that they like (e.g. sleeping in on Saturdays to train in the park).
- Risk they take on. B2B example: "I'll run your ads for free, but you spend $2,000 a month on ads" still risks their money and time.
- Effort, e.g. now having to get on calls with the kid running their ads.
- Delay between when you say you will help and when they actually get the promised result.
For every R a person declines to give you, find out why.
numbers: Example B2B ad spend of $2,000 per month as a hidden risk.

### SC-03#17 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:18
claim: You will often find that the biggest costs of a product are not the money, so removing the unpleasant parts of getting the result is one of the easiest ways to increase value.
detail: Thought experiment: a button delivering an instant six-pack would sell to enormous numbers at a huge price if you alone offered it. Competing solutions promise the same six-pack, yet take a year, risk injury, cost you early mornings, drinking and cheap food, and leave you sore. The promise is the same; the hidden costs differ. Eliminate the non-monetary costs and you can charge nearly anything. Ask how to make getting value from the product as effortless as possible for this person.

### SC-03#18 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:12
claim: Serve free customers as if they were paying, because they are paying you, with their time, reviews and possible future business.
detail: In practice:
- Be available all the time and respond fast.
- No job is too small ("clean the toilet if you have to").
- Hold no resentment about working hard for nothing.
They pay with their time, reviews and possible retention once you move to paid. What you do when nobody is watching, for very little in return, sets the precedent for later success. You are getting the better end of the deal.

### SC-03#19 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv, operator-mindset | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:03
claim: A beginner's only competitive advantage is ruthless execution: forget scalability, throw the kitchen sink at customers, and see what they actually value.
detail: Someone else probably has a better product with better service, so you win by doing as much as possible as fast as possible, with no limit on the scope of service. Remove the word "scale" from your mind for now, because first you must find what works. Each time Hormozi and Leila start a new business, they personally get into customer service at ground level to learn what works. Over-serving also builds reputation, because people whose minds are blown tell others.

### SC-03#20 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:51
claim: More customers leave because of bad customer service than because of product or price, so great service can beat a better, cheaper competitor.
detail: The lesson cites a McKinsey study finding that customers leave more often over bad service than over the product or the price. So a competitor with a better product at a lower price can still lose customers to you if its service is bad. Example: T-Mobile has objectively worse coverage and speeds than Verizon and AT&T, yet gained market share for a long time because its customer service did not suck.

### SC-03#21 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:25
claim: At stage 0 your tech only has to do two things: let you communicate with people and let you deliver your product.
detail: Communication is how you advertise and sell: people have money, you do not, so you must be able to talk to them. Delivery hardware depends on the business: a lawnmower for lawn care, a makeup kit for a makeup artist, a computer, internet and keyboard (and a comfortable chair) for a coder. To graduate, have a computer, internet, microphone, phone and email. If you lack hardware (e.g. you are watching on a public computer), save up and buy it. If you have it, you have already graduated this item.

### SC-03#22 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: hiring-talent | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:48
claim: Your first recruit is yourself: at this stage most people lack real work capacity, and out-executing can often beat 99% of people.
detail: Do not ask whom to hire or outsource first; do the work. After building their first business to $100M and selling it, Hormozi and Leila started over with no employees and did IT, customer service and HR themselves rather than hiring 17 people first. Nobody is too good for this phase, whatever their past job. A typical failure: starting at 10, stopping at 5, and thinking that is hard work. Getting off the ground may mean working 5 a.m. to 11 p.m. or midnight for 6–12 months (a voice adds "eight years"; speaker unclear). Many people never pass this level because they cannot recruit themselves to that capacity. In this phase you invest time and money and see little back, because you are the asset being built.
numbers: 5 a.m.–11 p.m./midnight for 6–12 months as a typical early sacrifice.

### SC-03#25 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:54

detail: Steps:
1. Find what works.
2. Do more of what works.
3. Find the thing preventing you from doing more of it.
4. Solve it.
5. Do more of what works again.
6. Repeat.

### SC-03#26 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:54
claim: You can get good enough at almost any skill with about 20 hours of focused effort; the problem is that most people delay the first hour for a decade.
detail: A lot of the skills in the roadmap can be learned in about 20 hours: half a week of mornings, or two full 10-hour days. Accept that you are bad at it now, as everyone starting from zero is. You are where you are because you have been "in your head". Get into your hands. Ten hours of testing whether something works teaches more than ten years of reading about it.

### SC-03#27 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:03
claim: At stage 0, form a business entity so a lawsuit against the business cannot reach your personal assets and credit.
detail: In the US most small businesses use an LLC or an S-corp. A business with investors probably needs a C-corp. Use an attorney or an online tool such as LegalZoom to set it up; it costs a couple hundred dollars. That is the only HR task at this level.
numbers: A couple hundred dollars to form an LLC.
applies_to: US founders at stage 0.

### SC-03#28 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:50
claim: Open a business bank account, fund it, and pay every business expense from it, because expenses paid from a personal account cannot be written off.
detail: Steps:
1. Open an account at a local or commercial bank.
2. Put in whatever money you need, or can, to cover business expenses.
3. Pay business costs only from it, and write off startup expenses.
Expect to owe tax: as an employee or a non-earner you may have received refunds, but once you make money you pay the IRS for having made it. Do the separation now. Personal expenses mixed into a business account are much harder to untangle later. In practice, carry two cards, one business and one personal, and ask before every purchase which one it is for.

### SC-03#29 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:56
claim: Starting a business, even before the first dollar of revenue, lets you expense the work share of costs you already pay personally, such as phone, computer, internet and a home office.
detail: Once you are genuinely trying to run a business, its costs are deductible. They come out before tax instead of being paid with after-tax income. Examples:
- A phone used mostly for work can go through the LLC.
- The computer and other hardware from the IT step.
- An internet plan, especially one upgraded for work.
- A share of rent for a room used exclusively as an office.
Most people use only part of their phone, internet and computer personally, so the remaining share can offset income. You become wealthier simply by starting a business.
