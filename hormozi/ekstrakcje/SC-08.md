### SC-08#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: money-models | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 5 Productize (10–19 employees): the founder becomes a director, and the core constraint is that customers have nothing else to buy, so LTV:CAC is too low.
detail: Structure: about 1.5 layers — two or three small managers each running 2–3 people, plus some people the founder still leads directly. Constraint → graduation step:
- Product: customers churn with nothing else to buy → connect CS and product, build a backend offer.
- Marketing: qualified leads too expensive → spend more on niche messaging, raise CAC limit, score leads, attribution, CRO.
- Sales: unrealistic expectations cause refunds and bad reviews → sales aids, weekly game tape, NPS/CSAT/refunds per rep.
- CS: inconsistent answers → CS playbook, empowerment wheel, game tape, ascension path.
- IT: personal devices, no tracking → company-owned hardware/software, dashboards.
- Recruiting: can't fill manager roles → deliberate promotion, referral incentives.
- HR: firing done badly → termination policy and process.
- Finance: little to reinvest → budget, forecast, track write-offs.
Next: 20–50 employees, Optimize.
numbers: ~2.4M businesses at this size (down from ~3M at 5–9 employees).

### SC-08#02 | BENCHMARK | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: LTV:CAC | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:35
claim: Hormozi typically prices the backend upsell at about five times the core offer; if 20% of customers take it, revenue doubles.
detail: Typical trigger: a business doing roughly $3–10M wants to scale but LTV:CAC keeps falling as targeting goes broader while LTV stays flat. Customers are fractal most of the time: even inside the niched-down best segment there is another 80/20, and that 20% can spend five times more. Do not dismiss a backend because only a fifth of customers buy it — those buyers might double the business and might double LTV:CAC, which lets you keep expanding.
numbers: 100 customers × $1,000 = $100K; 20 of them × $5,000 = another $100K → revenue doubled.

### SC-08#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Crazy Eight | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:17
claim: The Crazy Eight is a mental matrix of eight ways to make any customer more valuable, which an entrepreneur should be able to run on any business.
detail: Illustrated with chairs:
1. Increase price.
2. Get them to buy again (regular cadence, e.g. yearly refresh).
3. Decrease cost of delivery (not an upsell, but raises profit per customer).
4. Cross-sell a natural companion (chair covers — fries with the burger).
5. Upsell quality (wood instead of plastic).
6. Upsell quantity (a stack of ten instead of one).
7. Downsell quality (a flimsy lawn chair).
8. Downsell quantity (a smaller or kids' chair).
Downsells don't raise LTV but raise revenue per lead, because they turn a no into some money.

### SC-08#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: money-models | also: verticals | fw: Crazy Eight | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:49
claim: Applied to a marketing agency, the Crazy Eight turns into price rises, lower churn, cheaper delivery, added services, senior access, longer contracts and lighter versions.
detail: About 78% of businesses are services. Agency version: raise price; decrease churn so they keep buying; cut delivery cost by offshoring or raising the client-per-rep ratio; cross-sell SEO, creative, content or email on top of paid ads; upsell quality (senior rep with five years' experience, or the owner, instead of a junior); upsell quantity (more touchpoints, e.g. three check-ins a week instead of one, or a 12-month instead of a 3-month contract); downsell quality (work with a rep instead of the owner); downsell quantity (6- or 3-month term, every other week, chat support instead of Zoom calls).
numbers: One rep servicing 10 clients instead of 5 doubles margin; ~78% of businesses are services.

### SC-08#07 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: unit-economics | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:16
claim: When qualified leads become too expensive to keep advertising, use the second product to raise the CAC limit, spend more on niche messaging, score leads yourself and start CRO.
detail: The cap comes from LTV being too low for rising lead costs. Graduation steps:
1. Spend more on the niched-down messaging with the new offer.
2. Raise the allowable CAC because the backend now counts in LTV.
3. Move from leads scoring themselves (friction) to the company scoring incoming leads.
4. Begin conversion rate optimization: a regular split-test cadence across every funnel step (show rate, offer rate, close rate and the rest).
numbers: Lifts of 5–20% at four or five funnel points can sometimes double funnel throughput.

### SC-08#08 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Lead Scoring | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:06
claim: Score leads by qualifications, behaviour and source, then route the highest-scoring leads to the best closers.
detail: Qualifications = who they are; behaviour = what they did; source = where they came from. Example: a self-described CEO with 50 staff who watched every video, bought two books and arrived through organic LinkedIn is worth more than a small-business owner from TikTok who saw one clip before opting in. This needs attribution of the user journey, set up at an earlier stage. Don't give a super-qualified lead to your worst closer who could blow it — pairing best leads with best closers is a big lever on revenue.

### SC-08#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: paid-ads | also: sales-process | fw: CRO | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:40
claim: Split-test in this rough order of impact versus effort: headline and subheadline first, then the offer, image, benefit bullets, proof, and finally layout and order.
detail: Headlines are low investment, high impact — test them hard (the Acquisition.com homepage headline went through about 30 tests). The offer can lift almost as much but sits second because changing it means changing delivery and messaging. Images: medium-to-low impact, low effort. Proof is a big one. Then placement: what sits above the fold, moving the scheduler higher, the order of testimonials (e.g. the woman's before the man's). Install attribution software for accurate testing and reporting; at this stage you can afford it.
numbers: ~30 split tests on one homepage headline.

### SC-08#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads | fw: LTV:CAC | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:30
claim: After adding the backend, recalculate LTV:CAC to set a new acceptable CAC, and scale ads as long as 30-day cash collected at least breaks even on acquisition.
detail: Measure how much cash a new customer brings in within the first 30 days against what it cost to acquire them. Breaking even in 30 days is great; beating it means you make money acquiring customers with LTV still to come. This is where you "gas it". Portfolio companies that reached big scale were sometimes willing to lose money for three to five months before recouping, because they knew their numbers precisely. Expansion comes from being willing and able to outspend competitors.
numbers: Example: allowable CAC rises from $1,000 to $3,000; target 30-day cash collected ≥ CAC; some mature companies accept 3–5 months to payback.

### SC-08#11 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: retention-ltv | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:30
claim: When salespeople set unrealistic expectations and refunds and bad reviews rise, add sales aids, weekly game tape and per-rep satisfaction tracking.
detail: Symptom: the rep effectively promised it cures cancer. Graduation: sales aids and materials that standardize what is promised; weekly game tape between CS, sales and marketing for quality assurance and feedback; NPS, CSAT and refund rate tracked by sales rep rather than only company-wide, to connect expectations set in sales with what is delivered.

### SC-08#12 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: Sales Aids | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:17
claim: Move the repeated teaching part of the sales pitch into pre-call sales aids such as a VSL, so calls are shorter, more consistent and focused on the prospect.
detail: Sales aids: slide decks, VSLs, physical or digital sales letters, PDFs, charts, data visualization, calculators. Take everything reps say identically in the perfect pitch (education, belief breaking) and put it in a video before the call; the call then applies it to the prospect. Benefits: reps stop repeating the same 20 minutes seven times a day (higher utilization), prospects don't lose rapport listening to a monologue, and expectations are set consistently. Hormozi injects sales aids into almost every process to cut volatility in close rate.
numbers: Prefer a steady ~30% close rate to weekly swings between 20% and 40%; ideally replicate the best rep to ~40%.

### SC-08#13 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: content-brand | fw: VSL | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:11
claim: A pre-sale VSL opens with a detailed pain hook, then moves through promise, why it's unsolved, the solution, why it's better, proof, risks, and a hand-off to the rep.
detail: Sequence:
1. Strong, detailed, pain-based hook specific to the avatar.
2. Proof, promise (tied to the pain) and plan.
3. Why the problem hasn't been solved for them yet.
4. Main impact if it continues.
5. The solution and how it works.
6. What makes it better than alternatives.
7. Proof it works — woven through the whole video.
8. Openly call out the risks of investing that they are already thinking (you'll take the money and vanish, it won't work for them, it won't integrate with their tools) and handle each.
9. Hand-off: who they'll speak to next ("Sean will take care of you"), or book the call.

### SC-08#14 | FRAMEWORK | topic: management-ops | also: sales-process, retention-ltv | fw: Game Tape Review (Five A's) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:54
claim: Game tape is a recurring meeting where heads of sales, product, marketing and CS review one recorded call together and each commit to one change, run through the Five A's.
detail: Introduced by Leila in an early business and used in every company since.
1. Arrange: the head of sales books 60–90 minutes with the heads of sales, product, marketing and CS (others may join).
2. Assemble: the head of sales gets a recording from a rep beforehand — a call that went badly (no close) or one showing what good looks like. Start with sales calls; later rotate in onboarding and coaching calls.
3. Assess: each department asks what it can take — marketing: messaging gaps, customer insight; sales: script adherence, over- or under-promising; CS: promises not looped back into onboarding or not deliverable.
4. Analyze: pick the one thing each department will do.
5. Act: commit to doing it within seven days, before the next game tape.
If watching changes no behaviour, there was no point.
numbers: 60–90 minutes; actions due within 7 days.

### SC-08#15 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: Game Tape Review (Five A's) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:33
claim: Run game tape weekly when things are stable, daily or even twice a day while a red-hot problem exists, and as rarely as every other week once issues fade.
detail: Signs of a problem: high churn, customers falling off, missed expectations. Hit it with volume until fixed (Leila's examples: daily until it is fixed after 10 days, or twice a day until fixed after five; she also mentions three times a week). Weekly is preventive and improves every department. At every other week you might review one sales call and one CS call a month. Hormozi resisted at first: by salary cost per head per hour it looked like a $10,000 meeting, but it earned far more than that.
numbers: Meeting cost by headcount ~$10,000 per session; normal cadence weekly.

### SC-08#16 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: sales-process | fw: Game Tape Review (Five A's) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:00
claim: Leila explains that game tape breaks departmental silos so the expectations sales sets match what CS delivers, turning the company into a team of teams.
detail: Onboarding and CS teams have often never seen a sales call, and salespeople have never seen what happens after they close. In the meeting CS asks sales to stop a phrase that causes pain; sales learns about things CS does that prospects would love; CS borrows how sales overcomes concerns; marketers hear the more painful problem prospects bring and use it as a hook. Leaders must see themselves as company leaders who happen to oversee a department, or they optimize their department at others' expense.
quote: A leader in the company who happens to be over sales.

### SC-08#17 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: retention-ltv | fw: NPS | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:14
claim: Track NPS, CSAT and refund rate per sales rep; refunds usually trace back to one or two reps who "sell hot".
detail: Selling hot means setting bad expectations or over-promising — a polite word for lying. Per-rep data shows that a high company refund rate may be one person, so the fix can be targeted. The house rule in Hormozi's sales and marketing teams: state the facts and tell the truth, and if the truth is not compelling enough, improve the product until it is.

### SC-08#19 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:20
claim: Before hiring more CS staff, sort incoming questions into the three to five categories with most volume and write templated answers for each, so reps answer consistently.
detail: Constraint: customers arrive with unrealistic expectations and get different answers from different people. Example categories: change card, change address, different flavour, different delivery cadence, increase or decrease quantity. The CS playbook is the set of templated answers to the most common questions per category, delivered as email templates, a QA framework, Intercom macros or ticketing templates; reps fill in the name and specifics. Templates do for CS consistency what a script does for sales.

### SC-08#22 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: money-models | fw: Ascension Path | novelty: canon | ts: 00:30:22
claim: There are five moments to offer the next product, and most businesses ask at only one of them.
detail: Customers become ready at different times:
1. Immediately — some are in a buying frenzy right after the first purchase.
2. 24–48 hours later — after onboarding or first delivered value, excited before results; an early-bird discount can help.
3. First big win — they express appreciation, leave a testimonial or recoup their investment (Gym Launch: when owners made back the ~$16,000 they paid).
4. Halfway point through the product or service.
5. Last shot — a final Hail Mary after all other asks.
Build the path so the team knows to ask at every point. Customers usually don't ascend because they were asked when not ready, not because they don't want to.

### SC-08#23 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: money-models | fw: Ascension Path | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:30
claim: Tie each ascension moment to a leading indicator so the team knows when a given customer is ready.
detail: Immediate: they say on the sales call that they would love the next product. First big win: a glowing testimonial, "can't imagine not working with you", or proof they recouped the investment. Halfway: at Gym Launch, clients had to be netting a minimum monthly amount from their business to qualify for the halfway ascension pitch, which showed they could afford it.

### SC-08#24 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv | fw: Ascension Path | novelty: canon | ts: 00:35:08
claim: Every upsell is right product, right time, right way; when one fails, one of the three was wrong — usually because the business didn't really try.
detail: Most owners are lazy with ascension and get lazy results. Make it cool. Across the five opportunities, ask differently each time: a different incentive relevant to that stage or the customer's current problems, or a different hook for the same product ("I didn't know you also did this"). Be a Rubik's cube that turns a different face depending on where the customer is in the journey, and make the offer so good they feel stupid saying no.

### SC-08#26 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:37:32
claim: Give employees good company-owned hardware and software instead of letting them use personal devices, with clear return and damage policies.
detail: Constraint: personal devices and accounts cause problems when people leave, and nothing tracks how leads and customers interact; graduate with company-controlled tools plus a dashboard and reporting system. Don't skimp: cheap phones, bad service, slow internet or underpowered computers cut productivity, and people won't use work devices worse than their own. Write in the handbook that devices are returned on exit and who is liable for damage; insure everything. Hormozi, the cheaper of the pair, admits he lost out by economizing; new gear also gives the team a "wow" moment.
numbers: Good laptop and phone ~$2,000–3,000, recouped by one sale a year for a salesperson; $2,000 on a $100K employee is 2% of pay — worth it if productivity rises 2%.

### SC-08#28 | DEFINITION | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: Peter Principle | novelty: canon | ts: 00:40:20
claim: The Peter Principle is people being promoted to the level of their incompetence, usually because the owner promotes out of convenience.
detail: Unsure where else to find managers, owners promote whoever is there, producing managers who are inexperienced, incompetent or both, and the department they run suffers. You lose twice: a great individual contributor disappears and a poor manager drags the rest of the team down. Graduation at this stage: deliberate promotion from within plus incentivized employee referrals.

### SC-08#29 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:41:47
claim: Put internal promotion candidates through the same hiring process an outside candidate would face.
detail: Process protects you from your emotions. Ask whether they would be hired if they came from outside, have them prepare for the interview as normal, and ask the same questions with the same grading — a common failure is going through the motions with insiders. Promote by intention, never convenience.

### SC-08#30 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: Try Before You Buy | novelty: canon | ts: 00:42:56
claim: Alternatively, give an internal candidate a 60–90 day "try before you buy" trial in the new role with objective milestones.
detail: Example: a customer success manager wanting the director role gets a 60-day trial. Set milestones from what a new external hire would accomplish in their first 60–90 days. Highlight the target role's job description red (weak), yellow (okay) and green (strong); everything must be green by the end to get the role. Hold weekly or biweekly check-ins. Worst case they leave the trial with more skills; best case, more skills and the role.
numbers: Trial length 60–90 days; check-ins weekly or biweekly.

### SC-08#31 | BENCHMARK | topic: hiring-talent | also: lead-getters | fw: Cost to Acquire Talent | novelty: canon | ts: 00:43:17
claim: Leila: employees hired through referrals stay about 70% longer, and tenure is the employee version of LTV.
detail: Longer tenure lets you invest more in hardware, software, gatherings and training, just as higher customer LTV lets you spend more. ### SC-08#32 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: Cost to Acquire Talent | novelty: canon | ts: 00:45:10

detail: Agencies take a percentage of first-year salary; you could pay the team a large slice of that instead. When Hormozi's companies raised their referral bonus, effort to refer rose sharply — several referrals can equal a third or half of someone's salary. Pay up to your cost to acquire talent.

### SC-08#33 | CASE | topic: hiring-talent | also: unit-economics | fw: Cost to Acquire Talent | novelty: canon | ts: 00:46:06

### SC-08#34 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:48:18
claim: Firing should never be a surprise: make performance problems and job risk explicit beforehand, which also removes you as the cause.
detail: Constraint: firing incorrectly exposes the company to lawsuits and damages culture — unpredictable firings make the team feel unsafe. Leila's recent terminations were expected because the issue and the consequence had been discussed the week before. You can make someone feel bad now while they can still fix it, or later when they lose the job without knowing why. When owners ask how to know if they should fire a friend, the red flag is that the friend doesn't know how bad it is — then the owner is the problem. Give them the resources to improve before getting to firing.

### SC-08#40 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:55:22
claim: When there is little money to reinvest in growth, build a budget that saves for big upcoming expenses, a simple forecast, and a record of tax write-offs.
