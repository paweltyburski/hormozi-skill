### POD-0427#01 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:17:32
claim: His first gym grew from $5,000 in month one to about $35,000 a month by month seven or eight, and he was out of day-to-day operations by month nine.
detail: Monthly revenue stepped up by roughly $5,000 each month ($5k, $10k, $15k ... $35k). By month nine a manager and full-time trainers ran the gym. He banked about $50,000 in the first six months, which repaid the start-up money. Second location opened at month 15, third at month 21, six locations within three years.
numbers: Start-up cash left when opening: about $5,000. Revenue: $5k month 1 to $35k month 7-8. Saved about $50,000 in the first 6 months. 6 gyms in 3 years.

### POD-0427#02 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:18:17
claim: He sold two thirds of a profitable gym for almost nothing, and the regret pushed him into rapid expansion to win back his old income.
detail: The gym was making about $20,000 a month in free cash flow to him. He sold two thirds at an implied value of about $37,000, then bought back a 10% stake that his mentor held at a $200,000 valuation, netting roughly $17,000. With only a third left he felt he needed three gyms to match prior income, which drove the growth push. Partners were bought out one by one over about three years.
numbers: Equity sold: 2/3 of a gym earning about $20,000 per month, at roughly $37,000 valuation. Buy-back of 10% at a $200,000 valuation. Net about $17,000.

### POD-0427#03 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:17
claim: Before opening, he hired an expert as coach and apprenticed with him, treating that as the fastest route to a new skill.
detail: He cold emailed about 40 gym owners; one replied. He drove to him, joined his $10,000 mastermind with no gym yet, and the coach then hired him, effectively paying back the fee. He started at the gym at 4 a.m. daily to learn, and 90 days later found his location. Skill learned by paying and working beside an expert, not by reading.
numbers: Cold emails sent: about 40, one reply. Mastermind fee: $10,000. Lead time to location: about 90 days.

### POD-0427#04 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: money-models | fw: Gym Launch | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:16
claim: Early gym launches were free to the gym owner: Hormozi funded and ran the marketing and sales, kept the first-period upfront sales, and the owner kept the members.
detail: He flew in for about 21 days, ran marketing and sales, and signed 100 to 200 members. He collected all upfront cash from the first roughly six weeks of memberships; the owner got every customer and their ongoing payments for the next two years. He carried hotel, car, food, advertising and commissions himself. Owners liked it because acquisition was free and his pay depended on delivery of sign-ups.
numbers: About $100,000 in upfront sales per launch of about 21 days. 100-200 sign-ups per gym. 33 launches over two years.

### POD-0427#05 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:22:08
claim: To cover travel risk he asked each gym owner for a small deposit to reserve his date, framed as a token of commitment.
detail: He asked for $500 to hold the date while he was about to spend roughly $3,000 on flights and travel to their facility. The deposit was explicitly a commitment signal rather than payment for the service, and owners accepted it.
numbers: Deposit $500 against about $3,000 of his own travel costs.

### POD-0427#06 | CASE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:24:36
claim: Scaling launches to six or eight gyms a month created a trap where he sold and took payments but had no control over delivery, and refunds followed.
detail: Owners were slotted months ahead, so adding sales reps meant he needed more gyms booked the following month to keep them busy. Because he processed payments but not delivery, and the owners who sought help were typically weak at delivery (strong operators already had full gyms), two gyms told clients to ask for refunds soon after he left. Refunds exceeded his margin, so he had to sell more the following month to cover the prior month, for two months running.
numbers: Refunds about $100,000, roughly all of his savings then. Scale: 6-8 gyms a month, about 8 sales reps.

### POD-0427#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-process | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:24:36
claim: Customers who most need help to fill their business are usually the worst at delivering to those customers, so selling on their behalf without controlling fulfilment is risky.
detail: Hormozi's observation from the refund episode: weak operators come for acquisition help; strong operators already have full capacity. If you take the money but they deliver, the refunds land on you.
applies_to: Anyone selling on behalf of a client or partner who fulfils the order.

### POD-0427#08 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: verticals | fw: Gym Launch | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:30:11
claim: Under cash pressure he offered his gym system for sale, quoting a price he expected to be refused, and kept raising the price with each call as buyers said yes.
detail: First buyer paid $6,000, then $8,000, then $10,000, all accepted. About $60,000 sold in a day. Then he went back to the 33 gyms where he had run launches and offered to show how he filled gyms; nearly all bought. This became the coaching programme. The following month brought about $350,000, enough to cover refunds and overhead.
numbers: Prices quoted on consecutive calls: $6,000, $8,000, $10,000. $60,000 sold in one day. About $350,000 the next month. Monthly obligation then about $100,000 before overhead.

### POD-0427#09 | EXAMPLE | topic: verticals | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:29:24
claim: His wife's online fitness programme sold about $1,000 a day, which suggested replacing gyms with a digital product sold by a sales team.
detail: It was a 16-week transformation programme, done with you: set curriculum plus a weekly check-in by the coach. His idea was eight sales reps each selling about $1,000 a day, skipping the gym middleman, and he cancelled the eight gym launches booked for the next month.
numbers: About $1,000 per day per rep target, 16-week programme, about $1,000 price.

### POD-0427#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:26:19
claim: He argues that crisis forced the pivot because people know how to make money when forced but refuse to do it for themselves.
detail: Most owners have found money for payroll or tax bills under pressure. At his lowest point he dropped branding and enterprise goals and focused only on making cash, which led to selling his process and the coaching version of the company.

### POD-0427#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:56
claim: Building frameworks for recurring decision types saves decision capacity and improves decision quality.
detail: The variables change but the nature of a decision (spend more or less, add an acquisition channel, add a service line) repeats, so a stored framework makes the call faster and more likely right. Over a long horizon people are paid for the quality of their decisions. He says refining frameworks is what he does most.
