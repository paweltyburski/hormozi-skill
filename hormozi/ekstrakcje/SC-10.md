### SC-10#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 7 Categorize covers 50–99 employees; the founder's role is executive, the overall constraint is that everything is disorganized, and you graduate by triaging everything into buckets and systems.
detail: You graduate once you pass 100 employees, then move to specializing. Constraint → graduation step per function:
- Product: outdated, improvements roll out too slowly → one problem at a time with focused feature teams.
- Marketing: volatile lead flow from a single channel → fund a second channel with referrals and segmented nurture; pick the most similar platform.
- Sales: leads wasted, not worked consistently → schedule coverage, nurture checklist, kudos for show rates.
- Customer service: purely reactive → proactive customer journey aimed at renewals and ascensions.
- IT: data and tools scattered → cloud/data host, centrally located hardware.
- Recruiting: too slow, too many open roles, no tracking → ATS plus specialized recruiters.
- HR: employee information everywhere, no data → HRIS.
- Finance: team spends money stupidly → quarterly expense management, department budgets, card limits, insurance, projections.

### SC-10#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: At 50–99 people three layers of leadership form — managers, directors, an executive team — and the first executives are chosen by where the founder most needs leverage.
detail: Bottom: individual contributors. First line: managers (least experienced leaders). Above them: directors. On top: an executive team, usually not fully built — companies hire one to three executives strategically, and some director seats are still empty; that is fine and fills in with scale. Choose executives by the business's needs and the founder's weaknesses. Example: at Gym Launch the first executive was a CFO, because inventory, legal and rebuilding finance were areas the founders disliked and were not skilled in.

### SC-10#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:46
claim: When the product is outdated and improvements take too long, put focused teams on one problem at a time — the one with the biggest reward, not the easiest.
detail: After years in business the product ages and improvement projects drag. Rule: one problem, one team, one measurable objective (attributed to Peter Thiel's practice). Example: the GitHub founder asked for the top 100 complaints about the system, put the whole engineering team on problem #1, did nothing else until it was solved, then moved to #2. Putting all resources on one problem also creates urgency, because everyone knows the other problems are waiting.

### SC-10#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:58
claim: Parallel priorities are a fallacy: switching costs make five simultaneous projects take far longer than doing them in sequence, and split focus is about one-fifth as effective.
detail: The "fallacy of concurrent thinking" assumes five things done at once finish in one-fifth of the time; in reality they take about five times longer than doing them one after another. This holds company-wide. If leadership has five objectives with sub-objectives, it has not prioritized; the job is to say which single thing matters most and will be done first. Example: a quarterly planning day with the largest portfolio company spent the whole day agreeing on the one thing, and the team left with clarity.

claim: Adding executives and directors does not let you run more things at once; the scarce resource is the team's attention and discretionary effort.
detail: Bigger teams create the illusion of more parallel capacity, but it is still one company and departments draw on shared resources. More leaders let you do more things over one to two years by sequencing, not simultaneously. Example: once a meeting had solved the week's main problem, someone proposed using the remaining time on the next one; the speaker (speaker unclear in audio, probably Leila) refused, because starting it would split attention before execution. When all discretionary effort, "shower time" and dinner conversation goes to one problem, it gets solved in a third or a quarter of the time. ### SC-10#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:06
claim: Companies die from the inside out and from indigestion rather than starvation; a bigger company should pursue one bigger thing, not more things.
detail: Litmus test: you probably set five goals last year and did not hit them; planning five again will give the same result. Failure usually comes from internal organization, not the market or economy, and from taking on too much rather than too little. A big priority has many tendrils (e.g. tackling one objective touches email messaging, webinar language, telemarketing), so go deep on it. More capability means the one thing can be larger.

### SC-10#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:37
claim: Every change carries a guaranteed cost while its upside is uncertain, so the parts of the business you leave alone usually improve on their own.
detail: Hormozi found that businesses he left alone almost always did better. Each change forces people to reset and relearn the process. While discretionary effort goes to the one priority, the rest of the team keeps doing the daily work and gets better at it because it is finally allowed to breathe; some problems solve themselves, and you gain efficiency you thought would need brute force. ### SC-10#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: scaling-constraints | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:02
claim: When lead flow becomes volatile because there is only one way to get customers, fund a second acquisition channel — using referrals and segmented nurture for cash — and start with the platform most similar to the current one.
detail: Typically appears around $1–2M per month in revenue with one well-known channel. At high volume a small dip matters: lead flow down 20% can mean four salespeople with empty calendars. A few percentage points that went unnoticed when small now swing the whole business. Graduation: (1) referral process and segmented nurture by lead score to create cash padding, (2) a second channel chosen by the risk ladder (most similar platform first, a new core four last).
numbers: ~$1–2M/month revenue; a 20% lead drop ≈ four reps idle (example).
applies_to: 50–99 employees, single dominant acquisition channel.

### SC-10#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: paid-ads | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:13
claim: Adding a second acquisition channel is among the riskiest and highest-upside moves: it lowers dependence on one source and can double the business, but it drains resources and takes far longer than expected.
detail: Going from one channel to two removes single-channel dependence ("one-itis"); if the first channel holds steady, the second is pure upside — bigger and less risky at the same time. That is why it waits until this stage. Frame it as an investment: would you pay $500K to have, in a year, a second profitable channel equal to the first? At this size, clearly yes. He warns that after a month or two of $10K ad spend with zero return it is tempting to say ads don't work, whereas the typical experience is repeated losses and then a payoff after eight or nine months (expect about a 12-month delay for the return).
numbers: Example framing: $500K investment for a second channel in ~1 year.

### SC-10#13 | BENCHMARK | topic: paid-ads | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:16
claim: Expect about a 12-month delay before a new channel pays back; the typical pattern is months of total loss, then a sudden turn.
detail: Illustrative sequence per month: spend $10K, get $0 — repeated four or more times — then spend 10, get 5; spend 10, get 25; spend 10, get 50, when one month recovers all earlier losses after eight or nine months. Progress comes from fixing the funnel one stage at a time: clicks? opt-ins? scheduling (improve the page offer)? showing (improve nurture)? Keep pushing until you are on calls with the right people.
numbers: ~12-month payback delay; 8–9 months of losses in the example; full spin-up to the level of the existing channel: six months, maybe nine.

### SC-10#14 | CASE | topic: outreach | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:32
claim: Gym Launch's move from paid ads to outbound took about three months to the first sale and a year to reach 50% of revenue, and the exec team asked to kill it at month six.
detail: The team saw only zero sales and money going out, not the stage-by-stage progress. ### SC-10#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:48
claim: Staff new initiatives with "zero-to-one" people who can build from nothing, not "five-to-ten" people who are good at iterating on something that works.

### SC-10#16 | DECISION_RULE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:01
claim: His team's rule is not to put a new hire on a new initiative and not to put the same person on two initiatives; the person running channel one should backfill themselves before moving to channel two.
detail: The biggest mistake they see is asking the people running the working channel to start the second one, which splits resources. The person who runs channel one must first train a replacement who hits the same KPIs, then move. A newly hired person on a new channel leaves you unable to tell whether failure comes from the channel or the person. The founder or CEO should probably take a hands-on part in launching the second channel, so they can tell what is actually wrong if it stalls, and should allow a longer time horizon (30 days is very unlikely to be enough).

### SC-10#17 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:37
claim: Fund the second-channel bet by squeezing more cash from what you already have: a stronger referral program and lead nurture segmented by lead score.
detail: The new channel will lose money for months, so build padding first. Referrals extract more from customers; segmented nurture extracts more from existing leads. Lead scoring should already exist: score by who the lead is, what they have done and where they came from, and route the best to top salespeople. ### SC-10#18 | FRAMEWORK | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Seven Referral Plays | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:27
claim: Seven proven referral plays (from $100M Leads) can be stacked into a strong referral program.
detail: 1. One-sided benefit: pay the referrer your average CAC.
2. Two-sided benefit: split that value between referrer and new customer.
3. Ask on the sales call whether they want to bring someone.
4. Trade discount requests for referrals.
5. Referral events on a fixed cadence.
6. Ongoing program with promotional spikes (e.g. double-bonus days).
7. Unlockable referral bonuses tied to status.
Plays 3–4 are sales-team actions; the rest are owner-level programs. All are ones Hormozi has run.

### SC-10#19 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: unit-economics | fw: Seven Referral Plays | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:27
claim: Pay referrers your full average CAC rather than a small percentage, since you already pay ad platforms that much per customer.
detail: Owners happily pay Meta or Google around $3,000 per new customer but balk at paying a customer the same. Referred customers are more valuable: they stick longer, refer others, accept higher prices and convert better. So pay the referrer what you would pay anyone else, not 20% or 50% of it.
numbers: Example CAC ~$3,000 per customer.

### SC-10#20 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Seven Referral Plays | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:13
claim: Hormozi prefers two-sided referral rewards because they protect the referrer's relational capital.
detail: Same total cost, split between two people. If the referrer gets a big payout alone, the friend may suspect they are being used for money. With a split, the referrer can offer something exclusive ("through me you get 20% off, available nowhere else") and also receive a reward (e.g. 20% of revenue). Everyone wins and the ask feels generous.
numbers: Example: 20% off for the new customer, 20% of revenue to the referrer.

### SC-10#21 | EXAMPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: sales-process | fw: Seven Referral Plays | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:00
claim: A new salesperson at a portfolio company doubled the top rep's sales on the same lead volume simply by asking each prospect whether they wanted to bring someone else.
detail: Discovered by sitting in on the managers' meeting two levels down. The lesson: basic asks are forgotten, and at this stage you need the extra cash padding.

### SC-10#22 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: objections-closing | fw: Seven Referral Plays | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:47
claim: Use discount requests as barter for referrals: grant the discount only when the prospect brings friends or leads.
detail: Structure: you cannot give 10% off for nothing, but if they bring one or two friends (or give three leads) they get the 10% once those people sign. Or anchor on CAC: offer a fixed amount (e.g. $3,000 off) per person they bring in.
numbers: 10% off for 1–2 friends or 3 leads; or ~$3,000 off per referral.

### SC-10#23 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: verticals | fw: Seven Referral Plays | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:32
claim: Run referral events on a cadence and drive the ongoing referral program with short promotional spikes and status-based unlockable bonuses.
detail: Events: gather customers (they value meeting one another) and ask each to bring a friend — about quarterly for local businesses, two or three times a year for national ones. Many gyms get their entire month of sales from one event. Ongoing program: keep talking about referrals, but create special windows (e.g. "fast cash Fridays" in gyms, double bonus on Fridays during a month), since a constant offer stops feeling important. For national businesses, add unlockable bonuses that confer status.
numbers: Events ~4/year local, 2–3/year national.
applies_to: Events especially strong for local businesses.

### SC-10#25 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:17
claim: Choose the second channel by climbing a risk ladder: change one variable at a time, starting with a new audience and ending with a new core four method.
detail: In order of increasing risk:
1. Same core four, platform and placement — new audience (e.g. more Meta newsfeed targeting).
2. Same audience — new placement (e.g. Meta stories); harder because it needs a new creative format such as vertical video.
3. Same core four — new platform, choosing the closest analog (e.g. YouTube pre-roll, which like newsfeed ads interrupts consumption).
4. Repeat steps 1–3 inside the new platform (community ads, sponsored videos), then the next platform.
5. Only when paid platforms are exhausted: a new core four (e.g. paid ads → outbound).
Gym Launch went from Meta paid ads straight to outbound (step 5), which he calls the riskiest move possible; it took a whole year to reach 50% of revenue, and he says lower-risk levers would have been faster.

### SC-10#27 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:05
claim: Maximize schedule coverage: more days, more hours and more slot options, because the biggest throughput difference between similar businesses is how many hours they are available.
detail: Checklist:
- More compelling thank-you page headline; scheduler above the fold.
- 15-minute slots (3:15 may feel more precise than 3:00 and so may give a higher show rate; it also signals more availability).
- Open seven days a week, as many hours as possible.
- Do not book fake appointments to look busy: nobody cares, and a faked booking can take the very slot the prospect wanted; open slots simply show you have capacity. With 20 reps, one rep free all day gives full-day availability.
- Match slots to the customer's schedule (gym owners usually have the most availability midday; people with jobs more in the morning and evening).

### SC-10#28 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: sales-team | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:51

For two-step sales, hand off in a group message with setter, closer and prospect, so the prospect sees the setter still watching and is less likely to ghost a stranger.

### SC-10#33 | DECISION_RULE | topic: retention-ltv | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:30:31

### SC-10#34 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: lead-getters | fw: Four R's of Customer Service | novelty: canon | ts: 00:31:36
claim: The customer journey should be built around four R's — retain, review, refer, resell — in that order.
detail: 1. Retain: keep customers engaged with exceptional experiences and continuous value.
2. Review: collect feedback through surveys and reviews to find improvements and new products; complaints often reveal an add-on or upsell opportunity. Get feedback from those who dislike you, reviews from those who love you.
3. Refer: get happy customers to recommend you through referral programs and track advocacy.
4. Resell: upsell, cross-sell and renew to maximize lifetime value.
The order matters: referrals and resales follow from retention and feedback. If nothing is mapped, start with retain and review, so that customers ask for the next product instead of being pushed.

### SC-10#35 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: management-ops | fw: Customer Journey | novelty: canon | ts: 00:32:14
claim: Map the customer journey with separate lines for the customer, the employee and each of the four R's, then turn it into CRM triggers and team training.
detail: Earlier stages used two lines (what the customer does, what the employee does). Now each R has enough substance for its own line: everything done to retain, get reviews, get referrals and resell at each point in time. A journey is a process; without it a reactive team cannot become proactive and many opportunities are missed. Once mapped, configure it in the CRM and use it to decide whom to hire — often splitting customer service into a reactive team and a proactive team.

### SC-10#36 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: - | fw: Customer Journey | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:05
claim: Build customer-journey triggers on one of three programmable variables: time elapsed, customer behavior, or an event that occurs.
detail: Time-based: after X days, ask for a review or offer the next product. Behavior-based: once the customer completes an action (checks a box), ask for the review. Event-based: something happens to the customer or account. These triggers make proactive steps happen automatically.
