### OC-06#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: unit-economics, product-delivery | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:46
claim: An offer can solve the problem and still fail as a business because what you deliver is not high-margin or valuable enough.
detail: Offer component five (building the offer, part two) is about what you literally give the customer in exchange for their money. You may be solving the problem but not making money, so delivery must be designed for both value and margin. Three pieces: (1) understand the sales-to-fulfillment continuum, (2) generate delivery vehicles (the "how"), (3) trim and stack the options for maximum profit and value.

### OC-06#02 | FRAMEWORK | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, scaling-constraints | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:38
claim: Offers sit on a continuum where the easier something is to sell, the harder it tends to be to fulfil, and vice versa: done-for-you, done-with-you, do-it-yourself.
detail: Done-for-you (you do the work for them) is generally easy to sell but hard to fulfil, because you must do everything. Done-with-you sits in the middle — a personal trainer, a coach with a program you follow, mentorship, an advisor. Do-it-yourself (a book, a course) is easy to fulfil — you just give a login — but hard to sell, especially at higher prices, because it demands much better persuasion, value stacking, communication and marketing. The ideal is something both easy to sell and easy to fulfil; strong operators who are good at infrastructure, teams and culture can sometimes build an entire business around a new way of fulfilling that achieves this.

### OC-06#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, scaling-constraints, pricing | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:39
claim: Build top-down: start with an easy-to-sell, hard-to-fulfil, expensive offer for a few customers, then move toward cheaper, more scalable offers.
detail: Hormozi works on the easy-to-sell/hard-to-fulfil end first and slowly moves toward the other end. Especially under $3M a year, accept serving fewer people at higher prices so you build skills, earn cash flow, learn the business, and price-anchor yourself. Later you look at what you delivered, trim and restack pieces so you provide nearly the same value with less operational drag, and lower the price. Benefits: hard-to-fulfil things are more expensive, so you need fewer sales to get going and do not have to solve scale yet; and because you were positioned at the top, the cheaper offer is easier to sell than it would otherwise be.
applies_to: Especially businesses starting out or under ~$3M/year.

### OC-06#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: pricing | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:39
claim: Tesla's pyramid — $250,000 Roadster first, then about $100,000 cars, then about $30,000 cars — models how to build a business from the top down.
detail: Tesla started with an expensive, hard-to-make car for a small audience of wealthy early adopters who happily paid, positioning the brand at the top. Over time it moved down the pyramid to a roughly $100K car accessible to more people, then to a roughly $30K car accessible to almost everyone (in the US). Hormozi says Acquisition.com is built the same way: the top serves $3–10M companies trying to scale and exit, while the rest of the pyramid (free courses and content) is easy to fulfil.
numbers: ~$250K → ~$100K → ~$30K price tiers.

### OC-06#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers, content-brand | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:28
claim: Selling the expensive version first automatically anchors price, value and credibility for the cheaper scalable version, and gives you a simple "reason why" for launching it.
detail: Everything is judged in the frame of reference you create: if prospects saw you charge a lot for the custom version, the cheaper version looks like a deal, and your past results raise their perceived likelihood of achievement. It also hands you a natural story: "we couldn't keep up with demand for our custom one-on-one service, so we made something more people can participate in."
quote: It all exists within the context that we create.

### OC-06#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: operator-mindset, product-delivery | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:28
claim: Beginners who refuse to sell anything "not scalable" are solving a problem they do not have yet — get clients and cash first.
detail: A very common mistake: people starting out say they do not want to sell anything that does not scale. Hormozi's response: you do not even have clients or money yet, so do not worry about scale. Sell high, sell more custom work, get cash flow, use the money to build the business, and learn what customers value. Do not solve for problems that do not exist yet.
quote: Don't solve for problems that don't exist yet.

### OC-06#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, scaling-constraints | fw: Create Flow Monetize Flow Add Friction | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:59
claim: Create flow first, monetize the flow, then add friction — only after you have demand do you make the offer easier to fulfil and harder to sell.
detail: Order of operations: (1) create flow — get the yeses, even if what you sold is hard to fulfil; (2) monetize the flow — make money from it; (3) add friction — redesign for scalability, i.e. easier for you to fulfil, which means harder to sell (his definition of friction). Things that are easier for you usually deliver less value to the prospect (not always), which is why creating high-value, easy-to-fulfil offers takes a lot of time and thought. After delivering the hard version, you will find components you promised that customers did not value much but were hard for you to deliver — cut those, add things you lacked, and keep iterating until the stack is all high-value items.

### OC-06#08 | FRAMEWORK | topic: product-delivery | also: offers | fw: Delivery Cube | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:18
claim: The delivery cube is a set of prompts for generating every way to deliver a solution: group size, level of service, support type, consumption format, speed and convenience, and the 10x / one-tenth test.
detail: List each problem, then every way to solve it, using prompts: 1. Group size: one-on-one, small group, one-to-many. 2. Involvement: do-it-yourself, done-with-you, done-for-you (crossed with group size, six boxes). 3. Support channel: tech support, chat, email, live phone, expert access, Zoom, chosen by convenience and value. 4. Consumption format: video, audio, written/PDF, live or recorded. 5. Speed and convenience: availability (24/7 or 9-5 weekdays) and response time (5 minutes, 1 hour). 6. 10x / one-tenth test: imagine 10x and 1/10 the price. If the prompts confuse, just list every possible way; you trim later.

### OC-06#09 | EXAMPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, verticals | fw: Delivery Cube | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:18
claim: Applying the delivery cube to one weight-loss problem — "grocery shopping is hard, confusing, expensive, time-consuming and I won't like it" — produces many delivery options at each group size.
detail: One-on-one: first shopping trip to show where to look and how to be fast and cheap; a consultation building a personal list; full-service buy and deliver (done-for-you); in-person orientation away from the store; tech support while shopping (photo of the soup can, you confirm); a 30-minute call while they shop. Small group: group shopping trip, group framework session for building lists, buying for the group, group orientation. One-to-many: live virtual store tour with Q&A, recorded tour, do-it-yourself grocery calculator (meal plan to list by goal and calories), predetermined lists (30 lists by body weight and goal), grocery buddy system pairing customers, scheduled meet-ups at the store without you, pre-built Instacart carts.

### OC-06#10 | CASE | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: Delivery Cube | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:28
claim: Gym Launch once promised chat answers within three minutes during 12-hour days, but found clients valued access to a very senior expert who was available less of the time more than speed.
detail: The support promise: a chat team available roughly 9 a.m. to 9 p.m. would respond within three minutes. Research with clients showed it was more valuable to have an expert, even if less available, than fast answers from less expert staff. They switched to a very high-level person available every day but not all day. Hormozi notes the ideal would be an expert available 12 hours a day, and that the right speed/expertise trade-off is market-specific — test what your customers value.
numbers: 3-minute response promise; ~12 hours/day chat coverage (9 a.m.–9 p.m.).
applies_to: Gym Launch (B2B coaching/licensing for gym owners); the trade-off varies by market.

### OC-06#11 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: pricing, offers | fw: 10x and 1/10 Test | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:19
claim: Run the 10x / one-tenth test: ask what you would add to justify ten times your price, and how you would deliver more value than now at one tenth of the price.
detail: Take your planned price as X. 10x test: if you charged 10X, what else would you provide to make it ten times more valuable? This stretches you upward — and maybe you should actually raise the price and provide those things, since many buyers simply prefer more value. One-tenth test: if you were forced to deliver more value than today while charging only a tenth, how would you structure delivery? This pushes you toward building assets, tools and resources you create once and reuse, which are often more valuable than what you do now. Together they stretch both value and efficiency.

### OC-06#12 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: product-delivery, unit-economics | fw: Trim and Stack | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:41
claim: Trim and stack: rate every delivery option on value and cost, cut everything low-value, make sure every problem is covered, and typically keep no more than one or two high-cost items per set of solutions (his personal habit).
detail: 1. Start from the full list of delivery options per problem. 2. Rate each on value (value equation: worth it, likelier success, less effort, faster) and on cost to you. 3. Cut all low value regardless of cost; low-value/low-cost is a distraction, since each piece should make the customer say "wow, I'm telling my friends". 4. Keep all high-value items, noting cost. 5. Check coverage: tick every box of the problem matrix; some solutions cover several problems, gaps (e.g. "not sustainable") get a small extra such as a back-end guide. 6. Limit high-cost items to about one or two per set of solutions; cover the rest with low-to-medium cost items. Aim: high-value, mostly low-cost stack.

### OC-06#13 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: product-delivery, retention-ltv | fw: Trim and Stack | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:14
claim: In trimming, a live grocery tour may be swapped for a buddy system that delivers similar value without the owner's time, while a one-on-one accountability coach is kept despite its cost because execution is the main failure point.
detail: Reasoning examples: a live tour is valuable, but the buddy system probably solves the same problem and only requires pairing people, so it is easier. Two one-on-one troubleshooting consultations during the program are perceived as valuable, raise the likelihood of success, and are reasonably convenient to deliver. A dedicated one-on-one coach (weight loss, money, relationship — any domain) who holds the client accountable to the plan and checks in daily or two to three times a week is high cost because you must staff it, but Hormozi would keep it because most people's number one problem is actually executing, so the value outweighs the cost.

### OC-06#14 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing, product-delivery | fw: Trim and Stack | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:44
claim: Service businesses should run at 80% or higher gross margin — fulfilment should cost no more than $20 of every $100 charged.
detail: Why so high: the remaining 80% has to cover everything else and still leave profit. If you hope for about 30% net margin, around 20% goes to cost of goods, a chunk goes to cost of acquisition (marketing, sales, commissions, marketing teams, advertising), and more goes to infrastructure (software, admin, finance, billing, HR). Margins are usually highest at the start and shrink as you add people and infrastructure while scaling, so you should be most profitable in the beginning. Worked example: if fulfilment costs $200, you must sell for at least $1,000 to keep 80% gross margin. Exceptions: physical products can run lower margins because of volume, and some businesses (e.g. software) become more profitable over time.
numbers: ≥80% gross margin for services; $100 price → ≤$20 fulfilment cost; $200 cost → ≥$1,000 price; ~30% net margin target, ~20% COGS.
applies_to: Service businesses; physical products and software differ.

### OC-06#15 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:31
claim: Gross margin counts only the direct cost of fulfilling what you sell; test it by asking how many more people you would need to hire if you added 100 customers.
detail: Direct cost is what scales with each customer — in a service, the people who actually fulfil it. Indirect costs — rent, software, payment processing fees and similar overhead that accompany growth — are not part of gross margin. The test: if 100 more customers signed up, the extra fulfilment staff you would need is the direct cost.

### OC-06#16 | EXAMPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, unit-economics | fw: Trim and Stack | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:28
claim: Typical low-cost delivery items are recordings, tools, swipe files, PDFs, videos and group sessions; typical high-cost items are one-on-one work and personal reviews.
detail: Low cost: recordings, tools, swipe files, PDFs, videos, group sessions, calculators. High cost (but often high value): one-on-one sessions, personally reviewing a client's sales call or therapy call, personally reviewing ten real-estate deals, going to someone's house to clean out their pantry, taking someone on a grocery store tour. A finished stack typically has, per problem, one high-value/high-cost item and the rest high-value/low-cost.

### OC-06#17 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: objections-closing | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:28
claim: A real-estate coach personally reviewing a client's ten deals and signing off before they buy is worth its high cost because it removes the buyer's main fear of making a mistake.
detail: For a real-estate coaching client, the number one concern is making a costly mistake. Tools, swipe files and calculators help, but the coach's personal review of ten deals — and sign-off before the client pulls the trigger on one — adds the extra trust that they are not making a mistake. It reverses risk and raises perceived likelihood of success, so it earns one of the limited high-cost slots in the stack.
