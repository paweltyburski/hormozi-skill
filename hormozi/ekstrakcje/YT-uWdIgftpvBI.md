### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:45
claim: Start by selling something very expensive to a few or something very cheap to everyone; the middle is where businesses die.
detail: Starting expensive is easier because you must earn enough to serve the masses later. Tesla's first car was a $250,000 roadster sold to few buyers as a semi-finished beta, then a cheaper model S, then the Model 3. Business is the arbitrage between acquisition cost and customer income.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:25
claim: You learn more from fewer high-value clients, and landing a $15,000 client among $50 sales resets your sense of what the market pays.
detail: Higher-paying clients shift your belief about who is in the market and what you can build. For scale: $15,000 would take around 300 sales at $50 (his figure as spoken is garbled, 3,050 in audio).

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:12
claim: Everyone already has an hourly rate: last year's earnings divided by 2,000 hours; selling time is not what poor people do.
detail: Even investors trade time (research, due diligence on the many deals rejected). If the thing you sell your hours for pays above your current rate, you earn more. Time sold one-on-one also carries about 100% margin against only your own living costs.
numbers: Hourly rate = annual income / ~2,000 hours.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: pricing | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:46
claim: One-on-one with few clients gives delivery flexibility, so you can pivot instantly and beta test ideas without retraining staff or recoding.
detail: Because time is capped, supply stays small, which forces price upward when demand exists; he links the logo of Acquisition.com to leverage and the supply-demand curve. Every business can keep about five one-on-one clients, e.g. a lawn firm: who gets your cell phone, who designs the garden.
numbers: Cap at ~5 one-on-one clients (example).

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:53
claim: Instead of asking whether the work is worth your time, set the price so it is, and let people who want to pay more do so.
detail: The question "is it worth my time" has an answer at some price. Do not decide for buyers that nobody would pay it.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#07 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: content-brand | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:15
claim: A visible very-high-ticket one-on-one tier lifts the brand: even unsold, its price transfers perceived value to the scalable product.
detail: Narrative: many cannot afford me one-on-one, so I packaged my lessons for everyone. For the anchor to work you must state the price plainly, confront it, then stay silent and let them respond; if they refuse, offer the scalable version with about 90% of the components. List the price wherever you sell or say it on the offer.
applies_to: Any business, including ones with community subscriptions.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#08 | BENCHMARK | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:09:16
claim: With a $100 product at 40% margin and a $1,000 product bought by 10 of 100 buyers, the expensive tier produces about three quarters of total profit.
detail: 90 buyers of the $100 item at 40% margin give $3,600 profit; 10 buyers of the $1,000 item with near full margin add $10,000. He says revenue doubles and the expensive thing makes three times the profit of the cheap one. He suggests having a $1,000/month or $10,000 one-time offer available even if you run a $100/month community.
numbers: $3,600 profit from 90 cheap buyers versus $10,000 from 10 expensive buyers; revenue about 2x.
### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#09 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:10:26
claim: Design the premium offer with three brainstorming frames: what if you charged 10-100x, what if growth came only from one customer's word of mouth, and what if everything unscalable were stripped but it had to be worth 10x.
detail: Frame one: list everything you would do for 100x the price, cross out items with hard costs, and offer what you would do for $1,000 or $10,000. Frame two: ask what experience would make one client tell friends. Frame three: how to make it worth 10x without scalable elements. Private-client work also yields case studies and marketing material and better-quality contacts.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#10 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: content-brand | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:36
claim: The premium avatar is a different person than your cheap buyer, and you win them by describing their pain better than they can, using phrases lifted from reviews of books in your niche.
detail: Choose someone with money, pain and reachability. Pull review quotes from top books, since persuasion lives in the specific, never the vague. A precise description of pain can motivate more than a bigger promise.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:26
claim: One-on-one delivery raises perceived likelihood and ease, while your reputation works as an implied guarantee.
detail: Same outcome, but a daily conversation beats a PDF. Cap the time (5-10% of yours), charge ten times more, live on it and put the rest of the cash into aggressive growth.
numbers: 5-10% of your time at about 10x price.

### YT-uWdIgftpvBI#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:28
claim: Latency beats magnitude: wealthy buyers pay most for time saved, so halve delivery time and add priority.
detail: Add priority access, first sight of new things, fastest emergency response. Reverse-engineer ease by listing every action the customer takes and deleting friction points one by one. At ten times the price you can pay vendors more to prioritise your customers, building your own priority lane.
