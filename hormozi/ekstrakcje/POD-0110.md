### POD-0110#01 | SCRIPT | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: Three Core Beliefs | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:46
claim: Against "it sounds too good to be true", have the prospect design the ideal program themselves, then show that nothing is missing from yours.
detail: Steps: (1) ask what a program that would really get them there would include; (2) they usually do not know, so list components (fast support, personal coach, daily strategic coach, hundreds of peers in the same market who got the result recently); (3) ask what else is missing; (4) conclude that the program is complete, so the gap is belief, not product. The sales team calls this the unicorn close. Works the same in weight loss and B2B.
numbers: Example components: support answered within 3 minutes; 500 peers who achieved the result within the last 12 months.

### POD-0110#02 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: objections-closing | also: - | fw: Three Core Beliefs | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:33
claim: Disbelief has two roots, and each needs a different answer: being burned before, or never believing the goal is possible.
detail: For the burned prospect, argue that one bad experience should not burn them twice (one rejected dance invitation does not mean never dating again), and that the best way to get where you want is to learn from people who have done it; billionaires, for example, say they had lots of help. Trying to do it alone is unreasonable and prideful.
### POD-0110#03 | TACTIC | topic: objections-closing | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:10
claim: A prospect who wants a big result yet thinks it impossible holds two opposed beliefs, and must be asked to drop one.
detail: The paralysis in deciding comes from believing both at once (cognitive dissonance). Name it: either they stop wanting the outcome or they accept it is possible. Treat it as a belief to be broken, not a flaw in the offer; if they cannot name what is missing from the program, the objection is theirs and is being projected onto the business.

### POD-0110#04 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:07
claim: Ask how much evidence would make belief reasonable, then ask at what point continued doubt becomes the unreasonable position.
detail: Use the gravity comparison: nobody sees gravity, yet enough demonstrations make denial unreasonable. Ask "how many testimonials would you need?" If the bar keeps rising (for example, someone with identical DNA who did it six weeks ago), ask whether that standard is realistic and whether it helps them reach their goal.
numbers: Example offered to a doubting gym owner: 800 testimonials from the last 12 months and 20 current clients to call.

### POD-0110#05 | TACTIC | topic: objections-closing | also: - | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:41
claim: Use "if this were true, what else would also be true?" so the prospect can check the claim against outside facts.
detail: Applied to Gym Launch: if it truly helped many gyms quickly, the company itself would show rapid growth, the largest paying community, and so on, and these can be verified. Then ask what else the prospect would need to see; when they dismiss testimonials, Yelp, Google and Facebook reviews as fake, ask them to rely on their own judgment of the person in front of them.
numbers: Claimed company growth cited as the "what else is true": 20 to 90 employees in 12 months; $5M sales to a $50M run rate in 12 months.

### POD-0110#06 | TACTIC | topic: objections-closing | also: - | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:51
claim: Walk a doubter through the cost of faking all the proof, so the "it is a scam" theory collapses.
detail: Paid actors on calls, fake anonymous commenters in groups, fake videos, event venues costing around half a million dollars: the effort to fake it all would itself demonstrate the skill needed to build the business. Hormozi adds that holding this view often reflects scarcity and fear that the prospect must deal with on their own.
numbers: Large event venue rental cited as about $500,000.

### POD-0110#07 | TACTIC | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:27
claim: A damaging admission builds trust: tell the prospect plainly that no successful client calls the process easy.
detail: In weight loss, no client who lost large amounts will say it happened overnight or was easy; testimonials say it was the most work they have done but it worked as stated. Everyone wants the result until they must give up foods, be sore, and get up early six days in a row. Admitting the hardship also prepares gym owners for the hard conversations with their own staff.

### POD-0110#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: objections-closing | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:21
claim: After owners believe, the hard part is overcoming their staff's limiting beliefs about the changes.
detail: Typical staff pushback when a gym changes its model: no one will pay that price, no one will accept moving from unlimited to three days a week, members will revolt if barbells go. Hormozi's stated biggest value is helping the owner believe by showing many peers who have done it, repeatedly, until the "I can't" breaks. Seeing peers on stage at the summit with million-dollar run rates does this.
numbers: A Legacy-tier gym on stage claimed $600,000 a year; about 500 Legacy gyms average that.

### POD-0110#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-team | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:35
claim: Only accept into the premium tier those clients who earned back more than the program cost.
detail: Gym Launch's Legacy tier required that the client had made more money than Gym Launch cost them. His reasoning: if someone could not execute with the little given, how can he trust them to execute with more, so it doesn't serve them or him. This selectivity is tied to the reputation Legacy has.
numbers: As an example, someone who makes $16,000 during the programme is not considered legacy eligible.
### POD-0110#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:22
claim: Once acquisition is solved, the unglamorous routines are what raise lifetime value.
detail: Examples listed: a set communication rhythm with the team, putting the owner on payroll and budgeting accordingly, and contacting every customer within a 14-day cycle, with an extra reach-out midweek for those who missed that week. These are boring but double or triple lifetime value, and gym results within months came from them.
numbers: Customer contact cycle: every 14 days.

### POD-0110#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:10
claim: Test any promise by asking whether the seller's own business shows the growth they claim to deliver.
detail: If a guru says a method doubles profit, their business should be growing fast; Hormozi saw people in the gym space for ten years whose own business had not grown. Prospects can use "if this is true, what else must be true?" to decide when they lack data. Lack of data is ignorance, not stupidity.

### POD-0110#12 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:15:59
claim: The technology adoption curve, as Hormozi describes it, suggests later buyers tend to have more limiting beliefs and respond to data rather than pressure.
detail: Innovators are the first 2%, early adopters the next band up to about 15%, then a chasm before the early majority (about 35%, more than twice the size of both earlier groups). The early majority wants to see others go first and tends to be less emotional about the purchase, so when presented with data they can make sound decisions. For this reason Gym Launch, crossing into the early majority, avoided rush sales and pressure closing on the first call for a big investment.
numbers: Innovators 2%; early adopters "3 to 15%"; early majority 35% of the market.
### POD-0110#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:22:46
claim: Belief is a choice made by acting, not an after-effect of results.
detail: Hormozi frames belief as an action, like love or faith, and says people who accept very large possibilities are less limited by small beliefs. He suggests that the capacity to choose to believe is itself a competitive advantage.
