### POD-0332#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: There are seven common ways to quote business value, and each can mislead: contract value, lifetime revenue, valuation, yearly revenue, yearly profit, owner earnings (net free cash flow), and net worth.
detail: Hormozi ranks contract value as the worst, lifetime revenue second worst, valuation as decent only if a third party sets it, yearly revenue and profit as best read together, and net free cash flow plus net worth as his preferred measures.
### POD-0332#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: promotions-launches | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:45
claim: "Marketer math" counts the contract value of a one-time event sign-up as if it were repeating revenue.
detail: An event pitching a one-year programme collects one month of new payments, but the claim treats the full contract as guaranteed. The example has about 100 buyers at $2,500 a month, which really adds about $250,000 a month once, with churn likely; the speaker's stated headline figure was $2.5M a month (unclear in audio). Contract value only means something if contracts are fully enforceable and revenue only rises.
numbers: 100 buyers x $2,500 a month = $250,000 of new monthly payments.

### POD-0332#03 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:02:22
claim: Lifetime revenue flatters: $2M over ten years can mean an income any gig job matches.
detail: That is about $200,000 a year; at 40% margin it is $80,000 a year for ten years, similar to driving for a ride service without running a business. A frontline fast-food worker also earns about a million dollars over a 45-year career.
numbers: $2M over 10 years = $200,000 a year; 40% margin = $80,000 a year.

### POD-0332#04 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:11
claim: Typical valuation multiples of EBITDA rise with company size; 20x revenue needs an exceptional growth business.
detail: A fair claim for most businesses is 5 to 6 times EBITDA. Smaller companies might be 2 to 3 times, middle market 6 to 8 times, public markets closer to the 20s (even about 30 now), mostly because lots of capital and easy liquidity push prices up. Anyone claiming 20 times top line is mostly making it up, unless the business has strong growth, moats and unique technology. Only about 1% of businesses ever sell.
numbers: Smaller business might be 2-3x EBITDA; typical 5-6x; middle market 6-8x; public roughly 20s up to ~30x.
### POD-0332#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:16
claim: A purchase multiple is a return on capital: a $1M-profit business bought at 10x gives a 10% return.
detail: Deals at 10x still make large profits because buyers use debt cheaper than the business returns (leverage). Hormozi defers the detail to another video.
numbers: $1M profit x 10 = $10M price = 10% yearly return.

### POD-0332#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:51
claim: Report yearly revenue and profit together, because reinvestment can hide a healthy firm or inflate a weak one.
detail: A business doing $100M a year that reinvests profit down to $500,000 is very different from a $1M-revenue business making $500,000. Reinvestment is rational when returns on capital exceed what passive investing gives.
numbers: $100M revenue with $500,000 profit versus $1M revenue with $500,000 profit.

### POD-0332#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:50
claim: Value to the owner is net free cash flow: what can be taken out after reinvestment needed to keep the advantage.
detail: A manufacturer that must buy a new machine every three years with a multiple of its earnings gives owners little. Buffett's See's Candies was bought for $25M and produced over a billion dollars of free cash flow since its spending needs were small (candy, stable tastes).
numbers: Purchase $25M; cumulative net free cash flow over $1B.

### POD-0332#08 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:20:02
claim: Hormozi's own figures: his businesses are not capital-intensive, so he took nearly all EBITDA as personal income.
detail: His companies crossed $100M of sales in total around November 2020 (he thinks), and since then he says they did roughly another 30 or 40 million, without giving yearly figures yet. Owner earnings he extracted were just under $50M over the last four years. EBITDA in 2018 was about $17M (unclear in audio), and about 3-4 the year before.
numbers: $100M total sales crossed about November 2020; roughly another $30-40M since then; just under $50M owner earnings in four years.
### POD-0332#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:35
claim: Measure an entrepreneur by net worth, which holds investable assets plus owned business stakes valued fairly.
detail: He aims to grow net worth tax-free by buying for long-term holds with no plan to sell. For founders the stake is valued at a fair multiple of EBITDA, such as 6 to 8 times for mid-market companies. He warns against tying self-worth to net worth.
numbers: Mid-market = $10M to $100M yearly revenue.

### POD-0332#10 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:50
claim: Hormozi shares the company's financial figures with everyone in his firms, including downturns.
detail: Reason: he does not want to hide that expenses rose or profit fell, and wants staff to see the owner carrying the risk of paying everyone's mortgages in a bad period.
