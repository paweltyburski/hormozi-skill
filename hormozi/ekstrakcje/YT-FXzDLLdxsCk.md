### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#01 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:46
claim: Method 1 for LTV: divide total lifetime sales by total customers ever sold; it understates LTV for a growing business.
detail: Example: $1M, $2M and $3M over three years is $6M across 600 customers, about $10,000 each. Customers still paying are not counted for their future, so the figure is a floor.
numbers: $6M / 600 customers = ~$10,000.

### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#02 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: Formula: lifetime revenue = price / monthly churn | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:16
claim: Method 2: measure churn as customers lost over customers at the start of the month, ignoring new sales, then divide average monthly price by that churn.
detail: 100 customers falling to 95 is 5% churn. At $100,000 a month from 100 clients ($1,000 each), LTV is $1,000 / 5% = $20,000. Average churn over recent months, since churn from a year ago may no longer apply; churn calculations may overstate LTV.
numbers: 5% churn; $1,000 monthly price; $20,000 LTV; if 5 new clients a month hold revenue steady.

### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#03 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:04:50
claim: Method 3, sales velocity: when revenue has been flat for a few months, monthly revenue divided by new customers a month approximates LTV.
detail: At about $100,000 a month and 10 new deals a month, LTV is roughly $10,000. It only works near equilibrium, when lost customers are replaced. It is the method he uses most often.
numbers: $100K/month / 10 sales = ~$10K LTV.
applies_to: Businesses with stagnant revenue

### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:03
claim: A recurring business has a hypothetical max revenue equal to new customers per month times LTV, which you can compute without changing anything.
detail: With 100 customers at $1,000, 5% churn and 20 sales a month, the hypothetical max is 20 x $20,000 = $400,000 a month, so he says you don't need to change anything, just wait to hit it.
numbers: 20 sales x $20K LTV = $400K/month hypothetical max.
### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#05 | CASE | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:06:57
claim: Scenario: 10 sales a month at $5,000 a month with 20% churn caps at $250,000 a month, whether you start at $100K or $500K.
detail: LTV is $25,000; 10 x $25,000 = $250,000. A business at $100K grows to it, one at $500K shrinks to it, and one sitting at $250K is stagnant and must change sales, price or churn.
numbers: $5,000 price; 20% churn; $25,000 LTV; ceiling $250,000/month.

### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: retention-ltv | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:39
claim: To reach $1M a month from a $250K ceiling, fix churn first because it also raises LTV and makes later scaling profitable.
detail: Three routes: 4x sales to 40 a month, 4x price to $20K, or cut churn from 20% to 5%. Raising the price might be difficult and represents a serious risk. Scaling sales first might build infrastructure too quickly with weak hires while churn stays high, and margins probably compress. Fix churn, then scale: 40 sales at 5% churn gives about $4M a month.
numbers: $250K to $1M via 40 sales, $20K price or 5% churn; fixing churn then 4x sales gives ~$4M/month; margin example 30% falls to 10%.
### YT-FXzDLLdxsCk#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:47
claim: Weak LTV is one of the most common problems in companies at $3M to $10M in revenue.
detail: They do not make enough per customer, so margin squeezes and limits their ability to spend on acquisition.
numbers: $3M-$10M revenue range.
