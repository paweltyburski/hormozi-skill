### SC-09#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 6 Optimize (20–49 employees): the founder becomes a leader over two full layers of managers, and the business must grow by doing everything better rather than doing more.
detail: You now lead directors who lead managers who lead individual contributors, and can no longer do things yourself — everything goes through people. Overall constraint: time and resources wasted on a growing workload. Constraint → graduation:
- Product: two products slow improvements → incremental improvement process, pruning.
- Marketing: ads convert worse on colder traffic, CAC rises → weekly ad assembly.
- Sales: core-product closing becomes inconsistent → training system, individual coaching, team cadence.
- CS: churn rises with volume and the new product → cohort segmentation, activation points.
- IT: people join and leave with IP, data, access → cybersecurity, on/offboarding.
- Recruiting: needs exceed your network → headhunting, multi-interviewer process.
- HR: senior talent expects full packages → benefits, retirement, advanced comp.
- Finance: spending without growing → granular data, balance sheet, AR/AP, insurance.
Next: 50–99 employees, Categorize.
numbers: ~2.1M businesses reach 20–49 employees.

### SC-09#02 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: scaling-constraints | fw: RICE | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:37
claim: With two products slowing each other's improvement, run one incremental improvement process: list every problem, score gain, cost and risk, re-rank, and fix them one at a time.
detail: Steps:
1. List all problems with both products.
2. Estimate for each the gain (how much it will make), cost (time), and risk (likelihood it works); optionally how many people it affects.
3. Re-rank by highest impact and lowest cost.
4. Fix them in sequence.
Any scoring framework works — RICE (reach, impact, confidence, effort) is one; they all share the same three or four elements because they are all prioritization of resources.

### SC-09#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:08
claim: Put the whole team on one problem at a time instead of splitting people across problems concurrently; concentrated effort plus the urgency of other fires solves each one far faster.
detail: The best entrepreneurs talk mostly about focus and prioritization, and it is a cycle, not a box to tick: resources expand, so you re-prune and re-prioritize again. Say openly that a real fire (e.g. conversion rate) will not be discussed because it is not the biggest problem. With four people on one problem and other fires burning, it gets solved fast, then all four move to the next. Like a magnifying glass, spread effort only warms the paper; focused effort burns through. Contingent priorities (you must open the door before walking through) order themselves; most of the time you have a shared pool of resources and must choose.

### SC-09#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:03
claim: Missed opportunities are not losses; solve existing problems before chasing new opportunities, because chasing scales the old problems plus new unknown ones.
detail: Leila's distinction changed Hormozi's outlook. Missed opportunities stressed him for years, but you are not losing that money — you just could be making more. Problems exist in the business now. Pursuing a new opportunity instead multiplies current problems and adds the ones the new opportunity brings, with fewer resources to handle them. Ticking and tying off problems keeps things contained, and the more he focused on problems the more the existing opportunity expanded — growing by getting better, not doing more.

### SC-09#06 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:52
claim: Letting a community upvote which features to build is a mediocre method, because customers judge only by capabilities they know exist.
detail: It can work, especially in low-tech businesses. But customers cannot ask for what they don't know is possible, so you miss order-of-magnitude jumps. The most innovative businesses understand their technology better than the customer and surprise them — e.g. nobody would ask a space company for a phone, yet satellites could make one possible.

### SC-09#07 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:56
claim: Prune features with two survey questions, then quietly delete the lowest-ranked feature and watch what happens.
detail: Questions: "If we got rid of everything but one, which would you keep?" and "If we kept everything but one, which would make no difference to you?" This surfaces the top two or three and bottom two or three features. Delete the bottom one without announcing it — asking permission to remove anything upsets everyone. If nobody reacts, you earn more for doing less and the product is better. If some complain, before restoring it ask whether those are customers you want to grow; sometimes a strategic cut is worth it.
numbers: Example of an acceptable strategic cut: losing ~5% of customers for a healthier product overall.

### SC-09#10 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: unit-economics | fw: Ad Assembly Process | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:18
claim: When ads convert worse on colder traffic and CAC rises, assemble ads at volume every week from 50 hooks, 3–5 bodies and 1–3 CTAs.
detail: Unlike stage 5 (LTV too low), here CAC keeps rising as you go into more platforms and spend more per day, so audiences fatigue faster and need more variety. Ogilvy's point that the headline spends 80 cents of the ad dollar means most people never pass the hook, so 80% of creative time goes into hooks; recycling new hooks on the same body reaches new people and prevents banner blindness. What usually limits ad scale is not the market but creative quality — converting the coldest audiences takes much better creative. You stop making ads and start assembling them.
numbers: 50 hooks × 3–5 bodies × 1–3 CTAs = 150–750 ad variations per week; hook ≈ 80% of the ad's value.

### SC-09#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: Ad Assembly Process | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:19
claim: Source hooks in descending order of likelihood to work: your past ad winners, your content winners, others' ad winners, others' content winners, then ad-library research.
detail: 1. Your past winning ads — do more of that. 2. Your winning content: a top Skool ad uses a content hook about not wishing, at 85, you had fewer crazy stories. 3. Other people's ad winners, ideally from unrelated industries (a hotel chain's hook can fit your offer). 4. Other people's content winners. 5. Public ad libraries: an ad running for one or two months is probably a winner. The magic is often splicing the first half of one hook (e.g. from a TikTok Shop lash seller) with the second half of another. Early on with no winners, just write 50.
numbers: Ads live 1–2+ months in an ad library are likely winners.

### SC-09#12 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:56
claim: The body of an ad restates the offer through eight value drivers: the four value-equation elements and their mirror images.
detail: Dream outcome vs the nightmare situation (e.g. a black-and-white "this is your life now" ad). Perceived likelihood: the risk of not acting vs the low risk of your option. Time: slow, delayed alternatives vs your faster way. Effort and sacrifice: how hard other ways are vs how easy yours is. Show more good stuff and less bad stuff on both sides.

### SC-09#19 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: management-ops | fw: Game Tape Review | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:41

detail: Hormozi learned this the hard way: when the team listed everyone's mistakes, nobody wanted their call reviewed. ### SC-09#21 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: unit-economics | fw: Cohort Analysis | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:31
claim: Build a cohort table of retention by month since sign-up, find the month where drop-off spikes, and intervene one to two months before it.
detail: Each row is a sign-up month, each column month 1, 2, 3…; newer cohorts have fewer columns. A portfolio company's table showed a big drop between months four and five. People usually decide to leave one or two months before they do, so place the incentive to stay earlier than the average churn point. Examine both what happened at sign-up (which sets up success or failure) and the four to eight weeks before the churn point.
numbers: Decision to leave typically 1–2 months before cancelling; look 4–8 weeks back from the churn point.

### SC-09#22 | DECISION_RULE | topic: retention-ltv | also: sales-team | fw: Cohort Analysis | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:03
claim: Segment churn by product, by sales rep and by assigned CSM, so you don't overhaul everything for a one-off cause.
detail: Unsegmented churn leads you to solve the wrong problem for the wrong people on the wrong product — e.g. a level-one product may peak in churn at month four and a level-two product at month ten. Churn by sales rep reveals bad expectations; churn by CSM or account manager reveals service issues. Don't change the whole product for one rep, one bad month or one poor onboarding cohort.
numbers: Example churn peaks: month 4 (entry product) vs month 10 (second product).

### SC-09#24 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: Five M's of Activation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:27:35
claim: The Five M's of activation find and install the behaviour that makes customers stay: map, measure, make a hypothesis, monitor, modify.
detail: 1. Map the journey: every milestone from onboarding to ongoing delivery — both what the customer does (measure metrics, run ads) and what your team does (calls, emails, guides), colour-coded.
2. Measure patterns: find which behaviours your best, longest-staying customers share; interview them through the journey and ask what they do that isn't on it.
3. Make a hypothesis: e.g. 8 of 10 best customers do two things others don't — that may be the activation point.
4. Monitor results: test whether requiring it improves engagement and retention, one change at a time.
5. Modify onboarding and the offer: rebuild the journey around the activation point and, if possible, build it into the guarantee.

### SC-09#26 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: - | fw: Five M's of Activation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:31:28

### SC-09#31 | FRAMEWORK | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:40:40
claim: At 20–49 people, know the five basic cybersecurity protocols well enough to have someone install them.
detail: 1. Firewall: monitors and filters traffic in and out of the network.
2. Encryption: encodes data so only authorized people can read it, mainly legal and financial.
3. Multi-factor authentication on accounts across the company.
4. Software updates and patch management: outdated software is a common breach point.
5. Access control: the fewest possible people on high-risk systems (financial, legal, transactional, customer, confidential), with a list of who has access so a breach can be traced.

### SC-09#37 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:51:34

### SC-09#38 | FRAMEWORK | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:52:37
claim: Choose benefits in five steps: assess needs, analyze providers, appraise plans, assess support and compliance, then adopt and adjust.
detail: 1. Assess needs: what your workforce wants by age group (Acquisition.com: many first-time parents, so dependent coverage), plus legal obligations by size and state, and budget.
2. Analyze providers: popular in your industry; experience, reviews and technology — a dated portal means people never use benefits.
3. Appraise plans: compare the 3–5 plans offered on type, cost, coverage split and extras; have the provider model costs for an individual contributor, a manager and a director.
4. Assess support and compliance: pick a provider that handles regulation and administration, or you'll need a hire just for that.
5. Adopt and adjust: sell the benefits to the team like a celebration, collect feedback quarterly, reappraise rates annually at renewal.
numbers: Benefits add ~15–30% of payroll; an in-house benefits administrator ~$80,000/year.

### SC-09#44 | DECISION_RULE | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: Five S's of Advanced Compensation | novelty: canon | ts: 01:06:11
