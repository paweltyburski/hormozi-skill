### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: other | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: There are seven ways people quote how big or valuable they are, and each deserves a different level of trust.
detail: The seven: extrapolated contract value, lifetime revenue, business valuation, yearly revenue, yearly profit or EBITDA, net free cash flow, net worth. His verdicts: contract value no; lifetime revenue no; valuation yes, but only if the multiple and its basis are defended; revenue only together with profit; net free cash flow yes; net worth yes. No single number settles it, read them together.

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Discount any "per month" claim built by multiplying contract value; ask how much cash was actually collected and how enforceable the contracts are.
detail: Example: an event sells 100 people yearly contracts at $30,000 each and announces "$3M a month". The buyers pay roughly $2,500 a month, so real collections were about $250,000, one time. Such contract extrapolation is credible only when contracts are secured against assets or come from solid companies, or when the customer base has negative churn (spend grows over time, as with large CRM vendors).
numbers: 100 buyers x $30,000/yr contract = $3M claimed; about $250,000 actually collected at the event ($2,500/month each).

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#03 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:36
claim: Hormozi uses "revenue" to mean cash collected, not contract value, and notes newer founders mix the two up.
detail: Cash collected is what was processed and received by the business. Revenue differs from cash by business type: in a contract-based service they diverge, in purely transactional e-commerce they are close. Net revenue also removes refunds and chargebacks.

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#04 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:47
claim: Lifetime revenue is an inflating metric that is great for the ego and impresses people who don't understand it.
detail: He notes it does not mean that amount is earned per year. He compares it to a McDonald's employee collecting $1M over a whole career. When someone cites a $50M or $100M business, ask whether they mean yearly revenue, profit or total since the start.
numbers: A McDonald's employee can collect $1M over an entire working life.
### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:02:34
claim: Hormozi admits he uses the larger cumulative figure in headlines, while stating yearly revenue and profit when being transparent.
detail: A "$122M revenue" headline was cumulative; the yearly run rate was in the mid-$30Ms for the previous three to four years. A software-vendor award marked $100M total collected in about three and a half years, and he expected the following year to land just under $100M in a single year.
numbers: about $122M cumulative vs mid-$30M per year (around 2021); $100M cumulative in roughly 3.5 years.

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#06 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:27
claim: Valuation multiples rise with company size and are almost always applied to EBITDA, not revenue.
detail: Under about $5M revenue, businesses mostly sell at 2-3x EBITDA, sometimes 4x with a clear competitive advantage. Mid-market ($10M-$100M revenue) might get about 6-8x. Above $100M revenue, 12-15x is possible. Plenty of deals close at 1-3x and simply go unreported. Revenue multiples are rare: only fast-growing firms reinvesting heavily for future gross profit. A business that needs the founder's face fetches a lower multiple.
numbers: under $5M revenue: 2-3x EBITDA (up to 4x); $10M-$100M: might get 6-8x; over $100M: 12-15x. Example of 5x on $2M EBITDA for a $10M-revenue company that does not rely on its owner.
applies_to: Valuing or judging small and mid-market owner-run companies.
### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#07 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:23
claim: When someone brags about a very high EBITDA multiple, it is more likely they were making almost no profit than that they have a truly spectacular business.
detail: Example: a $5M business earning only $10,000 a year that sells for $5M has been sold at 500x EBITDA. Such figures are quoted because they sound impressive. Also distrust anyone claiming they could get "15x top line" with no basis.
numbers: $10,000 profit, $5M sale price = 500x EBITDA.
### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#08 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:02
claim: Low profit with high revenue can still be valuable if everything is deliberately reinvested in growth; read revenue and profit together.
detail: A software company with about $20M revenue and $500,000 yearly profit sold for $168M because buyers saw that profit was being reinvested for growth. He contrasts a business with $500,000 profit on $1M revenue, which he calls far less valuable (reasoning unclear in audio). Reinvestment logic is most typical of software.
numbers: $20M revenue, $500,000 profit, $168M sale price (about 336x profit, 8.4x revenue).

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#09 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:26
claim: Net free cash flow is what the owner can take out after reinvesting enough to keep and grow the competitive advantage, and it is the best base for a valuation.
detail: He credits it as the main metric of the best-known investors, who prefer businesses needing little added capital. Multiply it by a multiple and you land near what an investor would pay, apart from monopoly-type strategic deals. Question to ask an owner: what do you take home as owner earnings or dividends each year?

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:21
claim: Take cash out of the business every month while it is profitable, because most businesses fail and nearly all are never sold.
detail: His view is that about 99 of 100 businesses never sell for the hoped-for amount (asset or garage sale at best). Extracting dividends de-risks the owner. He supports it with the observation that only two companies from the Fortune 500 of 50 years ago remain, so building personal wealth during the business matters. He admits this is less popular with venture-style founders.
numbers: "99 times out of 100" a business never sells (his estimate); 2 firms of today's Fortune 500 were there 50 years ago.

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#11 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:14
claim: Entrepreneur net worth has two parts: after-tax cash extracted and invested, plus the owned share of business value at a fair valuation.
detail: He regards net worth as the most honest scoreboard because it is what is left after everything. He also defines "ultra high net worth" by investable assets, not total value, and says most of his own wealth was still in business valuations.
numbers: Ultra high net worth threshold quoted as over $30M in investable assets (unclear in audio).

### YT-al_WXFDbN8I#12 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: finance-exit | also: other | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:24
claim: Five questions expose an inflated money claim.
detail: 1. Is this lifetime revenue or yearly? 2. Is it extrapolated contract value rather than cash collected? 3. If it is a valuation, what multiple, on top line or bottom line? 4. What is the net free cash flow? 5. What do you take out as dividends yearly, and (if willing) what is your net worth?
