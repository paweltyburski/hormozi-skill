### POD-0762#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: pricing, management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:47
claim: Hormozi's four most common strategic traps, each a choice between short-term pain and long-term pain: mispricing, overpaid compensation, overexpansion, and the "woman in the red dress".
detail: Mispriced: full capacity, no profit, afraid that raising prices loses customers. Compensation: e.g. paying therapists 50% of revenue, afraid renegotiation loses them. Overexpansion: growing locations or clients too fast, abdicating, so delivery and reputation decay while payroll forces more selling. Red dress: a new idea looks perfect because only its upside is known. Each is a tax on past ignorance; usually accept the short-term pain. He opens with an asker who wanted CLOSER training at a 40% close rate — not his constraint.

### POD-0762#02 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:38
claim: To tell delegation from abdication, check what happens after the hand-off: if things keep running, you delegated; if they get worse, you abdicated.
detail: Hormozi: proper delegation means training and bringing someone up to speed, which impatient owners skip when expanding fast; the bill arrives later as poor delivery.

### POD-0762#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:49
claim: Hormozi: lead with the main topic that attracts the buyer and only sprinkle personal interests in; judge content by buyer metrics, not views.
detail: Business-first content with a mention of training or dessert keeps every business viewer and adds affinity; starting with food draws the wrong first person. Do not borrow lessons from entertainment creators: education is harder to consume, so fewer views are expected. Content costs resources, so ask which piece raises the chance of reaching the desired person.

### POD-0762#09 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: unit-economics, retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:16:46
claim: Very low-price tax service for tiny businesses with 1% monthly churn is told not to touch price and to pour everything into marketing and selling.
business: professional-services — tax and accounting for small businesses (market described as businesses with $0-600K revenue), deliberately the cheapest in the market, can onboard 10-15 new customers a day; possibly the same company as #04 (unclear)
detail: Owner asks whether to experiment with prices. Hormozi: if you go cheap there is no advantage in being second cheapest, and this works because the back end was built to make good margin at that price. With a huge value gap and tiny churn, focus on the front end. A price test is only worth it if operationally simple: tag that cohort, compare conversion and churn; churn moving from 1% to 2% halves LTV, so unless price more than doubles it is not worth the risk.
numbers: 1% monthly churn; 10-15 new customers/day capacity; churn 1%→2% halves LTV
speakers: owner: business facts; Hormozi: diagnosis, pricing math and advice
applies_to: low-price recurring service with very low churn and spare onboarding capacity

### POD-0762#10 | BENCHMARK | topic: pricing | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:56
claim: Hormozi: doubling price does not usually halve conversion (he illustrates with a ~25% drop), and cheaper prices bring lower churn but not proportionally lower.
detail: Worked example: at 20% margins, a 20% price rise with a 5% drop in conversion nets ~19% more revenue and nearly doubles profit. Fewer customers also cost less to serve. Owners on sales calls feel the fall from five yeses in ten to three and panic; do the math instead. Price, churn (price / churn = LTV) and conversion are the three inputs.

### POD-0762#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: management-ops | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:07
claim: Hormozi, to everyone: a test has a guaranteed cost and no guaranteed upside, so run one only when you can argue it hits the constraint.
detail: A price change forces new training, scripts, point-of-sale, systems and contracts. Frequent tests exhaust teams; businesses often earned more when he stepped away, which is why he meets portfolio companies about once a year to change a few big things.

### POD-0762#13 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Affiliate Army | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:24:22
claim: Hormozi: people promote others' offers when it adds value to their own audience, so design the affiliate offer as a benefit to the end user, not just to the promoter.
detail: Ask what would leave the partner's list better off after promoting you, then provide it (exclusive Q&A, a free service the partner bundles into their offer for margin). That gives the promoter a reason to send and the audience a reason to click, without spending the partner's goodwill.
