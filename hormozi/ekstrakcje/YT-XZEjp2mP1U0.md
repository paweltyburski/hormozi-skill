### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:37
claim: The "pie equation" is inflow divided by outflow rate, which gives the level a recurring business will plateau at if nothing changes.
detail: Inflow is a rate (new customers or new revenue per month), outflow is a churn percentage. The result is a hypothetical maximum, reached when monthly losses equal monthly additions. It shows whether you are growing or shrinking and where you will level out. Hormozi says he uses it about five times a day, including in coaching.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:03:26
claim: Customer ceiling: 5 new customers a month at 10% monthly churn caps the base at 50; 2 a month at 10% shrinks a 30-customer base toward 20.
detail: With 30 customers, 10% churn loses 3 and 5 sign-ups outgrow it, but the base grows only until losses reach 5. Past that point nothing changes unless sign-ups rise or churn falls. The same maths exposes a shrinking business early.
numbers: 5 / 0.10 = 50 customers; 2 / 0.10 = 20 customers; starting at 30.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#03 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:02
claim: Lifetime value equals average monthly revenue per client divided by monthly churn, which gives a rough ceiling for what you can spend to acquire one.
detail: $1,000 a month with 20% monthly churn (a five-month average life) gives $5,000. He says one could spend somewhat under $2,000 per customer, noting this is rough and below a 3:1 ratio. The figure is a number to market against.
numbers: $1,000 / 0.20 = $5,000 revenue LTV.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:26
claim: Revenue version: $10,000 of new monthly business at 10% monthly revenue churn caps at $100,000 a month.
detail: Compare the ceiling with where you are now: at $200,000 something is wrong; at $50,000 revenue roughly doubles before it caps. With churn above zero, growth slows every month because the same percentage takes a larger absolute amount.
numbers: $10,000 / 0.10 = $100,000 a month.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:11
claim: Growth in a recurring business slows toward the ceiling, and realistically a business stalls at 80-90% of the theoretical maximum because of volatility.
detail: Sign up 10 a month at 20% churn: month one 10, month two 18, then smaller gains each month as churn eats a bigger absolute amount. Only negative churn avoids the plateau, which most service businesses lack.
numbers: Practical ceiling about 80-90% of the formula result.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: lead-getters | fw: Referral Rate minus Churn | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:48
claim: The "golden ratio": when the share of customers who refer a new one each month exceeds the monthly churn rate, the business grows without paid ads.
detail: Example: 10% of customers refer someone monthly and 5% leave, so the ratio exceeds 1 and the base keeps growing. He calls it net negative churn. It rarely lasts forever because referral rates fall or churn rises, but a stretch of it produces very fast growth.
numbers: 10% referral rate against 5% churn.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#07 | EXAMPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:12:31
claim: Combine the two forms: 20 new clients a month with a $5,000 lifetime value levels out at $100,000 a month of revenue.
detail: Inside the business this does not feel visible: 20 clients at $1,000 a month looks like $20,000 and you cannot tell if you are on track. Recurring revenue takes time, so staying on course, without chasing new ideas, matters. Fix by raising price, lowering churn or raising inflow.
numbers: 20 clients a month x $5,000 = $100,000 a month ceiling.

### YT-XZEjp2mP1U0#08 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: scaling-constraints | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:20
claim: His first three questions to a struggling owner are: what is your inflow, what is your churn, what is your average price per user, then what is your current revenue.
detail: From these four data points he can tell whether the business grows or shrinks, how fast, and what level it will plateau at. The three levers on any plateau are price, churn and inflow.
