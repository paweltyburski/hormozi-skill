### POD-0540#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:01
claim: Hormozi's personal way to bucket what an entrepreneur can sell: seven categories, each in a physical and a digital version, giving 14 things to sell. Everything he has thought of fits.
detail: 1 products (water bottle, PDF), 2 services (massage, lawn mowing; ads, software, buying media), 3 access (a leased building, a content library), 4 attention (billboard, Facebook and Google audiences), 5 risk (insurance, cyber cover), 6 money (lending; interest is the price), 7 endorsement or brand (logo licence for a share of sales, a verification check mark).
numbers: 7 categories x 2 formats = 14.
### POD-0540#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:54
claim: Real offers combine categories, for example a concert is access to an experience plus drinks, T-shirts and recorded versions.
detail: Thinking in the buckets helps spot add-on revenue in other categories around a core offer.

### POD-0540#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:05
claim: With about $100 to start, you will most of the time likely start by selling your time, which is often services; with capital, the other categories open up.
detail: You can't sell money unless you have money, or insure risk without enough capital to cover the claims. An established business can add a risk category.
numbers: Example start budget: $100.
applies_to: Beginners.
### POD-0540#04 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:09
claim: A guarantee can be sold as insurance: customers pay for it, as with a paid extended warranty.
detail: An established owner of a product or service business can sell the associated risk as an add-on, rather than only giving the guarantee free.

### POD-0540#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:54
claim: Pick the first thing to sell by crossing two lists: the person you can give the most value to, and what it costs you to deliver that value against the money you have.
detail: The sweet spot is the customer who gets the most value for the least outlay from you. Resources, skills and experience decide the rest.

### POD-0540#06 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:41
claim: Mine your past for industry experience: family members' jobs, odd jobs, and adjacent roles all count.
detail: Hormozi cites a parent's profession, school jobs (catering, kitchen, warehouse) and learning both sides of a service. Direct personal experience with the problem is the strongest recommendation for a newcomer, since you cannot guess what customers want but you know what you wanted.

### POD-0540#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:43
claim: Entering a truly new industry carries "ignorance debt", while experience absorbed second-hand (a roommate or sibling in the field) transfers.
detail: Moving from catering to payment processing after one video means a steep learning curve; someone whose sibling spent years in it already knows a lot.

### POD-0540#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:33
claim: Place yourself on the easy-to-sell, hard-to-deliver spectrum by skill: weak at selling means leaning on heavier delivery at first.
detail: Over time you find the version that sells well and is not overwhelming to deliver, which drives profit and scalability.

### POD-0540#09 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: pricing | fw: Delivery Cube | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:31
claim: The speed and convenience lens has three parts, hours open, days per week, and response time, and the cost sits either with you or with the customer.
detail: Always-open with a 24-hour reply differs from nine-to-five with a three-minute reply. Overstaffing spares customers waiting but adds inefficiency; full utilisation puts the wait on them. Both strategies have produced big companies. Live beats recorded in perceived value; live in person is most valuable but least scalable.

### POD-0540#10 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery | fw: Delivery Cube | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:11
claim: With only your own time to sell, multiply your price by 10 or 100 and add high-touch delivery rather than discount.
detail: Hormozi's illustration: picture a $10,000 service as a $100,000 one; the exercise forces the question of what value would justify the price, and often the ideas stay profitable at the old price. The reverse test asks which perceived-value items cost almost nothing to give.
numbers: Example: $10,000 thing priced as $100,000.
