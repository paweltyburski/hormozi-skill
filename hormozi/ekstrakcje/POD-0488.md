### POD-0488#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:59
claim: Selling a cash-flowing business swaps income for a lump sum, and a business owner worth a billion on paper chose not to sell because replacing the cash flow is hard.
detail: The billionaire-valued owner told Hormozi that after a sale you have no cash flow and must buy real estate or other assets to replace it, so he keeps the business. A company that is squeezed efficiently becomes valuable enough to sell, but a sale removes the sense of control a perpetual asset gives.

### POD-0488#06 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:32:08

### POD-0488#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:36:28
claim: During a sale process, owners fall into treating the acquirer as the customer and optimise decisions for an imagined valuation; this was his mistake.
detail: Once he decided to entertain a sale he kept asking how a private equity buyer would value each move. The process took about two years: roughly one to fix everything and make the company sellable, one to sell. During the sale you cannot launch initiatives or make big hires, so the owner mostly maintains while the deal flips between on and off.

### POD-0488#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:37:21
claim: Fixing a company to sell makes you want to keep it, and when the business runs without you the lack of purpose can feel depressing.
detail: After a year of fixes he had more cash flow and efficiency, and doubted selling (the saying he cites: selling makes you rich, keeping makes you wealthy). For about 12 months he was not needed in the business and felt low, even with security. He concludes owners need a mission, not only money. His earlier ventures started from fear of being poor; this one does not.

### POD-0488#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit, strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:20
claim: In an inflationary period the best cash-flow businesses have low capital expense, meaning output can rise tenfold without much change in cost basis.
detail: Avoid models that need inventory, long supply chains, manufacturing or heavy equipment, where capacity requires buying more stuff. Services and digital businesses are the low-capital end; software is mixed because the development team may count as capital expense. Such businesses tend to throw off more cash and hold more pricing power.

### POD-0488#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: unit-economics | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:54
claim: Business types that have lasted a century or more, such as insurance and banking, signal very high leverage and gross margin; gross margin is the extra cost of one more unit.
detail: He reads longevity through wars and depressions as proof of cash generation. Pharmaceutical example: the first pill costs about $100M to develop, later ones about a penny, so a $10 pill has an enormous margin. He calls insurance a business made of math with no capital items.
numbers: First pill ≈ $100M, each following pill ≈ $0.01, sold for $10.

### POD-0488#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: unit-economics | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:22:21
claim: Small business owners often mark up cost 2-3x, giving a 50-67% margin that leaves little to pay for everything else; for his own service businesses, Hormozi requires gross margin above 80%.
detail: Starting at roughly 50% gross margin makes profit very hard, since the remaining half must cover all other costs. His own service rule is price at least five times the cost of goods: if delivery costs $100 a month, the minimum he charges is $500. The question becomes how to deliver value at the lowest cost to you, not how much you can charge.
numbers: Above 80% gross margin = 5x cost; $100/month cost => $500/month minimum price; markups of 2-3x given as the example of how small owners price.
### POD-0488#12 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:23:14
claim: Pick a narrow avatar early so the service can be productised; repeating the same work makes delivery faster and cheaper, which creates margin to reinvest.
detail: Doing everything custom keeps cost high and margin thin, forcing either fees owners are afraid to charge or slow growth. Doing the same job repeatedly yields process breakthroughs similar to what technology does to costs over time. The margin then funds hiring the best people and marketing.
