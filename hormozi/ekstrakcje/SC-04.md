### SC-04#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: sales-process | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:47
claim: Stage 1 Monetize: a one-person business that has made no money yet; the founder becomes a "starter", and graduates by making the first paid sale.
detail: Headcount 1, role starter (you stop gathering information and start), leadership just you. Graduating makes you one of ~30M US companies with at least $1 of revenue; only ~9% of people own a business, so this alone puts you in the top 10%. Constraint → graduation step:
- Product: not good enough to sell → remove the hidden costs and release V1.
- Marketing: nobody knows you sell → tell them, lead with proof, use free ad credits.
- Sales: you cannot sell → sell with free customers' results.
- Customer service: free users do not like or use it → ask them why.
- IT: no basic tools → free tools found through groups, forums and search.
- Recruiting: missing skills or time → people you know, freelancers.
- HR: freelancers do not deliver → vendor agreements.
- Finance: no way to get paid → a processor (ideally two payment methods) and a first deposit.
numbers: ~30M US companies with ≥$1 revenue; ~9% of people own a business.

### SC-04#02 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, sales-process | fw: Perceived Hidden Costs | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:34
claim: To turn a V1 into something sellable, ask people straight out why they wouldn't buy, then eliminate the non-monetary costs they mention.
detail: Hidden costs are every non-monetary cost that leads someone to refuse. Questions to ask:
- Why won't you pay for it?
- What is making this a no for you?
- What change would turn it into a yes?
- What would it take to get there?
"What would it take?" is a favorite. Leila learned it from Alex and uses it every day: when a person declines, ask what would change that. List the largest friction points, then choose which you can and want to remove.

### SC-04#03 | EXAMPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers | fw: Perceived Hidden Costs | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:29
claim: When overweight people would not buy personal training, the reasons had nothing to do with the sessions themselves: the drive, the parking, eating less and changing their lifestyle.
detail: Leila, as a personal trainer, wondered why people 50 pounds overweight would not buy training. The hidden costs: a 40-minute drive to the gym, five minutes to find parking, having to eat less, and having to change how they live (e.g. eating out every night). None of it was about the workout itself, but all of it made yes harder. Hypothetical fix: pick them up from home, train them, and have dinner with them every night. Whether you are willing to do that is a separate question, but it shows where the friction is.
numbers: 40-minute drive, 5 minutes parking, client 50 lb overweight.

### SC-04#04 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: offers, pricing | fw: What Would It Take | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:14
claim: The "magic question" fixes the price first and asks what the client would need in order to be excited to buy, so they design the offer for you.
detail: Structure:
1. State a concrete price, timeframe and goal, e.g. $3,000 for three months to lose 20 pounds.
2. Ask what it would need to look like for them to be excited to move forward.
3. Let them paint the ideal state.
4. Work backwards to what you can actually deliver to make yes as easy as possible.
Why it works: "What would it take?" assumes the purchase and works back to features. The magic question adds price on top, so the prospect has to solve for the offer at that price instead of objecting to it.
numbers: Example: $3,000 for 3 months to lose 20 lb.

### SC-04#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:18
claim: Many "I won't do that for clients" decisions are made without considering price; there is probably a price at which you would, and Hormozi advocates charging a premium early to deliver a different service.
detail: Leila would not drive to pick a client up. But would she do it for $1,000 a session? Probably. Sometimes the price at which you would do something is what a customer will pay you to remove one of their hidden costs. He advocates charging above most of the market early, so you can deliver a different and more valuable service. Do not refuse unscalable service because it would break at 10 clients when you have zero. That is like refusing to work out because your shirts might get too tight. Make money first, then use it to make the business scalable.
numbers: Example: would you pick a client up for $1,000 per session?

### SC-04#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:43
claim: Value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (time delay × effort and sacrifice); increase the numerator and reduce the denominator to make a product worth more.
detail: Hormozi created the equation (central to $100M Offers) to make "create value" actionable: the more value you build, the more money you can trade it for. The top two terms add value. The bottom two are negatives to cut; as they near zero, value heads toward infinity.
Example, with relaxation as the dream outcome. Xanax has high perceived likelihood, around 15 minutes of delay and nearly no effort, hence a multi-billion-dollar drug people buy gladly. Meditation offers the same outcome, but people doubt they'll succeed (lower likelihood, more risk), it takes months to master, and it requires effort and giving things up. A meditation seller must prove the method is likelier to work, shorten the delay (relaxed in 5 minutes, proficient in a few weeks), and lower effort and sacrifice (new activities they dislike, things they enjoy giving up, such as phone time).
Shorthand for the four levers: good, fast, risk-free (consistent), easy.

### SC-04#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-process | also: offers, lead-getters | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:47
claim: Proof beats promise: whether people believe your promise depends on your proof, so sell paid customers with the results of your free ones.
detail: Once you start charging, prospects naturally ask who else you have worked with and want testimonials and reviews. Illustration: two Mexican restaurants on the same street. One has a single five-star review; the other averages 4.4 stars across 20,000 reviews. Most people pick the second, because so much proof makes it lower risk. This is why the roadmap insists on doing work free at the start: it produces the proof.
numbers: 1 review at 5.0 vs 20,000 reviews at 4.4 — volume of proof wins.

### SC-04#08 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-process | also: product-delivery | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:41
claim: Hormozi's own practice: he never starts trying to sell until he has 10 or more testimonials.
detail: Two reasons. First, selling becomes much easier. Second, it gives you conviction that what you do is actually good. If you cannot get testimonials when the work is free, you certainly will not get them when people pay. Get good first.
numbers: 10+ testimonials before charging.

### SC-04#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: offers, lead-getters | fw: Proof Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:41
claim: Not all proof is equal; each element sits on a continuum from weak to strong, and the ideal testimonial is strong on every one.
detail: Stronger over weaker:
- In-person over virtual (e.g. a staff member who lost 100 lb).
- Live over recorded.
- Raw over processed: phone selfies are more believable than polished video.
- Show over tell (before and after).
- Others saying it over you saying it.
- Like the prospect over unlike them: do not sell IT to tech founders with plumbing testimonials.
- Specific over generic: "25 appointments a week for six months" beats "John's great."
- A mix of big and small results over bad or none, since some prospects will not believe only big ones.
- New over old: people assume your latest testimonial is your latest good result.
- Lots over one (overwhelming social proof).
- Numbers over none.
- Track record over none.
The worst proof is a single, generic, text-only, 10-year-old "I recommend" from someone unlike the prospect, with no numbers. Many websites use exactly that.

### SC-04#10 | EXAMPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: verticals | fw: Proof Continuum | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:15
claim: At Gym Launch, matching testimonials to each local market's demographics produced a large lift in throughput.
detail: Gym Launch ran ads for many gyms. Hormozi tested which testimonials to show relative to the market. In predominantly Black markets, Black testimonials worked better. In predominantly white markets, white testimonials worked better. When targeting women, female testimonials worked better. It is common sense, but almost no one does it. Make the proof look like the prospect.

### SC-04#11 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: verticals | fw: Overwhelming Social Proof | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:03

### SC-04#13 | SCRIPT | topic: outreach | also: sales-process, pricing | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:15
claim: First paid customers can come from a warm-outreach offer: limited spots, a big discount, and in return a promise to use it, give feedback and leave a review if deserved.
detail: The script (an excerpt from $100M Leads) runs in four moves:
1. Scarcity and a reason: you are only taking a handful (e.g. five) people.
2. Benefit of the scarcity: so you can give each the attention needed for "brag-worthy" results.
3. A deep discount (e.g. 80% off), conditional on them using it, giving feedback and leaving a strong review if they think it deserves one.
4. A fairness check close: "Does that sound fair?"
It piggybacks on the results you already got for free customers.
numbers: 5 spots at 80% off in the example.

### SC-04#14 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:00
claim: Raise the price by 20% after every five sales: free, then 80% off, 60% off, 40% off, 20% off, full price, and keep going.
detail: Each rung of five customers earns the right to the next price. Do not stop at "full price"; keep raising in 20% steps. The aim is to find the price that gets you the most customers with the highest LTV, and you only find the threshold by continuing to test upwards.
numbers: +20% every 5 sales.

### SC-04#15 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:00
claim: Gym Launch's package went from $6,000 to $50,000 through repeated price raises while the team searched for the best price.
detail: The sequence: $6k, $8k, $10k, $12k, $16k, $20k, $22k, $30k, $35k, $50k, and beyond. The goal was to find where the customer count and LTV combination was best for the business.
numbers: $6,000 → $50,000 package price.

### SC-04#16 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:46

### SC-04#17 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:33
claim: Separate customer problems into result issues (they did not get what they wanted) and experience issues (they did not get it the way they wanted); most people are more likely to stick around with a good experience and a bad result than with a good result and a bad experience.
detail: At stage 1, talk to free customers who do not like or use the product. Do not feel above it or worry about scalability, and list every problem they show. Sort each into result or experience. Because experience retains better than results, the novice has an advantage: you do not need to be the best yet, only to show you are trying extremely hard. If you must over-index on one, over-index on experience.

### SC-04#18 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: sales-process | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:30
claim: Fix result problems by setting expectations in the sale, matching the customer's goals and agreeing a workflow; fix experience problems by making it easier, faster and more pleasant.
detail: Results:
1. Set better expectations during the sale: how you will communicate, how often, your response time, your hours.
2. Match their goals: what will be accomplished.
3. Agree a workflow: communication cadence, milestones and objectives along the way.
Experience: apply the value-equation levers — easier, faster, more pleasant.

### SC-04#19 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:01
claim: At stage 1, fill skill and time gaps with freelancers (or people you know) rather than employees, because your needs change fast and you are unlikely to hire the right person yet.
detail: Example: you need your first website and cannot build it. Platforms such as Upwork, Fiverr and 99designs get specialists cheaply, for a few hundred dollars. They unblock small tasks without the commitment and fixed cost of a full-time hire, which minimizes risk. Freelancers are especially right now because your needs will change quickly.
numbers: A few hundred dollars per freelance task.

claim: In one presenter's first business, a $5,000 hard-coded website with PayPal checkout, built by a friend-of-a-friend freelancer, paid for itself in about two months.
detail: This was before website builders existed. The presenter did not know how to take payments, so the freelancer integrated a newly opened PayPal account into the checkout. That was the first way to collect money. The next month brought $4,000, and the month after another $4,000. You would not need to spend $5,000 today, and probably did not then, but it worked.

### SC-04#21 | FRAMEWORK | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:25
claim: A basic vendor agreement should settle: who is involved, who does what, where, by when, at what cost, for how long, who judges the result, and what follows if it falls short.
detail: Beginners tend to assume vendors can guess what they want. The agreement isn't meant for suing anyone but for clarifying, before work begins, your duties, theirs and the consequence of failure. Without a formal contract, at the very least write a document covering these points and have both sides agree to it first.

### SC-04#22 | EXAMPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:59
claim: A friend hired a videographer to deliver a set number of videos a month with no agreement; when a month passed with no videos, she had no recourse.
detail: She asked Leila what happens now. Leila asked where the agreement was, and there was none. They had never discussed what happens if something is late or goes wrong. That conversation is exactly what the agreement forces at the start.

### SC-04#23 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:59
claim: Beginners often skip vendor agreements because they do not know what they want and avoid confrontation; you have to decide what you want and say what happens if they do not deliver.
detail: Two failure patterns:
1. Not knowing what you want, and hoping the more expert vendor will tell you. You avoid deciding because you do not want to be accountable, but it is your money. Waiting for someone to create a "unicorn" sets you both up to fail.
2. If the vendor has no contract of their own, they are also a beginner, the blind leading the blind. Often it is a tech-savvy friend who is cheap. Both of you avoid saying "if you don't deliver on time, I won't pay", and the friendship is sacrificed. Saying it plainly is simply how business works.

### SC-04#24 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: sales-process | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:39
claim: Set up at least two ways to get paid, cash and card, and you graduate stage 1 the moment a real payment lands in the business bank account.
detail: Before this, free customers paid you in reviews and feedback. Now:
1. Accept cash.
2. Accept credit cards, the dominant way people pay, through a card processor, an online checkout, or both. Options: your bank, Stripe, or platforms with built-in processing (Skool, Shopify, Amazon). Bank and Stripe take an extra setup step; the platforms already have processors integrated.
3. Test it: run a payment and confirm the deposit hits the business account. Once a dollar has gone through the pipeline, it works.
