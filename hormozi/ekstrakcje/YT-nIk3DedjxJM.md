### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Choose what your business is about from the Three Ps: pain you overcame, your profession, or your passion.
detail: Pain: a problem you solved for yourself (example: a mother of nine with a lunch system). Profession: a skill from your current or past job that others in that job would pay to learn (accounting, HR, video editing), taught or sold as consulting. Passion: topics you read and watch for fun. A business needs only one very specific problem solved.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#02 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:01:00
claim: A hospital dietitian turned a narrow billing skill into a business teaching other dietitians how to bill insurance.
detail: She worked very long weeks and could see private clients only one day a week, so she mastered a complex billing process quickly, then taught it to peers. A very narrow problem is enough to build on.
numbers: ~5,800 Instagram followers; almost $1M a year take-home (as stated).

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:13
claim: You don't need to pick the perfect idea first; pick your "best bad idea" and improve it through customer feedback.
detail: Picking is the first step because it starts the feedback loop; with no customer contact you can't choose right anyway. Keep making the bad idea less wrong until it is good, and nobody will know it wasn't chosen correctly on the first shot.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:04:06
claim: Pick the "who" from three sources: people like you, people you have helped before, or an underserved group.
detail: If forced to choose he recommends people like you, since you already know their pains, goals and demographic and don't need much research. In his opinion this tends to produce "missionaries" who care about the customer, and he thinks that keeps you in for the long haul, unlike people who start purely from analysing markets and trends. Examples: Spanx (founder's own underwear problem) and his own first paying client.
### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:41
claim: His first dollar came from an informal one-and-a-half-hour food chat, paid without a price being named.
detail: A woman at his gym asked him to help with her food; he reviewed her groceries and gave recipes to raise protein. She wrote a $100 check unprompted. The lesson he drew: people will pay for help with a problem you have already solved.
numbers: $100 check after ~1.5 hours.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#06 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:18
claim: Narrow your avatar until at least three of these five frames are specified: age, gender, profession, problem, interest.
detail: Test: picture a room full of that type of person; if you would be excited to go in and help them, it is narrow enough. Example: 35-year-old male accountants bored at their jobs; or 45-year-old women struggling to advance at work. He counters the fear that narrow means fewer buyers: narrower lets you charge more and removes competitors.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#07 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:09:59
claim: A time-management product rises in price as the niche narrows.
detail: Broad time management as a PDF about $19; for salespeople about $199; for outbound salespeople more specific still (the quoted figure, $19.99, is probably misheard, unclear in audio); a full outbound time-management system for garden and power tools sellers could be $10,000, because a buyer in that niche earns the cost back from a couple of sales and has almost no competitors.
numbers: $19 broad; $199 for salespeople; up to $10,000 for a niche system (speaker's illustration).

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#08 | BENCHMARK | topic: offers | also: verticals | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:12:22
claim: Describe the upside in the four value-equation terms stated positively: easier, guaranteed, faster, and exactly what they want.
detail: B2B example from Gym Launch: the average gym owner takes home about $36,000 a year, so a result of $30,000 in the first 30 days is framed against that. A guarantee line is where you state stats and what you have done; for speed, give a timeline.
numbers: Gym Launch average gym: $30,000 in first 30 days (as stated); average owner take-home $36,000 per year.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:16
claim: Every motivator is either removing something bad or adding something good, and specific pain wins belief.
detail: Generic claims such as "lose weight fast" no longer work because buyers have learned not to trust them. A narrow, concrete pain (example: thighs chafing in the sun) passes the buyer's ad blindness because it feels like you know them. If you are the customer, you can describe it back from your own experience.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#10 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Perceived Hidden Costs | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:49
claim: Write a "bad stuff" list as the inverse of the upside: risks avoided, delay avoided, and effort they no longer carry.
detail: For effort, list two things: what they normally must give up that they no longer have to (keep their usual dinners out with friends), and what they would normally have to start doing that they hate (waking early, working out). What makes things hard is doing disliked things and giving up liked things. Risk and speed parts: state proof for the guarantee and a timeline for the wait.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#11 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: offers | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:23
claim: When you aren't the customer, interview prospects as if on a sales call to fill both "how" lists.
detail: Questions in order: what problems they struggle with now; how long; what makes it painful; what it feels like when it goes wrong; what the ideal experience looks like; what they have had to stop doing that they liked; what they have had to start doing that they hate.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#12 | SCRIPT | topic: offers | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:03
claim: A one-line offer statement: "I help [narrow who] get [good outcome] without [bad stuff], through [unique mechanism]".
detail: The mechanism part is optional for beginners without a process yet. Examples: new mothers back into old jeans without giving up family time; business authors onto a major bestseller list without looking cringe. It needn't be perfect: if the reader thinks "that's me", it works.

### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#13 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: content-brand | fw: MAGIC Naming Formula | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:00
claim: A unique mechanism is a named checklist or sequence of steps that wraps your method and makes prospects feel it is the missing piece.
detail: Choose either a set of things to do in any order or a sequence, then give it a proprietary name; it differentiates you when several people serve the same niche. Pair it with promise, proof, plan (promise the result, proof of past results, plan of steps). Examples: muscle confusion in a fitness programme (sold hundreds of millions despite critics), the points system of a weight programme. Naming: he personally likes rhyme or alliteration when he can do it.
### YT-nIk3DedjxJM#14 | BENCHMARK | topic: promotions-launches | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:00
claim: He reports about one in two people who start a paid community in the School Games make their first dollar.
