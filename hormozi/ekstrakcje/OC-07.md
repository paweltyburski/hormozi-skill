### OC-07#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: objections-closing | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:45
claim: The same contents are perceived as more valuable when presented as one core item plus free bonuses than as a single bundle at the same price — which is why bonuses get people to say yes today.
detail: Infomercial logic: "one knife for $19 plus 38 more free" beats "38 knives for $19". Every offer sets a price anchor and a "what you get" anchor; bonuses push the second far beyond the price. Their main job is a decision today.

### OC-07#02 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: sales-process, objections-closing | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:33
claim: Present the core offer and its price first, then stack bonus after bonus to stretch the prospect's sense of value far past the anchor they were already justifying.
detail: First match their problems to your solution, then state core offer and price (anchor; prospect is justifying it), then stack bonuses so perceived value keeps expanding. Credit: Jason Fladlien's webinar training.

### OC-07#03 | TACTIC | topic: objections-closing | also: offers, sales-team | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:33
claim: Instead of discounting to get a decision today, offer one-time bonuses for acting now, adding more each time you re-ask for the sale.
detail: "If you act today I'll give you this"; if they hesitate, "this and this". Keep asking and concede bonuses, not price, choosing ones that address the objections raised. Define the set your reps may give.

### OC-07#04 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: product-delivery | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:35
claim: Take the full solution list and split it into a core offer plus separately named bonuses — the same deliverables then feel like five things instead of one.
detail: Present "the core thing" and "these additional things" instead of one service. People struggle to value intangibles but can count items. Some pieces can be delivered later as bonuses when needed.

### OC-07#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Price to Value Discrepancy | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:43
claim: The total value of the bonuses should exceed the value of the core offer, which both widens the price-value gap and makes the core seem even more valuable.
detail: Each bonus widens the discrepancy. People assume bonuses are worth less than the main item, so bonuses clearly exceeding the core imply the core is worth even more.

### OC-07#06 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: promotions-launches | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:31
claim: Put scarcity or urgency on the bonuses themselves to supercharge them — scarcity limits the number available, urgency limits the time.
detail: Scarcity: "only members get bonuses one, two and three, never sold elsewhere"; or "three tickets left to my $5,000 virtual event, free with this $1,000 program". Urgency: "buy today and I add this $1,000 bonus to reward action takers". Scarcity is limited units; urgency is limited time.

### OC-07#07 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: sales-process, content-brand | fw: Bonus Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:29
claim: Hormozi's checklist for presenting bonuses: always offer them, name them with a benefit, explain how each relates to the prospect's problem, prove it, paint the after-picture, attach a justified price, favour tools over more work, address a specific obstacle, and solve the next need.
detail: 1. Always offer bonuses. 2. Name each with the benefit in the title (e.g. "how to grocery shop quickly so you spend less"). 3. Say what it is and how it ties to their problem in value-equation terms: faster, easier, less effort. 4. Give proof: a statistic, past client or experience; sales teams must know the stats per bonus. 5. Paint the picture of them having decided and getting the result. 6. Always ascribe and justify a price. 7. Prefer tools, templates, checklists over more training. 8. Each addresses a specific obstacle to success and disproves it. 9. Bonuses can solve what they need next. One of the big mistakes he sees: tossing bonuses in without selling why they are valuable. Be deliberate but not obsessive; the first version will be wrong.

### OC-07#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: - | fw: Bonus Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:26
claim: Tools and shortcuts beat extra training: "100 hours of recordings" sounds like work, while "$50,000 of contract templates you won't have to pay for" sounds like an instant win.
detail: Bonuses with "sizzle" are easy to grasp, deliver at once and sound like no work. More content hours raise perceived effort; ready-made tools cut it.

### OC-07#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: retention-ltv | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:26
claim: A bonus that solves the problem customers will face after they succeed implies the core offer works, which raises perceived likelihood of success.
detail: Preparing them for the next stage signals confidence they will reach it, so the core must work, raising perceived likelihood.

### OC-07#10 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: lead-getters, money-models | fw: Four Levels of Bonus Mastery | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:30
claim: Bundle other businesses' products and services into your offer as bonuses — partners will give them free for the introduction to your customers, and often pay you as well.
detail: A chiropractic clinic selling a $1,000 back-relief program adds free massages at three massage businesses, discounts on orthotics and bands, a low-inflammation meal-prep partner and supplements via a discount code. The savings can exceed the program price: 20 therapists each giving one $60 massage is $1,200 of value, and partners would also pay the clinic for referrals.
numbers: $1,000 program; 20 free massages × $60 = $1,200 of value.
applies_to: Any business whose customers buy adjacent products; example is a local health clinic.

### OC-07#11 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: lead-getters, money-models | fw: Four Levels of Bonus Mastery | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:34
claim: There are four levels to using other people's products as bonuses: give them away; give them with pre-negotiated discounts or free units; also get paid for it; and also get exclusive features or licences.
detail: Level 1: give partner products away (fine to start). Level 2: pre-negotiated discounts or free units available nowhere else. Level 3: discounts plus affiliate commission to you. Level 4: pre-negotiated rates plus exclusive features or licences plus commissions. Treat as checkboxes; he usually gets two or three per partner.

### OC-07#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: money-models, unit-economics | fw: Four Levels of Bonus Mastery | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:32
claim: When negotiating with partners, your leverage is the buying power of all current and future clients — and the partner fees you earn are pure profit.
detail: Frame it: "I sign up 20 clients a month and could send all 20 to you; what is that worth?" Partners would rather pay you than Facebook for the same customers. Pure profit because the partner bears fulfilment. A clinic earning $1,000 per package might earn another $1,000 per client from partners, doubling revenue per customer.
numbers: 20 clients/month referred; $1,000 program + ~$1,000 partner revenue per client.

### OC-07#13 | CASE | topic: offers | also: verticals, money-models | fw: Four Levels of Bonus Mastery | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:07
claim: Hormozi's gym-coaching business bundled five partner products — landing-page, fulfilment, texting and DM software plus pre-negotiated financing — each with commissions and various exclusive features or discounts.
detail: 1. Landing-page software with exclusive templates, commissions, no discount. 2. Fulfilment software preloaded with communications and meal plans, auto-sent to every new gym customer; exclusive, commissions, no discount. 3. Texting software with discount and commission, no custom features (he prefers preloaded). 4. Social-DM software with client-only features and commissions; clients save probably $1,000+/month. 5. Pre-negotiated financing: he took client businesses' combined revenue to banks, set packages, prices and terms, and negotiated rates and approvals, with a commission costing clients nothing. These introductions earned millions in pure profit.
numbers: Clients save ~$1,000+/month on DM software; commissions totalled millions of dollars.
applies_to: B2B coaching/licensing businesses whose clients need tools and services.

### OC-07#14 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: product-delivery | fw: Four Levels of Bonus Mastery | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:40
claim: Using partners' products instead of building them yourself keeps you flexible — you can always switch to the best option and use the threat of leaving as leverage.
detail: Not beholden to a vendor: move clients to better software, or tell the vendor to fix features or everyone leaves, without build and maintenance costs.

### OC-07#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: lead-getters, retention-ltv | fw: Four Levels of Bonus Mastery | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:40
claim: Customers are likely to look at buying related products because they bought from you, and often ask you what to buy — so pre-negotiate exclusive deals: they get a discount, you get paid.
detail: A gym joiner buys apparel, gloves, supplements, apps, meal prep and asks the trainer what to get. Instead of sending that money away free, negotiate exclusive deals: discount for them, payment for you, higher perceived value.
