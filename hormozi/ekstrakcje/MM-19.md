### MM-19#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Menu Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: The menu upsell runs in four steps: remove options they don't need, prescribe what they do need, let them choose between A and B, and offer to charge the card already on file.
detail: Hormozi's favourite upsell, developed over years of supplement consultations; he learned each step separately and they combine "like Lego". (1) Unsell — strike out options that don't suit them so your recommendations gain credibility. (2) Prescribe — state precisely what they need and how to use it, as though already purchased. (3) A/B — ask which of two purchase options they like better instead of yes/no. (4) Card on file — suggest charging the card they used before. Best with several offers and in businesses with many transactions; unselling low-margin items steers buyers toward higher-margin ones.

### MM-19#02 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process, verticals | fw: A/B Close | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:48
claim: After going 0-for-19 pitching supplement science, a worn-out Hormozi simply asked a customer which flavours she wanted and charged her card on file — and then closed everyone else that day.
detail: Inspired by a friend making about $80K/month on supplements, he bought $5,000 of inventory and got only pity sales, pitching hypertrophy and creatine to a mostly female clientele who didn't care. He batched nutrition consults into one day and had failed 19 in a row. Exhausted, he asked the next client: "Vanilla or chocolate protein?" — "Which do you like?" — "Chocolate." Same for pre-workout flavour. Having members' card details at hand (consults also fixed failed payments), he asked, "Use the card ending in 4696?" — "Sure." No product pitch at all. He closed the remaining ~12 consults that day. Lessons: the A/B assumed close and the card-on-file close.
numbers: $5,000 initial inventory; friend at ~$80K/month; ~70% female clientele; 0 for 19 → closed ~12 in a row.

### MM-19#03 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Prescription Close | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:50
claim: Writing one talkative client a detailed regimen led the next client to ask for "what you did for her" — and buying everything with no pitch at all revealed the prescription close.
detail: By then he ran 48 consults a day, two people every 30 minutes. One client kept asking "what if I'm travelling / morning or night?", so he wrote on her meal plan exactly what to take, how many and when. The next client, waiting, asked for "what you did for her"; running late, he skipped the pitch — "two of these, one of these…" — then "card on file?" She bought everything. Lesson: detailed, personalised post-purchase instructions assume the purchase and beat vague ones; saying no afterwards feels like wasting the time spent. Strongly recommended, never required.
numbers: $5,000–10,000/day in supplement sales; 48 consults/day (4 people per hour, in pairs).

### MM-19#04 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Unsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:25
claim: Running out of stock and pointing a customer to buy some items elsewhere made her buy everything else effortlessly — so Hormozi started keeping products specifically to cross off.
detail: Launching gyms on the road, he sold out of key items. A friend of an earlier client wanted exactly what her friend got; he told her where to buy the missing items down the street ("not as good, but it'll get you through"), then prescribed the rest and asked for the card on file — it went "like water". Crossing out what she didn't need built goodwill to sell what she did. He then stocked items his mostly female clientele wouldn't need, purely to unsell: "You're not trying to gain weight, are you?" (weight gainer) and "not after chest hair or a deeper voice?" (testosterone booster) — two or three cross-outs per menu. Give away low-margin items: skip $3 profit on fish oil to close ~100% on a $40-margin fat burner.
numbers: Fish oil ~$3 margin vs fat burner ~$40 margin.

### MM-19#05 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Unsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:04
claim: Unselling: start by telling customers what they don't need, so they trust you when you tell them what they do need.
detail: Instead of asking whether they want to buy, explain which options don't fit them, as a way to build excitement and credibility for what does. Unsells vary by customer need; when a few options work best, cross out the rest. Lower-margin items are good unsell candidates because they steer purchases toward higher-margin products.

### MM-19#06 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Prescription Close | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:04
claim: Prescribing: explain how the product fits with what they already bought and exactly how they will use it, as if the purchase has already happened.
detail: Prescription upsells work best when offering a choice is inconvenient or only one product solves the problem (e.g., a fat burner with no flavours — just "this many at this time"). Two components: (1) explain how it integrates with the offers they already bought (their weight-loss service, workouts, etc.); (2) personalise in detail how to maximise its value for them. You remove the "buy or not" option, which lowers the chance they don't buy.

### MM-19#07 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: sales-process, objections-closing | fw: A/B Close | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:38
claim: Ask which of two buying options they prefer instead of whether they want to buy — then nudge them toward the one you want.
detail: Offer a way not to buy and some won't; offer two buying options and either is a sale. Anything can be A/B: Monday or Tuesday, coach John or Amy, morning or evening, paper or plastic, DM or phone support. Then nudge, based on their needs: "I like strawberry", "a lot of people like Amy", "big dogs usually like beef", "people come back for the crème brûlée". The illusion of choice, then the friction of choosing removed; nudge toward the highest-margin option, overstocked items, or simply what fits them (00:13:26).

### MM-19#08 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Card on File Close | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:38
claim: Close by asking "Do you want to use the card on file?" — it removes the hidden second decision of physically paying.
detail: Instead of asking whether they want to pay, refer to how they already paid before. Saying yes is one decision; reaching for the wallet, choosing a card, looking at it and being reminded of past bad purchases is another. The card-on-file question skips all of that. It also saves sales from people in a rush who would otherwise say "I'll pick it up later" and never return — it is the fastest close.
quote: Want to use the card on file?

### MM-19#11 | SCRIPT | topic: money-models | also: verticals, sales-process | fw: Menu Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:39
claim: Menu upsell for a digital product: say they don't need all eight courses, supply free material for two of their problems, prescribe a single course for the third, then offer A/B choices on support and start date.
detail: Unsell: "You don't need all eight courses yet — you only need to fix X, Y and Z. I'll send free material covering X and Y." Prescribe: "For Z you need one course, and you should work through it in this particular way — can you give it an hour today? That heads off other Z problems later." A/B: "Direct message or phone support?" and "Start today or Monday?" Pay: "Use the card on file?" Any product follows the same four steps.

### MM-19#12 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: product-delivery | fw: Menu Upsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:16
claim: When stock is low, nudge toward what you have; when sold out or at capacity, still take payment and set a clear later delivery or start date — then beat it.
detail: Nudge toward products you have plenty of (25 bottles vs 3). If completely sold out, take the order and ship later: people don't need to leave with it, they need accurate expectations. Services at capacity (7 new clients a week, already 8 sold): take the deposit and start them in 14 days rather than lose the sale.
numbers: Promise 7 days; 4 = happy, 10 = angry; at-capacity service start delayed 14 days.

### MM-19#13 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: money-models | fw: Unsell | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:13
claim: Script unselling for staff as "homie hookups by design" — employees love giving them, and customers trust them more than the owner's.
detail: When training staff he found employees love unselling: giving a customer a "hookup" gives them authority, a feeling of power and rapport. So design it: specify the three items they can cross out and the two products they can tell customers to buy down the street. It works even better from an employee than from the owner, because customers know employees often don't put the business first — "don't tell the boss, but you do need these ones" lands as insider advice, though the whole thing is scripted.

### MM-19#14 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Decoy Pricing | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:28
claim: The Economist's digital-only and digital+print offers cannibalised print until it added a print-only decoy at the same price as the bundle, making the bundle the obvious choice.
detail: Print was more profitable relative to price than digital (people perceive digital as less valuable). The Economist offered $59/year digital and $125/year digital + print, expecting most to take the bundle; instead almost everyone took digital only and print sales collapsed, so they made far less. Fix: add a print-only option at $125/year — the best offer cut in half at the same price. Now for $125 the bundle effectively includes digital for free, so it feels like the deal, and many more people bought digital + print. (Figures are for relative-price illustration.)
numbers: Digital $59/yr; print-only decoy $125/yr; digital + print $125/yr.

### MM-19#15 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Decoy Pricing | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:09
claim: If you sell two things and want people to buy both, present three options — A, B, and A+B — and price the bundle the same as the more expensive single option.
detail: Option C (A and B together) priced equal to the pricier of A or B makes the bundle the easy choice; before, people pick one item, after, more take both. Price all three so margins are preserved.
