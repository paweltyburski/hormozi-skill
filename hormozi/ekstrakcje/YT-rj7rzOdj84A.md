### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: The vast majority of businesses are stuck because they work on the wrong thing; the problem that decides growth is usually not the one the owner thinks it is or likes doing.
detail: Owners stall because they keep working on what got them from zero to a million, when the next stage needs a harder skill. The real business is whichever function carries the hardest problem (sales, talent, product, distribution). Before entering a market, ask people already in it what is the hardest part of their business.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#03 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:01:24
claim: In supplements he thought he was in the product business, but the real business was brand, media and distribution.
detail: He hired a top biochemist to make the most effective formula and found that mattered little. For capsules without flavour the game is brand and distribution. For a flavoured product that people buy for taste and habit, strong media and traffic drive sales, since customers cannot feel the ingredients working.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#04 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:03:11
claim: His software company sold easily but the outsourced product could not deliver, so the real business was product.
detail: Software automates work so it sells easily if the product-market fit is real. He hired an outsourced development shop; sales ran far ahead of what the product could do. Dev shops raise fees once they see your revenue, and with no technical person in-house he could not check their work. He sold to a strategic buyer who could rebuild the code, in an all-stock deal.
numbers: Run rate reached $1.7M per month within six months (unclear whether revenue or bookings in audio).

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#05 | CASE | topic: hiring-talent | also: verticals | fw: Internal Core Four (Recruiting Mirror) | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:37
claim: A gym owner who started an Airbnb cleaning business found his bottleneck was cleaner recruiting, not customer acquisition.
detail: He began cleaning his own rentals, then sold cleaning to other hosts. When asked for LTV and CAC, he said LTV was very high and CAC tiny, but finding cleaners who show up, do good work, don't steal and communicate was the problem. Gyms attract fitness enthusiasts as trainers; cleaning has no equivalent. Hormozi had him mirror his customer funnel for talent: lead generation for applicants, interviews instead of sales calls, onboarding and training of staff.
numbers: CAC about $6; revenue rose from about $30,000-$40,000 a month to over $150,000 a month in 12 months.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:35
claim: Big professional-service firms are recruiting and training machines; guru-led coaching businesses cannot scale because one talented person is diluted by weaker staff.
detail: The firms profit from the spread between what they pay people and what they charge for their expertise, so they keep raising the quality of people and train them faster than rivals. Risk: trained stars can leave with clients, which is why such firms become partnerships and give a path to ownership so staying beats leaving.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#07 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:27
claim: In his investing, a handful of deals produce nearly all returns, and growing a large company costs about the same work as building a small one.
detail: Investing turned out to be about saying no faster to more deals, because one outlier is enough. Growing a company already near $100M revenue to $150M was similar or easier work than going from 0 to $1M or $1M to $10M, and larger profit earns a higher multiple, a scale premium rather than a volume discount.
numbers: 22 deals in about 2.5 years; 4 of them gave ~90% of returns; $100M to $150M revenue = +$50M.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:15
claim: Entrepreneurs plateau by restarting new ventures where they can again use the skill they already have, instead of facing the next problem.
detail: Using a game analogy: they beat level-one and level-two bosses repeatedly, then quit at the level-three boss that needs a different skill. The shiny-object "woman in the red dress" promises the same skills will work better elsewhere, but every opportunity hides its own problems. Real progress comes from naming and confronting the hairy problem, which can feel unpleasant (the product is weak, you must learn coding or hire technical talent).

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:18
claim: Price a hard problem by the enterprise value on the other side of it.
detail: He pictures a concrete wall and a sledgehammer; not knowing the thickness is the difficulty. He told a portfolio CEO that building a new product line would add about $200M of enterprise value and might be "$10M hard", which is a good trade. Ask what the solved problem is worth before deciding whether to push.
numbers: ~$10M of effort for ~$200M of enterprise value (example).

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:38
claim: In his opinion, the good kind of hard is when your first-principles assumptions are believed true but results don't come immediately; then you work through it step by step.
detail: His example is TikTok ads: he believes accountants are on TikTok and can be reached profitably. If ROI doesn't come immediately, he calls that the good kind of hard and goes back to the start: enough clicks, opt-ins, right people, then maybe change messaging, targeting or lead magnet. He sees money lost on the ads as an investment in enterprise value via another acquisition channel.
### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#11 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:22
claim: A second customer-acquisition channel raises enterprise value both through more profit and lower buyer risk, even if it loses money at first.
detail: Example: a business earns $2M EBIT at a 5x multiple, so $10M value; a diversified acquisition system could add $10M at least, realistically up to $15M. Framed as "invest $100,000 and six months for a $10M return", testing a channel such as TikTok ads is obviously worth it; people instead conclude "ads don't work for us".
numbers: EBIT $2M x 5 = $10M; added value $10-15M; test cost ~$100,000 over ~6 months.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#12 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:23:48
claim: A canned premium-cocktail founder with thin sell-through was told to compress distribution, then try transport, or shut down.
detail: She had a distillery, about 1,000 stores stocked on fronted inventory, Amazon, a Shopify store and wholesale, zero profit, no staff and no repeat-purchase data; the product was ready-to-drink so direct-to-consumer shipping was too costly. A big brand route would need money and media skill she lacked. Hormozi gave two paths: regional store-by-store sampling for 10-15 years, or one last attempt selling to transport businesses (airlines, trains, cruise lines).
numbers: Revenue about $1.5M a year across ~1,000 stores; 5% ABV; UK market.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#13 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:26:27
claim: For packaged consumer goods, ask what percentage of ideal-customer first-time buyers purchase again.
detail: He calls repeat-purchase rate a very important metric in consumer packaged goods, measured on the ideal avatar only, since a wrong audience (e.g. kids' chocolate sold to old people) would probably not rebuy anyway. When the caller lacked this metric, he said it would be really tough, and he stressed the product must be good enough that people keep buying it.
### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#14 | EXAMPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:29:19
claim: A celebrity-backed product with big distribution still faded when it was not good enough to rebuy.
detail: The founder of a caffeinated strip product launched with a famous athlete and strong retail connections. Sales rose for four straight weeks and then fell in weeks five to seven because repeat demand was missing. Great brand and money speed a failure; a good product keeps growing slowly on repeat buys.
numbers: Sales up weeks 1-4; down weeks 5-7.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#15 | TACTIC | topic: verticals | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:27:36
claim: With little money, sell hand to hand in a small region and pay shop staff commission to push the product.
detail: Narrow from many stores to a region, stand in each store sampling and telling the story a few times a week, then give store sellers commission on sales so they move the product. It works only if the product is good: sellers won't spend relationships on something customers won't rebuy.

### YT-rj7rzOdj84A#19 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:35:46
claim: When someone says a business "isn't scalable", rephrase it as "I don't know how to scale it" and check the physics.
detail: The founder earned most of her money from property development on a few hours a week yet called it unscalable. Hormozi pointed out she already had the loans, markets, designers and contacts; what she lacked was people management and training. Reframing moves the issue from circumstances to a controllable skill problem. Ask why, physically, it cannot grow.
