### MM-07#01 | DEFINITION | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: A money model is a series of offers; a good one is sequenced to minimize CAC and maximize gross profit collected in the first 30 days.
detail: An offer is one unit (the subject of $100M Offers); a money model deliberately orders several of them. The three targets map to the three acquisition numbers: how cheaply you get customers, how much they pay, how fast they pay.

### MM-07#02 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:56
claim: A rental-car counter turned Hormozi's $19-a-day online booking into a $400 three-day rental through a quick sequence of offers he barely noticed.
detail: Sequence: (1) "We don't have the car you reserved — OK with a red Tundra?" — an upgrade from a Yaris that took the rate from $19 to $69/day and solved a big-guy-in-a-small-car problem. (2) Late return so you need not worry about fees or timing. (3) "Upgrade your insurance?" — declined, then "just the minimum then?" — accepted. (4) Prepay fuel at $3.75/gallon vs ~$3.50 locally, which sounded like $2.50 extra but meant buying a full tank (~$93) up front. (5) Implicit offers: damage charges and late-return fees. He was not annoyed, just impressed by a well-engineered model.
numbers: $19/day booked → $69/day upgrade; ~$400 for 3 days; fuel prepay ~$93.

### MM-07#03 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:14
claim: You can invent rules and the fees attached to them — "late" checkout exists only because a company chose an arbitrary return time.
detail: Someone in rental cars once decided that returning an hour after a made-up time costs extra; the rest of the industry copied it and now it feels natural. As the game maker of your business, you set its rules and the fees that come with them.

### MM-07#04 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: pricing, objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:27
claim: Phrase upgrades so the base purchase is implied, and name your lowest package "minimum" so most people take it as the default.
detail: "Do you want to upgrade your insurance?" assumes you already have insurance. After a no, "Just the minimum then?" is the real sale, because "minimum" feels mandatory although it is optional (many credit cards such as Amex already cover rentals, so it is nearly pure profit). Calling it "minimum" instead of "economy" lets you downsell to it and, he estimates, perhaps 90% take it.
numbers: ~90% take a package named "minimum" (his estimate).

### MM-07#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:41
claim: Prepaid fuel is a fee to avoid a fee: a steep refuelling penalty makes buying a full tank up front look smart, and unused fuel is extra profit.
detail: The company could simply refuel and charge you; instead it penalizes returning with less than full ($5–10/gallon) and sells a prepaid tank at a slight premium. Hormozi stresses making sure customers understand what they are buying. He defends the model: rental firms run thin margins with costs like crashes, and businesses need profit to scale and serve people.
numbers: Penalty $5–10/gallon vs prepay $3.75 vs market $3.50.

### MM-07#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:04
claim: Do not withhold extra offers from 19 reasonable customers because one in 20 might be upset.
detail: Among the general public, a small share will be unhappy no matter what. The alternative cost — no profit or no business — is far worse than an occasional complaint.
numbers: ~1 in 20 customers may complain.

### MM-07#07 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:50
claim: Study the money models of long-established big brands and transplant them across industries.
detail: A company that has survived 30 years in an industry has figured out its monetization. Money models run on psychology more than industry specifics, so a rental-car tactic can work in insurance, teeth whitening or plumbing.

### MM-07#08 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: Three Levels of Advertising | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:36
claim: Gym Launch went from $1,000 in the bank (December 2016) to $17M profit in 20 months on a level-three money model.
detail: Actual metrics: 30-day gross profit $6,000 per client against $500 CAC (12:1 in the first month), and 12-month gross profit $48,000 (96:1). Growth came from the money model plus extreme client-financed acquisition.
numbers: CAC $500; 30-day GP $6K (12:1); 12-month GP $48K (96:1); $17M profit in 20 months.

### MM-07#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:23
claim: With a proper money model a marketing budget is pointless — spend as much as you can while staying within your KPIs.
detail: If a machine returned $48,000 for every $500 you put in, you would not cap yourself at $5,000 a month; you would feed it everything. A well-designed money model out-earns any budget.

### MM-07#10 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: verticals, sales-process | fw: Offer Stack | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:08
claim: The Gym Launch offer stack: free content, a free strategy call, a $16K premium intensive with weekly payments, a free supplement-selling program, and a halfway call meant to move clients to a higher continuity tier.
detail: (1) Free book, lead magnets and podcast bring people in. (2) Free strategy call to apply it — "leave your card at home"; good fits were invited to a second call. (3) $16,000 Fill Your Gym intensive on very attractive terms: $1,000 down, then $1,000/week — possible because gyms made more than $16K in their first month. (4) Free supplement-selling training, conditional on buying product. (5) Free feedback call at the halfway point, carrying the continuity offer.
numbers: $16K intensive; $1K down + $1K/week; 16-week program.

### MM-07#11 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: pricing, verticals | fw: Offer Stack | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:57
claim: Protect affiliates' pricing: keep your online price above the in-store price so their customers keep buying from them, not from you.
detail: As a gym owner Hormozi hated supplement brands undercutting him online after he had paid to acquire the customer. Prestige Labs set an online price above the in-store price and gave gyms back-door links so the gym stayed the good guy. Combined with no inventory and subscriptions, the supplement company did $1.7M in its first month.
numbers: $1.7M first-month revenue.

### MM-07#12 | CASE | topic: retention-ltv | also: money-models | fw: Free With Commitment (continuity) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:11
claim: At the week-8 feedback call of a 16-week program, clients were offered three years of extra value now, at their current price, if they committed to the higher continuity tier.
detail: "Say yes today and get everything you'd get over the next three years; keep paying what you pay now; after the remaining eight weeks your billing drops to a lower rate for a longer period." Clients paying $1,000/week got more value without paying more. The goal was to move them from the cheap built-in continuity to the premium continuity tier. This is a free-with-commitment continuity offer, covered later in the course.
numbers: $1,000/week during program; built-in continuity ~$500/month; target tier $3,200/month.

### MM-07#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: management-ops | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:46
claim: Simple scales: use as few offers as possible that produce the most gross profit in the least time.
detail: Gym Launch ran this simple stack for years; complexity is not the goal.
