### POD-0825#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: objections-closing | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:05:32
claim: Being believable persuades better than being persuasive, and the way to get there is to state your biases and damaging facts before the claim.
detail: Open a teaching, ad or call by naming what could make the listener doubt you (your bias, the cases that did not work), then give the good news. The admission is what makes the later positives credible. Hormozi calls this one of the most basic persuasion moves. Example: asked if his wife is a good CEO, he first says he is biased, then argues from his incentives.

### POD-0825#02 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:10:44
claim: A four-box of promise size against product quality ranks the combinations from worst to best.
detail: Worst: big promise, bad product. Next worst: big promise, good product. Second best: small promise, weak product. Best: small promise, good product. Big promise with a good product is bad because outcomes differ between customers and, as he puts it, you probably haven't cracked the code to human motivation, so some will fall short of a promise they were given.
### POD-0825#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:34
claim: Low stated expectations mean that nothing happening still meets expectations, which is the only durable form of under-promise and over-deliver.
detail: Most sellers say "under-promise, over-deliver" and then promise heavily in the next sentence. The test is behaviour: the advertised claim itself must shrink. Educators and community sellers cannot control what a customer's background, effort and motivation will produce, so they should say that up front.

### POD-0825#04 | DECISION_RULE | topic: objections-closing | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:43
claim: Promising less and disclosing more will probably sell to about the same number of people, but shifts the mix of buyers you convert.
detail: Heavy promises and guarantees mostly convert less experienced, lower-spending buyers; experienced business owners have sharp detectors for hype. Hormozi expects the count of buyers to probably stay about the same while the slice of the customer spectrum changes.
### POD-0825#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:31:51
claim: Truthful, modest selling brings better customers, fewer refunds, fewer chargebacks and better results because expectations are lower.
detail: Prospects already suspect inflated claims, so honesty raises trust and purchase likelihood. Customers who were not promised rescue do their own work. Hormozi says that one trial is enough to see that the same number buy, and that sellers then switch for good.

### POD-0825#06 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:48
claim: Make the truth as precise as possible, using a measured fact instead of a vague "some were satisfied".
detail: Truth has degrees: "some people were satisfied and some were not" is true but weaker than a counted statement such as 98% rating the product 4.5 or higher. Use the more precise statement, and add the plain disclaimer that results cannot be controlled, in ads, on sales calls and at the start of webinars.
numbers: Example figure used: 98% of buyers rating 4.5 or above (illustrative, not a real result).

### POD-0825#07 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: objections-closing | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:19
claim: Your own story is a claim too: you are testimonial number one and must disclose that your result is not typical.
detail: What you did to get your result differs from what a customer will go through, so treat your story like anyone else's. State that results vary in outbound messages, videos, paid ads, landing pages and about pages. Disclosure makes viewers believe the story more, not less.

### POD-0825#08 | FRAMEWORK | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:17:04
claim: In the United States an earnings claim needs four things: permission, disclosed context, substantiation and proof of typicality.
detail: 1. Permission from the person. 2. Disclosure of the circumstances and how the data was collected. 3. Substantiation: bank or processor statements from before and after your help, plus a causal link between them (a screenshot is not enough). 4. Evidence the result is typical, which means tracking every customer. Hormozi says earnings claims, the way listeners are likely making them, are probably not legal.
applies_to: Info, coaching and education sellers in the US; local law elsewhere differs.
### POD-0825#09 | SCRIPT | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:18:59
claim: The intention close answers an ROI question by addressing the question behind it, whether the buyer will be happy, and then uses subjective proof.
detail: Steps: say you will not talk ROI; name the underlying worry (will I be satisfied with this purchase); introduce a similar customer whose praise is purely subjective (no income or outcome numbers); remind the prospect that this person's experience is not typical and theirs will vary. Repeat with as many such stories as needed. Subjective claims like "best teacher" cannot be disproved.

### POD-0825#10 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:12
claim: A compliant call opens with name, company, topic and a recorded-line notice, then agenda, then an outcome disclaimer.
detail: Step 1: the first sentence names the caller, company and reason for the call and says the line is recorded (quality assurance wording is fine); anything said before that is a liability in the US. Step 2: ask permission to dive in and read out a three-point agenda. Step 3: before discussing the prospect's history, state that no specific result is promised because the buyer does the work, and get a "fair" agreement.

### POD-0825#11 | SCRIPT | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:24:17
claim: When a prospect restates your offer as a promised outcome, answer in three steps: agree it would be great, repeat that nothing is guaranteed, then list only inputs.
detail: The prospect says "so if I do this I will lose weight, right?" to get an implied promise. Reply that it would be fantastic, remind them results depend on their effort and background, then restate what you will do (mentor sessions, library, support for 12 weeks). Talk about inputs, never outputs.

### POD-0825#12 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:25:02
claim: Avoid false urgency, false scarcity and fake qualification frames.
detail: Many sellers claim to qualify but only look for a card and a pulse. A "why should I accept you" frame that is untrue is suspect even when you do sometimes reject people; "let's see if this is a fit" is fine.
### POD-0825#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: objections-closing | also: offers | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:25:59
claim: Persuasion has two sides, promise and pain, and pain is the compliant one: describe the prospect's struggle better than they can.
detail: A prospect who hears their exact issues described trusts that you can help, even when you promise nothing. Hormozi says advertisers fixate on the top half of the Value Equation (outcome, likelihood) and he now leans on the bottom half (delay, effort and sacrifice), which is the opposite of pain.

### POD-0825#14 | FRAMEWORK | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:29:18
claim: Layering the plus/minus four-box (which includes "selling against the don't") over What-Who-When gives Hormozi an endless variety of persuasive points.
detail: Plus-plus: more good (access, materials). Minus-minus: less bad (less overwhelm and confusion). Plus-minus: the decision may bring more bad (risk, delay). Minus-plus: if you don't move forward, you might not get the outcomes you wanted. Many sellers argue only the decision in front of the buyer and ignore the path not taken. What-Who-When adds who is affected (family, colleagues, rivals) and when (past, present, future).
### POD-0825#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:27:33
claim: A salesperson is a one-to-one motivational speaker, and motivation needs deprivation, created in two ways.
detail: Way one: show how far the thing they want is from where they are. Way two: show they are further back than they think, which is pain-based. The CLOSER pain cycle repeats four questions: what have you tried, how did it work, what did you like, what did you not like; then "what else". That raises the felt relevance of the pain.

### POD-0825#16 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:28:26
claim: Two pain-deepening questions: what has this problem stopped you doing, and what unwanted thing has happened because of it.
detail: Ask "what have you not been able to do because of this?" and "what has happened that you did not want to happen?" Add that you are not promising they will get to do it; you only want to understand.

### POD-0825#17 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: objections-closing | fw: Unconditional Guarantee | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:33:03
claim: Someone who asks you to guarantee something they control is asking you to do the work for them.
detail: A satisfaction guarantee (you will like the experience) is fine; an outcome guarantee on the customer's own effort is not. The same logic supports not promising results.

### POD-0825#18 | EXAMPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:33:56
claim: Big brands do not use outcome testimonials, and a restaurant owner fearing staff will leave over disclosed profits is solving the wrong problem.
detail: Blackstone does not advertise an investor turning $1M into $10M and still makes money. A restaurant owner wanted to hide his numbers from staff; Hormozi's answer: with ten locations they will know anyway, public companies must disclose and still hire, and staff who leave over your profit should leave. Find people glad you earn.
