### POD-0557#05 | CASE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:09:30
claim: The six-week challenge was an iteration of other people's ideas: money-back-if-goal-hit (testimonial play), shortened to six weeks, then templated into one-to-many bootcamp format and fed with Facebook ads.
detail: A rich entrepreneur told one gym owner he would not pay, but if he hit his goal he would get a refund and give a testimonial. That owner built an eight-week offer on it. Another owner cut it to six weeks around personal training. Hormozi saw that personal training capped how many could be sold per trainer, moved it to a bootcamp where headcount is unlimited, added a nutrition template and ads. The combination is what made it explode.
numbers: Price point tested: ~$500 (owner doubted people would pay); first ad was run for the owner before Facebook ads were known to work, sold the first 10-12 people.

### POD-0557#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-process | also: sales-team | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:00
claim: Selling skill comes from repetitions in live face-to-face conversations, and he outsold other reps simply by doing 20-25 consults a day for years.
detail: Setters filled his calendar three days ahead, so he stacked consult after consult. The learning is in tiny cues: pauses, phrasing, what order changes how the rest is heard. If you must think about your next sentence you do not know the material yet. His advice to beginners: take a retail or door-knocking job, because the employer effectively pays you to be trained.
numbers: 20-25 consults/day; ~4,000 closed sales (closes, not consults) visible in his gym CRM after four years.

### POD-0557#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:57
claim: Selling is leverage: you can build a strong business knowing one thing, getting others to do the other things, and it works when you show people how it is in their interest.
detail: Instead of learning every skill, persuade someone else to do it. Leadership and sales are both influence; sales is the more transactional version. Frisella adds that managers who explain how the work benefits the employee get compliance that owners who just give orders do not.

### POD-0557#09 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: money-models | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:30
claim: The launch-and-go model began when a stranger handed him a card for $5,000 "to see what he gets" and he offered to run a six-week gym launch for upfront sales while the owner kept the converting members.

### POD-0557#10 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:26:36
claim: A partner he trusted took half the pooled cash and wired it abroad, leaving Hormozi with a gym to run and forcing him to stop selling.
detail: After selling his five gyms, he partnered with an operator who would staff new gyms while he filled them. He signed a personal guarantee on the lease and put launch cash and sale proceeds into the joint account. The partner emptied it and accused him of skimming; he had been indicted for fraud two years earlier, which Hormozi knew and dismissed. Hormozi could not sell more without keeping the gym open, so payroll and rent drained his savings.
numbers: 376 members sold in six weeks (~$600 each); savings fell to ~$20,000.

### POD-0557#11 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:33:18
claim: A payment processor froze a launch payout during the busiest week of the year, leaving him with a national launch campaign and no way to take payments.
detail: Launch cash was being run through his local gym's merchant account, which the processor treated as a violation and held for six months. He owed a salesman commission and had ~$1,000 left when the campaign costs started. For a month he collected contracts and cards without a processor, then found a high-risk processor.
numbers: Campaign cost ~$3,300/day; owed ~$22,000 commission; 60-80 contracts/day; no processor Dec 26 to Jan 29.

### POD-0557#12 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:36:40
claim: A high-risk processor charged 8% processing plus a 10% reserve held about six months, with a $50,000 monthly cap.
detail: Of every sale, 8% went to the processor for risk and 10% sat in escrow, released on a rolling basis later. The cap was per month, so he ran $50,000 on Jan 29 and another $50,000 on Feb 1. Lesson he states: if you cannot process money, there is nothing you can do in the business, and back then (around 2013-14) processors had all the power, though once you are big they change their tune.
numbers: 8% fee + 10% six-month reserve = 18% of sales withheld/lost initially; $50,000/month limit.
### POD-0557#13 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:37:37

### POD-0557#14 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:39:43
claim: Pivot out of the crisis: his partner's online coaching (all margin) was white-labelled and sold with a two-day sales letter, then he sold the method itself to gym owners, who said yes to every price he named.
detail: Layla's online personal training already made $3,000-4,000/month at high margin; he wrote a sales letter and ran ads, and she closed about $1,000/day. Gym owners then asked to be shown what he had done. He quoted a high number hoping to be refused, got a yes, and quoted higher on each next call. Most material (sales training, nutrition) already existed from internal use; only the ad and landing page know-how had to be built.
numbers: Prices quoted: $6,000, then $8,000, then $10,000, each accepted; ~$60,000 cash collected in one day; ~$215,000 profit the next month, covering all refunds.
