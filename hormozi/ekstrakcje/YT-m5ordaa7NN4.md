claim: People who beat you are usually doing around 100x the work, not 2x, and we misjudge because we think in increments.

### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#05 | BENCHMARK | topic: management-ops | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:14
claim: For work you do over and over, he says you can have about a one-to-four prep ratio: one hour of prep per four hours of work.
detail: Applied to meetings: 15 minutes before a 60-minute meeting, 5 minutes before a 20-minute one, 2 hours in an 8-hour day. In the prep block ask who is in the meeting, the background, the agenda and the problem to solve; he says this makes you a driver in the meeting. With repetition the ratio can fall to about 1:10 (5 minutes for 60). Prep as close to the event as possible.
numbers: 1:4 start (15 min for 60 min); about 1:10 later (5 min for 60 min).
### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#06 | BENCHMARK | topic: management-ops | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:14
claim: For one-off performances he says prep should be much larger, with diminishing returns as the audience grows.
detail: One-time, never-done-before events need far more prep than routine ones: roughly 10-20 hours of prep per hour presented. His audience example: a thousand one-on-one meetings at about 10 minutes of prep each would be roughly 167 hours, though that may not make sense because of diminishing returns. Preparing means mastering the material first, then approximating the real setting (for example going live on social media to simulate an audience).
numbers: ~10-20 hours prep per hour presented; ~167 hours for 1,000 meetings at 10 minutes each (his rough illustration).

### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#11 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: strategy-positioning | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Leverage | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:12

### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:40
claim: Short, last-minute cramming is high return for one-off business events, and work expands to fill the time allowed.
detail: Using Parkinson's law in reverse, he argues work also shrinks to the time you give it. For something done once (client pitch, company presentation), studying the day before and the morning of gives the best immediate result; he distinguishes this from long-term retention. The risk is over-preparing the wrong thing.

### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#13 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: Proof Over Promise | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:44
claim: Spend five minutes before a call on the person's profile and personal details, so they notice you prepared within the first minute.
detail: Examples: check where the contact is based, their team, a hobby they tagged, a spouse's name, and greet others by role. Visible prep also saves a fact-finding phase and makes a hard sell unnecessary: a prospect who sees 10-20 hours of prep from a vendor says, in effect, send the invoice.
numbers: ~5 minutes for a 60-minute call.

### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#14 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:12
claim: For conversion problems, research from the outside as a prospect would, instead of asking the owner.
detail: When diagnosing why prospects are not converting, he looks at what a non-customer sees (site, funnel, ads) rather than interviewing the owner, because owners colour their explanations and the outside view is faster. He says this kind of prep can be more valuable than the call itself.

### YT-m5ordaa7NN4#15 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:04
claim: In customer success, review call notes and the sales team's notes for five minutes before a customer call, even if the call starts late.
detail: He believes starting five minutes late but prepared beats starting on time unprepared. Customers rate such calls much higher for little effort. He also estimates return on prep with a volume lens: ten minutes of prep across a thousand hour-long meetings is about 167 hours, which only makes sense where the returns justify it.
numbers: 10 min x 1,000 meetings = ~167 hours.
