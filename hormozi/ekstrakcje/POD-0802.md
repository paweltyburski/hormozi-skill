### POD-0802#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:48
claim: Knowledge businesses scale through three delivery models: fractionalise access (one-to-few), train other people to deliver, or put many experts on one customer.
detail: One-to-four, one-to-eight and so on scales fast but stops when churn grows, because the value per client shrinks. Training coaches in templated pieces is slower and limited by supply of capable people. Many-to-one (consulting, law and accounting firms) is also supply-constrained but built big firms through career paths. He prefers keeping the full value for a smaller group over a diluted version for more people.
applies_to: Coaching and consulting businesses

### POD-0802#02 | BENCHMARK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:48
claim: A coaching business with coaches as the delivery tends to stall somewhere around $20-30M a year.
detail: Hormozi says the two big problems of such a business are revenue retention and, depending on the model, finding people who deliver as well as the founder. Beyond that range it typically gets very difficult, and a billion-dollar annual sales business is very unlikely in this model.
numbers: Roughly $20-30M per year, typical difficulty point for coach-delivered models; a $1B/year business very unlikely.
### POD-0802#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: hiring-talent | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:30
claim: Teaching someone in seven days to deliver your service means either you taught only a fraction or the service is too simple to be worth much, which creates churn.
detail: Coaching programmes that hand a new person a client roster after a week usually transfer about 10% of the knowledge while charging the same price. Firms that endured instead built multi-year career paths, and bright, hardworking people who could do the job as well as the founder do not want to be coaches.

### POD-0802#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:02
claim: A business meant to outlive its founder has one thing to solve: revenue retention, and the way to do it is a core product or tool customers cannot drop.
detail: His hair example: stylists earn much more from selling hair than from cuts and colour, so the product becomes the core engine and they stay. A bolt-on product fails when customers earn more elsewhere: Gym Launch gyms made far more from services than from supplements, so they drifted back. Options are making the product the core business, or giving every client a software or hardware tool used regularly ("build the Kindle, get them on Prime").

### POD-0802#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:48
claim: Gym Launch is moving into a software platform (gymowners.com) to retain customers, not primarily to build enterprise value.
detail: Hormozi frames it as the only way he sees to turn that service business into recurring revenue. Enterprise value follows from retention, not the other way round.

### POD-0802#06 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:06:41
claim: A motorcycle YouTube channel earned most of its $3.7M from ad revenue and was leaving money on the table by not knowing who its advertisers were.
detail: Channel: 2.3M subscribers, about $3.7M top line, mostly YouTube ads, a few hundred thousand dollars from own products, sponsorships just starting. Hormozi's reasoning: advertisers pay about $3.5M a year and must earn roughly 10 to 20 times that from the channel's audience, and they would pay more directly because the audience trusts the creator. Options: own products, direct sponsorships, affiliate (less preferred, for proof early), and partnerships.
numbers: RPM $30 (per 1,000 long-form views); 10-15M long-form views per month; roughly 10-20x advertiser return implied.
applies_to: Media and creator businesses

### POD-0802#07 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: outreach | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:30
claim: Find out who already advertises on your channel and treat them as a warm leads list for direct deals.
detail: Post to the community asking viewers which ads they see on the channel, then compile the advertisers and approach them: "you already advertise on my channel, let's make this official." Direct sponsorships and selling ad space himself could roughly double revenue and drop to the bottom line. Look at the products sold and pick the best seller to partner with.

### POD-0802#08 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:08:16
claim: For a big bet, partner with an expensive-to-build product by putting your brand and some cash behind it in exchange for equity.
detail: Pitch it as a reverse acqui-hire: you bring a marketing department far larger than theirs and some money, take a large equity share and some cash flow. He calls it the most complex but biggest-upside move; sponsorships are the smaller bets. His own preference is to buy or partner with what already earns most on his channel rather than start another software company.

### POD-0802#09 | CASE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: sales-process | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:10:49
claim: A chronic-pain membership with an automated chat funnel loses most people at the end of the conversation; adding a call to sell a higher-priced annual deal would earn more.
detail: Organic CTA, then an AI chat, then a $99/month membership with a seven-day free trial. Hormozi suggests having people who reach the end book a call with an expert and sell something like $3,000 for a year. His point: do not make it scalable before it is valuable, because the premium price pays for scalability later.
numbers: 46,000 CTA engagements in a quarter; 50% engage with the chat; 3% reach the end; 17% of those sign up; ~40% cancel in the trial; owner says ~25 sales a week (does not reconcile with the quarterly funnel, unclear in audio); $99/month.
applies_to: Low-priced online memberships

### POD-0802#10 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:02
claim: A Kickstarter-driven D&D publisher with revenue only at launches should add Amazon and other book platforms first.
detail: Owner: two books, a third in progress, about 90% of revenue from one Kickstarter a year (first gap 14 months), roughly $30,000 spent on Facebook ads with weak results, products also on Shopify. Hormozi: this is a launch business with no revenue between launches. Step one this quarter: maximise distribution on every platform where books are bought, then expand the audience (TikTok later). Keep launching through Shopify to own the customer list.
numbers: His own books: Amazon sells ~50x more than Shopify; 30-40 SKUs from two books across four formats, five languages and 13 platforms (Kobo, Draft2Digital, Ingram and others).

### POD-0802#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:27
claim: Amazon works as a traffic source: readers buy a book, then find the author elsewhere; a product with thousands of five-star reviews sells without an audience.
detail: He rejects the idea that a big launch needs an existing audience. Reviews on the biggest book platform do the persuading. Where 90% of people buy books is where the books should be.
numbers: ~90% of book buyers use Amazon (his estimate); he buys any business book with ~10,000 five-star reviews.

### POD-0802#12 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:45
claim: Once there are three books, sell bundles across formats at $99-$149 so ads become profitable.
detail: Three books in audio, digital and hardback make nine units in one bundle, so a bundle price of $99, $125 or $149 gives enough margin to pay for cold traffic.
numbers: $99-$149 bundle price for nine units (three titles x three formats).
applies_to: Authors and publishers

### POD-0802#13 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:49
claim: Audio versions sell even for workbook-style content, and listeners repeat them because comprehension per listen is lower.
detail: Narration takes about half a day and can be removed if it fails. Listening is a second attention stream, so people get something new each time and report listening six times.
numbers: Audio share of his book sales rose from about 25% to about 40%.

### POD-0802#14 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: verticals | fw: CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:19:49
claim: For a hair-extension education company earning most from product, price the education low enough to maximise the number of stylists who sell hair.
detail: Education prices: $500 a month for stylists under six figures, $2,000 for six-figure stylists, $4,000 for salon owners; hair is 90% of the business at around 65% margin. Hormozi: treat education as CAC offset for the hair business. Find the highest price that does not lose buyers, since the key metric is the share of education buyers who become permanent hair sellers (about 80%, unclear in audio). Test $300 versus $1,000 or $2,000.
numbers: Price tiers $500, $2,000, $4,000 per month; ~65% margin on hair; 90% of revenue from hair.
applies_to: Education-plus-product businesses

### POD-0802#15 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:22:09
claim: Activate partners by having them buy and use the product first, with the education free.
detail: Prestige Labs had each gym buy a supplement stack for its trainers, who ran a 30-day challenge and gained conviction from their own before-and-after results, which made later sales easier. The gym still has buy-in, and product is easier to sell than info because buyers can hold it.
numbers: Activation purchase of about $5,000 in product (approximate, Ed would know).

### POD-0802#16 | CASE | topic: retention-ltv | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:24:06

### POD-0802#19 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:30:51
claim: Add a $25,000-$100,000 anchor offer, change only the secondary features, and pour the cash from the few buyers into advertising.
detail: Suit story: a $500 budget became $2,500 after a $16,000 suit was shown first. For a coach, the cheaper version keeps the same result but swaps secondary features: for instance Slack access instead of a direct cell phone. One in ten may take the top tier.
numbers: One in ten at the anchor; anchor $25,000-$100,000.
applies_to: Coaches with one offer and spare capacity
