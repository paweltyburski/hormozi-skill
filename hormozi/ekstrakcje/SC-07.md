### SC-07#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 4 Prioritize (5–9 employees): the founder becomes a manager of a first fully functioning team, and the overall constraint is trying to be everything to everyone.
detail: Leadership structure: one layer. Graduation theme: niche down to serve only people like your best customers, then adjust every function to that change. Constraint → graduation step:
- Product: said yes to anyone who paid → specialize (product V2) and price for a narrower avatar.
- Marketing: too many unqualified leads → better free stuff, more creatives, qualification and friction.
- Sales: speed to contact drops, no metrics → basic KPIs in a spreadsheet, faster contact, lead nurture.
- Customer service: no CS metrics → KPIs, centralized notes, basic CRM.
- IT: new team not using the tools → shared platforms, password manager, project management, chat.
- Recruiting: bad candidates, mis-hires → two-step four-way-fit interview, reference checks.
- HR: employees don't know how to behave → employee handbook and policies.
- Finance: lumpy cash flow → P&L, cash flow statement, general liability insurance.
Next stage: 10–20 employees, Productize.
numbers: ~3M businesses have 5–9 employees, versus ~30M businesses overall.

### SC-07#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: product-delivery | fw: 80/20 Customer Analysis | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:02
claim: Once feedback from every kind of customer has produced too many product directions, run an 80/20 analysis and rebuild the offer for the best 20% of customers.
detail: The best 20% pay the most, have the highest LTV, complain least, take the least effort to find and to fulfil, and love the product more than the rest. The other 80% usually consume most of the headaches, time and resources while returning the least. Build product V2 to be perfect for that 20% (your raving fans) and change the offer to reflect the value to that narrower customer.

### SC-07#03 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: 80/20 Customer Analysis | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:48
claim: A B2B lead-generation agency wanted to focus on plastic surgeons, but the data showed law firms were its best 20% of customers.
detail: The owner chose plastic surgeons only because he had more of them. Analysis of who paid most and complained least pointed to law firms: they had almost double the LTV, barely used customer service and said better things about the product, yet he was putting less effort into acquiring them. The diagnostic question for any business: who are your "law firms" — the customers that are least effort to find, least effort to fulfil and like you most?
numbers: Law firm clients had ~2x the LTV of the other segments.

### SC-07#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:58
claim: Saying yes to every type of customer is one of the "five horsemen" sticking points, typically hit at $1–3M revenue, because disparate customers make it hard to templatize and so harder to scale.
detail: The trap: say no to money and you fear revenue will drop; keep accepting everyone and nothing can be standardized. Many avatars and products make every step harder: attraction (all things to all people means nothing to no one), sales (reps must be trained on every prospect type and product), and fulfilment (everything is custom). One message for one avatar lets you train reps easily and templatize delivery, which raises margins, advertising effectiveness and sales conversion. The transition is mostly scary because it is new.
numbers: Usually hits between $1M and $3M in revenue (he gives no time period).

### SC-07#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:31
claim: Generalize first, then specialize: replacing the bottom 80% of customers with more of the top 20% can multiply revenue without new infrastructure.
detail: Early on you spray and pray and take money from anyone with a credit card; at this stage you decide who you are really in business to serve. Because the top 20% use relatively little capacity and produce a disproportionate share of revenue, the infrastructure already in place could serve several times more of them. Drop the 80%, refill with the 20%, and the business may grow sharply on the same team.
numbers: Current infrastructure could handle ~5x more top-20% customers; the business might triple without adding infrastructure.

### SC-07#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:31
claim: Don't become the CRM of your industry: resist expanding into every adjacent service just because more money seems available.
detail: The software joke that every product eventually turns into a CRM applies to all businesses: owners see they could serve more people and do more things, and slide into being all things to all people. Example: a marketing-agency owner noticed his clients failed to close the leads he generated and wanted to start closing for them too — which amounts to running thousands of other businesses instead of his own. Specialize product, avatar and price rather than widening scope.

### SC-07#07 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Niche Pricing | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:32
claim: Price for the narrow avatar you deliver the most value to, not for the weakest customer you can barely get results for.
detail: If the best 20% get several times the value the other 80% get, the price should rise to match. Then you may not need to multiply the number of top customers to replace the revenue you drop: selling only about double might be enough.
numbers: If the top 20% get ~5x the value, selling ~2x as many of them may replace all the lost revenue.

### SC-07#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: Niche Pricing | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:12
claim: The same time-management product gains price with each level of niche specificity, because value rises with who it is for, not what it is.
detail: From the offers book, levels of niching down: generic time management → time management for sales professionals → for B2B outbound sales professionals → for outbound sales professionals selling power tools and garden equipment. The core deliverable stays about the same; the buyer who sees something that sounds exactly like their own day feels it was made for them and pays more.
numbers: ~$19 generic → ~$99 sales professionals → ~$1,000 B2B outbound → ~$10,000 outbound in power tools and garden.

### SC-07#09 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: unit-economics | fw: Lead Magnets | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:47
claim: With more resources at stage 4, upgrade the free stuff to things that have a hard cost your competitors cannot afford to give away.
detail: Constraint: too many unqualified leads. Graduation: better free stuff, more creatives for volume, and qualification/friction so the extra volume self-selects. The lead magnet built four stages ago should be revisited. A costly giveaway is worth it if the extra leads lower cost to acquire a customer enough to net a gain over the delivery cost.
numbers: Example: a lead magnet costing 4 man-hours (~$100 in labour) still pays if it brings ~4x the leads and lowers CAC.

### SC-07#10 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: sales-process | fw: BANT | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:37
claim: Give expensive free value only to prospects who meet your qualifications, not to tire kickers.
detail: State the criteria up front: if you meet these, you get this for free. Test: would you give a bit of free work to the perfect avatar who has budget, need, authority and timing right now? Yes — so offer it to them and withhold it from everyone else.

### SC-07#11 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:24
claim: After niching down, rewrite the lead magnet for the new avatar and align every step from click to close with it.
detail: Different lead magnets attract different avatars. Example: moving upmarket from individual attorneys to bigger law firms, "how to go from 100 to 200 clients at your law firm" attracts a different buyer than "how to get your first five clients". Align the ads, headlines, lead magnet, offer, funnel headlines, qualification questions, sales script and nurture with the new avatar.

### SC-07#12 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:10
claim: Make call-outs more specific so the ads themselves qualify, and produce far more creative because current volume starts to fatigue.
detail: Instead of "looking for attorneys", call out "attorneys with at least five full-time attorneys at the firm" or "doing at least 100 cases a month" — whatever qualifies your best customers. The level-up at this stage is both better (more specific) messaging and much more of it than the one piece of content, set number of outreaches or fixed daily ad spend you are used to.
numbers: Typical starting volume that fatigues: e.g. ~$100/day ad spend.

### SC-07#13 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: BANT | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:06

detail: Budget, authority, need and timing still apply, but add criteria specific to your best customers. Example for gym-owner clients: must have at least one employee, 30 members and a signed lease. For law firms: not just "are you an attorney" but firm size or case volume.

### SC-07#14 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: management-ops | fw: Basic Sales KPIs | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:59
claim: At stage 4, response speed to leads slows and you may not know your close rate, so track basic sales KPIs manually in a spreadsheet.
detail: Symptom, as an example: leads once contacted the same day may now be reached seven days later. Basic KPIs (Google Sheets works): contact, schedule, cancellation, show, offer and close rates, plus cash collected — each also split by rep. Advanced (if the basics are already tracked, common for founders from sales): scheduled-call-to-close % and opt-in-to-close %. Also track absolute weekly sales and the percentages across everything.

### SC-07#15 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:41

detail: A page with no copy wastes the moment after opt-in. The headline should give a reason to book now. Use real constraints only — limited capacity, limited seats at an event, limited ability to fulfil; stretching the truth or lying is not the advice. State the facts and tell the truth.

### SC-07#16 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:05

detail: Owners often wonder why few people book, when the calendar sits at the very bottom of a long page. Get people to schedule as often and as early as possible: calendar at the top, plus repeated prompts to book further down.

### SC-07#17 | BENCHMARK | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:50
claim: In data from Hormozi's lead-working software ALAN, availability was the number one correlate of companies making more sales.
detail: Raise availability three ways: sell seven days a week; open the widest hours across time zones; offer 15-minute increments, because a prospect who cannot do 3:00 may take 3:15. One salesperson need not cover it all — stagger the team's schedules to get coverage. Most competitors won't do this, so it is an easy way to beat them on throughput.
numbers: 15-minute slots, 7 days a week, 9 a.m.–9 p.m. (spanning Pacific to Eastern time).

### SC-07#18 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:39
claim: Pay setters a percentage of sales on calls they set that close, so leads get booked quickly and setters own the manual reminders.
detail: The commission aligns setters with calls that actually show and close, not just with bookings. Because they are paid on the outcome, they can also handle the manual reminder sequence.
numbers: Setter incentive: ~3% of each sale from a call they set.

### SC-07#19 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:39
claim: Use both automated and personal reminders, label the automated ones as automated, and send manual reminders at set times.
detail: Personal reminders always beat automated ones, but both together are best. Don't disguise automation — say it is an automated reminder, so that when a personal message arrives the prospect knows a real person is behind it. Manual reminders: 24 hours out, the morning of, and one hour before. ALAN data: even when AI customized the automated responses, a human rep sending the messages still doubled the likelihood of showing.
numbers: A human rep's reminders ~2x show likelihood versus AI-personalized automation.

### SC-07#20 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:48
claim: Give daily kudos for the leading indicators of a sale — show rates, outreach, reminders, nurture, following the script — not only for closed deals.
detail: Use a Slack channel where people shout each other out for working leads, and call people out in huddles. Ask the praised person what they did; a typical answer is "just following the process", which in his view means other people are not. People will do more for status than for money.

### SC-07#21 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-process | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:44
claim: A lift in show rate raises revenue exactly as much as the same lift in close rate, and show rate is likely easier to move because it is more process-based and less skill-based.
detail: Every step of the funnel multiplies the same way, yet most owners obsess over close rate and ignore show rate. Raising close rate by a fifth feels unrealistic; raising show rate dramatically just takes working every lead. So every closer should work their leads for a phenomenal show rate.
numbers: Close rate 35% → 42% = 20% lift; show rate 50% → 80% (achievable with full lead-working) = 60% lift.

### SC-07#22 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:36
claim: Bribe people to show up, either by adding a positive or removing a negative, matched to what the avatar values.
detail: Removing a negative: a discount or something free. Adding a positive: money or a gift card (e.g. "$100 gift card if you show"). Hormozi showed up to an HR-software demo because attendance earned a credit toward the first month. Brick and mortar (tested through ALAN): ask which colour and size of shirt they want and set it aside, then remind them personally ("Sandy, your medium pink is waiting, see you at four"). No-shows still tend to reschedule.
numbers: HR software: $500 credit on the first month; shirt cost ~$6; a 10% show-rate lift ≈ 10% revenue lift.

### SC-07#23 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing, scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:13
claim: With unlimited options and limited resources, growth depends on the quality of prioritization; at an acquired 14-location medical business, one move came first.
detail: Many improvements were possible (customer service, show rates, team training), but the first priority was specializing the product and tailoring messaging to the right avatar, plus an ambassador program for that avatar — which allowed tripling the price. Second priority: dial in the sales script to improve the upsell. Solving the biggest constraint at any given time gives the maximum return for the effort.

### SC-07#24 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:31
claim: Stage 4 customer service has no metrics, so install basic CS KPIs and centralized per-customer notes in a CRM.
detail: Customer service = getting customers to refer, retain, review and resell. The early CRM is usually sales-driven; now decide whether to extend it or add a separate system for CS and delivery so every customer is tracked. Core KPIs everyone should know (Google Sheets is fine), separate from activation metrics: NPS, CSAT, churn % (share of customers leaving from last month to this month), refund % and chargeback %.

### SC-07#25 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:23
claim: Centralize notes from every touchpoint so the company seems to have one brain and customers never repeat themselves.
detail: Having to repeat yourself to a new rep signals nobody is acting on what you said. Before each call spend about five minutes reviewing prior notes, the goals captured at onboarding, the action steps assigned and the outcomes of calls with colleagues, then open by building on them. This looks far more professional and buys wiggle room for future mistakes. Also log complaints and improvement opportunities centrally, so a founder no longer delivering keeps a finger on the pulse of current problems.
numbers: ~5 minutes of note review before each call.

### SC-07#26 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:08
claim: Leila: customer feedback data comes in three depths (shallow, medium, deep), and right now the focus should be the shallow layer.
detail: Shallow: NPS, CES and CSAT, for a quick pulse. Medium: surveys where people can type longer answers. Deep: customer focus groups and interviews. Focus on the shallow metrics right now.

### SC-07#27 | DEFINITION | topic: retention-ltv | also: - | fw: NPS | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:53
claim: Net Promoter Score asks how likely, from 0 to 10, a customer is to recommend you to a friend or colleague, and is the most sensitive shallow metric.
detail: Only 9–10 count as promoters; 7–8 are passives and count as zero; 6 and below are detractors who are actively steering people away. Score = % promoters − % detractors, ranging from −100 to +100. Prefer it because generic CSAT or five-star scores make you feel better than you are, and because industry benchmarks are easy to find (search the typical NPS for your industry, e.g. lawn care or personal training, and save it as your reference). It is far harder to impress people than owners assume.
numbers: Average company ~−12; Apple ~61; Chick-fil-A ~58; Acquisition.com workshops ~91.

### SC-07#28 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: CSAT | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:37
claim: Use CSAT immediately after a specific value moment to measure and improve that one part of the service in real time.
detail: CSAT is a five-point scale from very dissatisfied to very satisfied. Ask it right after the event: "How satisfied were you with the support you just received?", "…with our onboarding process?" after onboarding, "…with our help materials?" after using them. Useful when targeting improvement in one specific area.
numbers: 5-point scale.

### SC-07#29 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: Revenue Churn | novelty: canon | ts: 00:27:24
claim: Revenue churn is revenue lost to churn divided by starting revenue, excluding new-customer revenue; track it alongside logo churn so expansion revenue becomes visible.
detail: Logo churn counts companies that leave. Revenue churn as a parallel metric lets you see expansion: with multiple price points, service levels or ascension paths, remaining customers can spend more than the departing ones took away. That is net negative churn — the business compounds on its existing base, which every business wants. First step: just start tracking it.
numbers: Example: $500 lost ÷ $10,000 starting revenue × 100 = 5% revenue churn.

### SC-07#30 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:20
claim: Move the new team from siloed to shared technology: work chat, a password manager and a project management tool.
detail: Constraint: the new team is not using everything. Steps:
1. Replace phones and texts with a work chat tool — Slack (best), or Zoho's Cliq.
2. Stop saving passwords on computers; use a centralized password manager. Hormozi has used LastPass for about a decade: when someone leaves you deactivate the user and all their access, and logins are tied to the person, not the device.
3. Replace individual to-do lists with a shared project management tool (monday.com, Asana, ClickUp) so several people see the same work and progress.
Sharing more requires more security and formal logging.

### SC-07#32 | FRAMEWORK | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: Two-Step Four-Way Fit Interview | novelty: canon | ts: 00:31:18
claim: To stop wasting time on bad candidates and mis-hiring, use a two-step interview that checks four fits: expectations, culture, skill and potential.
detail: Leila presents this part. Step 1 — screen (short 5–15 minute call) for expectations fit: pay matches, willing to relocate, accepts the hours, other must-haves. Step 2 — one interview by one person (the team is still small) checking: culture fit (matches the company's core values), skill fit (the top 3–5 skills the job needs), and potential fit (can they move up or into the next planned role; for roles you want filled indefinitely, such as collections or staff accountant, upward movement is not what you are looking for). Then select: the interviewer decides on the offer and checks references.

### SC-07#33 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:32:49
claim: Check several references thoroughly before hiring, because people have more experience lying than you have detecting liars.
detail: You would call several families before hiring a babysitter, and a business is arguably less resilient than a child, yet owners rarely check even one reference. Spending an hour or two is trivial against the monthly bill you are committing to. You would research a costly software purchase (reviews, consumer sites, other customers) but hire an equally expensive person on gut feel — don't assume you are a good judge of character.
numbers: Reference checks take ~1–2 hours; comparison: a $7,000/month software vs a $7,000/month employee.

### SC-07#34 | FRAMEWORK | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: Employee Handbook | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:35
claim: When employees don't know how to behave (PTO, sick days, dress, conduct), write an employee handbook that each person reads and signs.
detail: Templates exist online, or pay a service like LegalZoom. Sections:
1. Welcome statement: history, mission, values.
2. Employment policies: at-will status, job classification, employee records.
3. Code of conduct: professional behaviour, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, ethics (drugs, alcohol, fraternizing).
4. Work schedules: hours, attendance, punctuality, remote vs office.
5. Compensation: pay timing and method, salary grades, overtime, bonus rules, raises.
6. Benefits.
7. Leave: PTO, sick days, vacation, death, pregnancy.
8. Performance evaluation.
9. Safety and health.
10. Anti-discrimination and harassment.
11. Technology use: internet, social media, privacy, company devices.
12. Resignation and termination: notice, exit interviews, returning equipment.
13. Signed acknowledgment of receipt.

### SC-07#35 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: Employee Handbook | novelty: canon | ts: 00:36:10
claim: The handbook is how you graduate past 10 employees: new hires arrive playing a different game, and you need written rules to point to.
detail: You are no longer a tiny company — customers visit, people need to look like a business. A new hire's last job was chess and yours is checkers; you must explain the rules of engagement. Some will read the handbook like a bible and some not at all; what matters is that it is accurate so you can point to it when issues arise. ### SC-07#36 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:38:53
claim: Stage 4 cash flow turns lumpy from random one-time expenses; graduate with a P&L, a cash flow statement and general liability insurance.
detail: Lumpy costs come from bigger one-time investments that, in practice, recur regularly — expect and plan for them. Insurance is protection against what could kill the business: if it doesn't die, it keeps going. General liability covers bodily-injury claims, e.g. a customer or employee slipping on the premises; Hormozi needed it for his gyms because members used equipment.

### SC-07#37 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: P&L | novelty: canon | ts: 00:39:39
claim: The P&L shows whether the business made or lost money over a period; the cash flow statement shows whether you can survive until tomorrow and make payroll.
detail: P&L in lemonade terms: what you earned selling lemonade minus what you spent on lemons and cups. Cash flow statement: cash in versus cash out over the month. If you are profitable for the month but prepay two more months of lemons and cups before month-end, you can be cash-flow negative while showing a profit. You need both views.
