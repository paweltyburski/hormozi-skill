### POD-0153#01 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:47
claim: Hormozi sold five of his six gyms (and closed one) in 120 days at a low price because the next business was worth more than waiting for a better exit.
detail: Mentors and parents objected: years of work sold for little. He judged that a year spent chasing the ideal exit would cost him a year of the new business's income. Sales went to three buyers (three gyms to one, one to a competitor, one to a client), some on payment plans. The next month the new venture made over $200,000, replacing most of his gym income.
numbers: Sold at about 1 to 1.5x earnings; a broker and bundling might have fetched 3 to 4x but taken about a year; all gyms gone within 120 days; over $200,000 in the first month of the new business.

### POD-0153#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:32
claim: Settle conflicting advice by opportunity cost: compare what the new vehicle earns during the delay with what the delay would add.
detail: Estimate earnings from starting the new thing now against staying in the current vehicle. If the gain from a longer, cleaner process is smaller than what the new venture would earn in that extra time, take the quicker, worse outcome and move on.

### POD-0153#03 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:22
claim: A business sellable now for $3M or for $10M after a proper nine-month process is worth waiting for only if the new endeavour cannot make the extra $7M in that time.
detail: The test is the delta between a short-term and a long-term outcome, set against the opportunity cost of the alternative use of those months. Whichever side is larger wins. He stresses the logic applies to any decision, not just exits.
numbers: $3M now versus $10M after about 9 extra months; the extra $7M is the hurdle for the alternative.

### POD-0153#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:38
claim: Decide in a neutral state with realistic numbers (what comparable people actually earned), then commit fully and stop re-opening the decision.
detail: Avoid deciding from a momentary emotion or from an optimistic best case. After deciding, "done" and "get the ideal outcome" are contradictory action sets, so pick one. He says each day spent not acting on a made decision leaves him less sure of himself.

### POD-0153#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:55
claim: Well-meant advice is usually right for the adviser's situation, so test whether it fits your context now.
detail: Example: "never buy coffee, save your way to a million" suits a fixed-income employee but stops applying once you earn through a business. Security-driven people also warn against moving fast while ignoring that their own job depends on an entrepreneur taking risk.

### POD-0153#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:47
claim: Take advice from people who have achieved what you want, not merely people who earn more than you.
detail: People far ahead give advice shaped by their own lens, which can be poor for your stage. He still recommends listening to the very large operators for the high-level view of what drives outcomes. This refines the canon's "learn from anyone who out-earns you" and is not a full reversal.

### POD-0153#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:37
claim: To serve people well you should be many steps ahead of them, not one step, because one step hides the backtracking they will need later.
detail: Seeing about ten steps ahead lets you plan a route that takes six. He grants that being one step ahead is enough for selling someone something, but not for serving them over the long term.
numbers: Roughly 20 steps ahead is his stated ideal; a path of 10 steps can be reduced to 6.
