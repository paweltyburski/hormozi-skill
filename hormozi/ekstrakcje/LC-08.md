### LC-08#01 | DEFINITION | topic: paid-ads | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:09
claim: Paid ads are one-to-many advertising to cold audiences: you pay a platform to put your offer in front of its audience — renting eyeballs you don't own.
detail: Last of the Core Four. An ad interrupts someone else's content stream, with the platform as paid intermediary; borrowing other people's audiences gives instant scale. Goal: make more back from the ad plus the monetization behind it than it cost. Hormozi says at least half his wealth came from paid ads, though he rarely talks about them.

### LC-08#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:46
claim: The question is never whether paid ads work but how efficiently you extract value from the reach you buy relative to its cost.
detail: Reach is guaranteed because you pay for it. With enough money to reach everyone you would get some return; the question is whether it exceeds the cost. That is why paid ads scale fastest — and why cost management decides everything. Restated at 00:08:43.

### LC-08#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Four Problems of Paid Ads | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:46
claim: Paid ads pose four new problems: where to advertise, getting the right audience to see it, making the best ad, and getting permission to contact them again.
detail: Targeting is probably the hardest part (later: one of the hardest), because the right thing shown to the right audience gets a good response, yet platforms still target imperfectly. Making the ad = getting the highest share of viewers to act. Permission = capturing contact info (landing page). The lesson solves them in order.

### LC-08#04 | CASE | topic: paid-ads | also: verticals, offers | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:03
claim: Hormozi's first paid ad (Facebook, 2013) — all-caps text, no image — turned his mentor's $1,000 into about $5,700 in sales.
detail: After a two-day Facebook ads workshop, his gym mentor (who said Facebook didn't work) staked $1,000: fail and drop it, succeed and split profit after repaying spend. The ad: looking for five Chino residents for a free six-week challenge in exchange for using their before/after photos; click to sign up. Leads were called, brought in and sold. The mentor repaid himself, gave Hormozi a generous share, and said do it again.
numbers: $1,000 → ~19 sales × $299 ≈ $5,700.
applies_to: Local fitness.

### LC-08#05 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: verticals, retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:08

### LC-08#08 | DECISION_RULE | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Four Problems of Paid Ads | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:36
claim: Advertise where four things are true: you've used the platform as a consumer, you can target your ideal people, you know its native ad format, and you can afford its minimum spend.
detail: Familiarity makes your ads look like the platform's content; if you know none, pick where most customers are and learn it. Targeting avoids paying for wasted eyeballs (sell to women 25–45 → exclude everyone else). Action: start with one platform meeting all four and study its ads.

### LC-08#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:36
claim: Ads should look like content; running your best-performing content as ads merges the two, and that is when you become an exceptional advertiser.
detail: Native ads get consumed, not skipped. His gym-owner ads looked like content only gym owners (or competitors) would watch — pulling in the right audience and excluding the rest.

### LC-08#11 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:18
claim: Start where competitors advertise — same platform and same placement — and replicate before you iterate.
detail: Placements differ: LinkedIn message vs newsfeed; Instagram feed vs stories; YouTube sidebar, mid-roll, pre-roll. If others in your space make a placement work, so can you. Model, don't copy; experiment only after matching them.

### LC-08#12 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: Hook Retain Reward | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:26
claim: Use ad libraries to find the longest-running, most-engaged ads in your space, transcribe about 50, then write around 10 versions of your own.
detail: Note how each hooks, retains and rewards, how it makes the offer, and what the offer is. Don't copy one ad; let 50 blend in your head, write your own, format them similarly, and place them where ads already convert.
numbers: ~50 ads studied → ~10 own variations.

### LC-08#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:35
claim: Err on too narrow: start in the puddle where ROI is highest, then widen to ponds, lakes and oceans as profits let you afford lower efficiency.
detail: Choosing a 100M-user platform already cuts 99% of the world; keep narrowing. Each ring's returns fund the next, less efficient ring.

### LC-08#14 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Lookalike Audience | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:18
claim: Platforms offer two ways to target, alone or combined: lookalikes built from a list you upload, and filters you choose (age, gender, income, interest, time, location).
detail: Lookalike quality follows list quality: customers find more customers; opt-ins find more opt-ins (worse, but better than cold). Merge warm and cold outreach lists to reach the minimum size if needed. Layer filters on top, depending on market size. Filter examples (00:14:54): exclude ages that never buy; car-parts ads around car shows; "pets" interest if only pet owners buy.

### LC-08#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: Four Problems of Paid Ads | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:54
claim: With an exceptional offer, targeting is the only problem left: inefficient ads mean the right people aren't seeing them in enough quantity.
detail: Framing it this way massively simplifies what to fix.

### LC-08#16 | DECISION_RULE | topic: paid-ads | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:44

detail: Be as specific as possible but no further. Maybe one filter (older people, men, or women), and he would not add much over that; small markets are covered with modest spend anyway.
numbers: Local market ≈ 0.1% of a nation.

### LC-08#17 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: verticals, sales-process | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:44

### LC-08#18 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Lookalike Audience | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:29
claim: Targeting steps: pool all lists, separate customers from warm and cold contacts, seed lookalikes from the closest-to-customer list that is big enough, then add filters.
detail: If no list is big enough, combine them. Later, re-seed with customers or scheduled calls once you have enough. If lookalikes aren't available, start with interest targeting alone.

### LC-08#19 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: Hook Retain Reward | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:29
claim: Every ad has three parts — call-out, value, call to action — which is hook-retain-reward restated.
detail: Call-out gets the right people's attention; value retains them and builds trust and liking; the CTA is the reward ("if you got value from this, you'll love X"). Conclusion (00:46:53): call-outs make them notice, value gives a reason to act, the CTA gives a way.

### LC-08#20 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:36
claim: Never skip ads: drop ad-free subscriptions, click every ad in your industry so your feed fills with them, and turn the volume up.
detail: What works right now is the best advertising school. It turns a nuisance into constant learning — you learn from both content and interruptions. Action step repeated at 00:29:32.

### LC-08#21 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:36
claim: When studying others' ads, analyze the words used to call out the customer, how each value element and its opposite is presented, and how the CTA is delivered.
detail: Call-out language: gym owners vs health club owners vs micro gym vs trainers vs PTs — use the term customers self-identify with; this also reveals which avatar you want. Value: dream vs nightmare, likelihood vs risk, speed vs delay, ease vs effort. Model the language, don't copy it.

### LC-08#22 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: Hook Retain Reward | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:28
claim: The call-out matters most by far: put about 80% of your effort into the first five seconds, since each section's job is to sell the next.
detail: The headline is the first sale; nail the first five seconds with the right audience and almost any ad works. It is what Hormozi tests most. Ogilvy (~00:24:11): after writing the headline you've spent 80 cents of your ad dollar.
numbers: ~80% effort on first ~5 seconds.

### LC-08#23 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Hook Retain Reward | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:51
claim: Record value bodies and hooks separately — 10 bodies × 30 hooks = 300 variations — find the best hook, then the best body behind it.
detail: Each body breaks one belief, demonstrates one skill, or explains one nuance from experience. This test-and-iterate loop repeatedly produces winners you can scale.
numbers: 10 × 30 = 300 variations.

### LC-08#24 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:51
claim: When ads stall at $1K, $10K or $100K a day, he says the cap is ad quality, not the platform — probably too little creative volume, so test far more.
detail: Platforms want your money. An ad converts with audiences of a certain warmth and stops beyond it. Top 1% of Facebook advertisers test ~11x more creative and scale 10–100x more. Owners who "make tons of ads" often mean ~10 a month — diagnosis complete. Unless targeting is very broad, scale requires testing hooks, value and CTAs to find a package that reaches 100M people profitably.
numbers: Top 1% test ~11x more creative; ~10 ads/month is too few.

### LC-08#25 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: Call-Outs (Labels, Yes Questions, If-Then, Ridiculous Results) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:11
claim: Four verbal call-outs are, in his experience, the most common or best-working: labels, yes questions, if-then statements and ridiculous results.
detail: Labels group people by trait, title or place — "homeowners", "Clark County moms", "gym owners", "looking for five Chino residents" (local hack); the customer must identify with it. Yes questions get a nod: "Do you snore? Wake up with cotton mouth? Pee more than once a night?" If-then helps self-selection: "If you spend over $10K/month on ads, I can save you 20%+"; class-action "if you were born between X and Y in Z". Ridiculous results are bizarre outcomes: "Massage studio booked two years out, clients furious"; "Woman lost 50 lb eating pizza, fired her trainer".

### LC-08#26 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Contrast Likeness Scene | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:45
claim: Visual and audio call-outs — contrast, likeness and scene — make the right person notice before words land.
detail: Contrast: what stands out early (bright shirts beat dull, attractive faces beat plain, motion beats stillness). Likeness: showing the label — lab coat for doctors, pipes or under-sink set for plumbers, seniors for senior products. Scene: showing the yes question — tossing in bed (sleep), a pear by an hourglass (body shape), a rock through a window, a local landmark. Align words, contrast, likeness and scene; that alignment separates a $1M/year campaign from a $100M/year one.

### LC-08#27 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Contrast Likeness Scene | novelty: canon | ts: 00:27:33
claim: People buy from people who look and talk like them, so if your customers all resemble you, your ads are calling out people like you — cast faces matching the customers you want.
detail: Broad customer base: cast varied ethnicities, ages, genders and personalities like the customers, not you. Narrow base: cast that group. Scaling a brand with a diverse audience: use several talking heads as faces of the brand while the founder steps back and owns it.

### LC-08#28 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:32
claim: People value an offer when benefits look big and costs small, so the best ads maximize perceived benefits and minimize perceived costs.
detail: If costs outweigh benefits, the offer or lead magnet isn't valued; the What-Who-When framework exists to tip that balance.

### LC-08#29 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: What-Who-When | novelty: canon | ts: 00:30:28
claim: What-Who-When is Hormozi's ideation framework for ad value: what (eight value drivers), who (whose perspective grants the status), when (past, present, future).
detail: 8 elements × many perspectives × 3 timeframes yields a very large number of angles (he says not literally unlimited) to attack the prospect's beliefs. Not every ad will include all eight elements; the more you weave in seamlessly, the more powerful the ad. An ideation tool, not a fill-in template.

### LC-08#30 | FRAMEWORK | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: What-Who-When | novelty: canon | ts: 00:31:17
claim: The "what" is eight elements: the value equation's carrots (dream, likely, fast, easy) and their sticks (nightmare, risky, slow, hard) — show and tell both.
detail: Dream: the maximum benefit after buying. Nightmare: worsening pain of going without, strongest for problems that grow. Likelihood: past failures make buyers assume risk — explain away failures, show people like them succeeding, authority, guarantees, a better chance than now. Risk: show repeating past failures if they don't act. Speed: how much faster with you; slowness: they'll never get there at their current rate. Ease: avoid what they hate without work or skill; effort: without you they keep sacrificing and still get nowhere. Keep it compact.

### LC-08#31 | DEFINITION | topic: offers | also: paid-ads | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:22
claim: Ease comes from a lack of needed work or skill.
detail: People assume change means doing what they hate and giving up what they love; showing little effort and no special qualifications are needed makes the outcome feel easy.
quote: Ease comes from a lack of needed work or skill.

### LC-08#32 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: offers | fw: What-Who-When | novelty: canon | ts: 00:35:24
claim: The "who": humans are status-driven and status comes from how others treat them, so describe each benefit through the eyes of those who grant or withhold the customer's status.
detail: Spouse, kids, parents, colleagues, bosses, friends, rivals. Motivation comes from deprivation, and you can't know where a prospect is most status-starved (great marriage but beaten up at work?), so hit many keys: wife, work rival, boss, kids, "frenemy dad".

### LC-08#33 | EXAMPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: What-Who-When | novelty: canon | ts: 00:36:13
claim: A benefit lands harder when others notice it: the wife saying he may fit his wedding tux, a colleague saying he looks great, golf partners gaping at the new $400 driver.
detail: Self-view: tightening the belt two notches. Others' view adds status, plus angles like "people who lose weight get promoted more". Income works the same: what matters is the wife, colleagues or golf partners noticing.

### LC-08#34 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: What-Who-When | novelty: canon | ts: 00:37:48
claim: The "when": make prospects see their own timeline — past, present and future — including a negative future if they don't act.
detail: How his wife saw him at the wedding, today, and after losing weight; past struggles and lost respect. Hardest but most compelling is negative future visualization: "+5 lb a year means +25 lb in five years — what will your wife and kids think? You on the sideline while the rival dad plays with your kids." Combined example (00:40:35): spouse noticing how fast he fits the suit she loves; kids eating healthier month after month; catching his reflection in a mall months later.

### LC-08#35 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:40:35
claim: Make ads as specific as you can, but no more specific than the platform's space allows; use visuals to add specificity without length.
detail: Specific copy is more efficient but longer, and an overlong ad loses efficiency. Contrast, likeness and scene can say more than words without adding length, making the ad more profitable.

### LC-08#36 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: Hook Retain Reward | novelty: canon | ts: 00:42:56
claim: The CTA must be clear, not clever: in the brief window of motivation, say exactly what to do next and make it easy.
detail: Many ads have no good CTA. Spell it out: click this button, call this number, reply "yes", go to this site. Simple numbers, obvious buttons, short memorable URLs (acquisition.com/training, not a long coded path).

### LC-08#39 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Four Problems of Paid Ads | novelty: canon | ts: 00:45:14
claim: Capture contact info with a simple landing page; the three layouts he uses most are: headline + sub-headline + contact fields + submit; that plus an image; or plus image and three bullets.
detail: Pick one and test. Simpler pages mean fewer variables to test and fewer things for the visitor to do: they wanted it, the page offers it, they submit — high conversion. Match the page to the ad: same words, colors and person. Underrated because it takes effort.
