### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:02:00
claim: Five levels of competence mindset, from best to worst: positive active, positive passive, neutral passive, negative passive, negative active.
detail: Level 1: nobody proved it impossible, so I will make it work whatever the teacher's quality (Bannister, Elon). Level 2: someone did it, so I can (A-minus players; replicate first). Level 3: let's see if it works for me; where the majority of people sit, and they are sometimes mediocre customers. Level 4: works for others, probably not me; half-hearted effort. Level 5: works for everyone but me; actively trying to prove it fails. Making success depend on the teacher hands them the power.
applies_to: Students, customers and employees alike.
### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#02 | CASE | topic: lead-getters | also: operator-mindset | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:17
claim: A salon owner said the gift-card campaign failed; it turned out he did not do it, so a different thing failed.
detail: Hormozi asked whether he got a referral name from each person; clients came back to use the card on themselves. The owner insisted he had told them, but the telling was clearly insufficient. Hormozi refused to accept blame for a play that worked for many other businesses. Once the owner admitted he had not run it properly, he could rerun it correctly.

### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#04 | CASE | topic: operator-mindset | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:10:11
claim: A gym owner who blamed his market for failure switched to executing as if the system worked and became one of the top facilities out of thousands.
detail: He had insisted his market, zip code and weight-loss leads were different. After a few coaching calls he decided to put the energy he spent proving the system wrong into doing everything as though it worked. ### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#05 | CASE | topic: retention-ltv | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:11:04
claim: An early gym client who claimed nothing worked for her (a thyroid, calories not applying) became a top client and referrer after being asked to choose between being right and getting the result.
detail: She was among his first ~50 gym customers. After 2-4 weeks, the others who started with her lost weight. He asked which mattered more, being right or being thin; she chose thin, followed the plan, lost a lot of weight and referred everyone. Converted level-5 customers become brand champions.
numbers: about the first 50 customers; 2-4 weeks before peers showed results.

### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: objections-closing | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:05
claim: With a customer who is arguing it will not work for them, offer the explicit choice between being right and getting the outcome.
detail: Say you are fine either way, but they are the one who has to live with it. In his opinion, level-5 people have the highest potential to jump to level 1 because they already put in active effort, only aimed at proving it wrong; sometimes, if you can get the aim to flip, they become extremely committed. Do not lose hope on them.
### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:15:57
claim: Past learning is a bridge of bricks: credit goes to the last teacher only because it was the most recent, not the most important.
detail: Each programme adds bricks, and skills build on each other, as with arithmetic before algebra. People who succeed with a given course are the ones who arrived with the earlier skills. Disparaging the basics teacher is a mistake: they supplied what you needed to reach this step.

### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:41
claim: The more skills and meta skills a person brings, the vaguer the instructions they can act on; "it did not work" often means a remedial level at one step.
detail: Example: he could build a nose-strip company from a vague request because he already has marketing, sales, product and logistics skills. Someone can be advanced overall and a beginner at one prerequisite step, so the programme appears to fail. When something fails, ask which skill was missing before blaming the product.
numbers: he claims ~30% of people cannot read well (no source given).

### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:05
claim: Every experience, even a terrible one, pays off, either directly or by showing what to do the opposite way.
detail: Inversion: list everything that made the bad course, boss, product or parenting awful and do the reverse. He says he has never had a negative ROI on a purchase of education, including from bad teachers.

### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#10 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:13
claim: Use the five levels to grade each team member and yourself, per domain, then borrow the mindset from the domain where you do best.
detail: People can be level 1 in business and level 4 in health or marriage. Look at the thought process in your strongest domain and apply it to the weakest. For a team, ask who wants to prove the product will not work, who is waiting to see, and who believes it can be done.

### YT-3Ju1I37jWUM#11 | TACTIC | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:23:05
claim: Replicate exactly before you iterate; only then improve using your other skills.
detail: Follow the teacher's steps to the letter to get the same result, retrace why each step exists, and then list ways to do it better. Iterating first means you do not know why it worked. Stack teachers: use what you learned from the first two to improve what the third taught you. He frames it as a no-fail position: you learn either what to do or what not to do.
