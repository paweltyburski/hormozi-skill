### MM-12#01 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches, verticals | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: The giveaway model came from a coach who gave away one full scholarship to his year-long program and made his money selling "partial scholarships" to everyone else who applied.
detail: The owner advertised a free scholarship for his year-long flagship program. Applicants opted in and answered questions about their business and why they should be picked. He ran a countdown, then a live webinar drawing where he sold how great the program was and picked the winner. The money came from the "participation trophy": he then contacted every applicant (who had written out their pain points, aspirations and reasons) and ran a second countdown for them to redeem a partial scholarship — a discount off the advertised value of the grand prize. He taught the same structure to the online fitness trainers he coached ("I'm doing a one-year transformation giveaway… partial scholarships for everyone else"), showing it was a repeatable framework: grand prize + promotional (participation) offer.

### MM-12#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:03
claim: Giveaways work because people who might win listen differently — they imagine owning the prize — so you can sell its value ethically to a highly engaged crowd while generating leads and goodwill.
detail: Pitching a product from a stage is one thing; pitching it while everyone in the audience is waiting to see if they will be picked is another — they lean in and picture themselves winning, which creates a much higher level of receptivity. Giving things away also builds goodwill. Because persuasion works so well when there is a chance to win, sell the grand prize as hard as you can (00:13:46): everyone who entered has already shown interest, and anyone who shows interest should be given an offer. The mechanism is powerful enough to be legally regulated — charging for entry makes it an illegal lottery — so check with your lawyers that your format is legal in your area (00:15:45).

### MM-12#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:54
claim: A giveaway runs in five steps: pick a grand prize and a promotional offer, collect contact info and eligibility, put entry on a deadline, announce and deliver the grand prize, then offer the promotional offer to everyone else on a new deadline.
detail: A giveaway (also called sweepstakes, scholarship, "enter for a chance to win") advertises a chance to win a big prize in exchange for contact information and anything else you ask for; after picking the winner, everyone else is offered the big prize at a discount. Steps: (1) Choose the grand prize (your main thing) and the promotional offer (the participation trophy — usually a discount on the main prize, a truncated or shorter version, with a reason it is a bit less and a reason to buy now). (2) Ask for contact info and eligibility criteria. (3) Put the giveaway on a deadline. (4) Announce the winner and actually give the prize. (5) Contact everyone else with the promotional offer — also on a deadline, or nobody acts. Urgency is needed at every step.

### MM-12#04 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: pricing, offers | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:54
claim: Make the grand prize the thing you want everyone to buy, and give it an explicit monetary value so it anchors the price of the promotional offer.
detail: Assign the grand prize a stated value; it becomes the price anchor for the discounted offer later. Example: if you sell something worth $5,000 for $2,000, advertise the $5,000 value. Prefer giving away your own main product over unrelated prizes (a vacation only makes sense if you are a travel agent). Leads enter because they find the grand prize interesting, so when you sell the promotional offer you do not need to create desire — they already said they want it; you just need an offer good enough to say yes to. Name the promotional offer however fits: scholarship, gift card, voucher, dollars off, store credit. The bigger the discount, the more compelling.

### MM-12#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:47
claim: Give your own high-ticket service as the prize instead of cash: its retail value far exceeds what it costs you to deliver.
detail: Make the grand prize roughly equal to the worth of your expensive offer. Something sold at $10,000 might cost only $2,000–3,000 in hard costs to deliver, which is inexpensive next to marketing spend — an arbitrage between your cost and the retail value. Cash prizes are his least preferred: they are valuable to everyone, but the least original. If you work in a specific niche, a prize built around your service builds in that cost-versus-value gap and makes doubled prizes (see Refer a Winner) affordable.
numbers: $10,000 retail prize ≈ $2,000–3,000 hard cost.

### MM-12#06 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:36
claim: A portfolio company's giveaway flopped because the prize ($300 event tickets) was tiny relative to its $15K–40K offer; rerun with $50,000 of equipment, it worked.
detail: Troubleshooting rule: if a giveaway does not work, the grand prize was not grand enough. This B2B company sold offers in the $15,000–40,000 range but gave away tickets to its own event worth about $300. Hormozi's point: to sell $40,000 products you need at minimum $40,000-level giveaways. They reran it offering $50,000 of equipment relevant to that business-owner type and it worked. Do not be cheap on the grand prize — people have to really want it.
numbers: Failed prize ~$300 vs offer $15K–40K; successful prize $50,000 of equipment.
applies_to: B2B high-ticket offers.

### MM-12#07 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: sales-process, leads-fundamentals | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:43
claim: To enter, people must provide contact details, meet eligibility criteria and can take optional promotional actions; only qualified entrants are able to win, and the entry form works as a sales application.
detail: Three requirement types: (1) contact details, for follow-up; (2) eligibility — only people able to purchase your core service (no Georgia veterinarians entering a giveaway at a Nebraska retailers' conference); (3) actions turning them into promoters or stronger prospects — bring a friend, join a group, share a post, leave a review, attend a call or event. Extra friction reduces entries, but an attractive prize gets people through the steps. Application questions as sales ammo (00:15:45): Why pick you; why this program; why now; why it matters; what their goals are; optionally income. Score and rank leads by how likely they are to buy. People write pages about themselves to win, compared with two or three fields on a standard lead form (00:24:24).

### MM-12#08 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:07
claim: Run entry for 3–7 days, update entrants daily across channels, and use each countdown message to sell a benefit of the grand prize paired with proof.
detail: Make it urgent: about a week works best because attention spans are short. As soon as someone enters, update them daily — how long until the winner is announced — via email, DM, text, social posts, publicly in your community and one-to-one at scale via broadcast; once a day across all platforms is fine for short windows. For a longer (e.g., 30-day) giveaway do not message daily; follow the Whisper to Shout pattern from the Leads book (a reverse launch). The step most people miss: do not just count down — each day sell a different benefit of the prize (vacation example: the drinks, restaurants, weather, beach, views, hotel room) and pair it with social proof of that benefit (past winners John and Sally loved Egypt; Kevin and Kelly said the room was the best they'd ever stayed in). That way reminders feel like value, not a take.
numbers: Entry window 3–7 days; one update per day per platform (short giveaways).

### MM-12#09 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches, sales-process | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:09
claim: Announce the winner at a live group event, then privately tell every other qualified entrant they won a partial prize and must book a call to redeem it by a deadline.
detail: Bring everyone together for the drawing (webinar, stage, virtual call, group) because it maximises your ability to sell and convert. Announce the winner publicly; message everyone else privately (email, text, DM). Key idea: "many will enter, many will win" — only one gets the grand prize, but every qualified entrant gets a promotional offer as their participation trophy. Give a reason they earned it (great participant, commented, shared, told a compelling story). On the webinar you can say your team will reach out to some attendees about partial scholarships. Then restart urgency: ask them to schedule a call to redeem, again with a 3–7 day deadline, and repeat the benefits/social-proof messaging during this second countdown.
numbers: Redemption deadline 3–7 days.

### MM-12#10 | BENCHMARK | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:05
claim: Size the promotional discount at about 10–30% of gross margin, but present it against the grand prize's full value so it looks like a massive deal.
detail: Rule of thumb: make the promotional offer equal to 10–30% of your gross margins. Example: grand prize advertised at $5,000 value, normal retail price $2,000; the promotional winner pays $1,800 (10% off retail). When telling them they won, frame it as $5,000 of value for $1,800 — which reads like 64% off instead of 10%. Travel-agent version: booking it yourself would cost $5,000; I normally retail it at $2,000; participation winners get 10–30% off ($200–600). The comparison always bakes in the efficiencies they would lose doing it on their own.
numbers: Discount 10–30% of gross margin; $5,000 value / $2,000 retail / $1,800 promo price; 10% real discount vs 64% perceived.

### MM-12#11 | EXAMPLE | topic: money-models | also: verticals | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:34
claim: Giveaway examples across business types pair a high-value grand prize with a voucher or partial version as the promotional offer.
detail: Dentist — "Free Perfect Smile Giveaway": grand prize invisible braces ($6,000 retail); promo a $2,000 gift card toward braces. Physical products — a year of organic dog food ($1,000 retail); promo a $300 gift card usable only with a one-year subscription or if prepaid (you can attach terms). Services — free one-year package ($5,000 retail); promo a $2,000 voucher toward one year of service; shrink the voucher if your margins are poor. Consulting (business, health, relationships, etc.) — free 16-week turnaround ($12,000 retail, the top version); promo a partial: half the time, half the price, or same duration with one component removed.
numbers: Braces $6,000 / $2,000 card; dog food $1,000 / $300 card; services $5,000 / $2,000 voucher; consulting $12,000.

### MM-12#12 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: lead-getters | fw: Refer a Winner | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:40
claim: Give away two grand prizes — one to the winner and one to whoever referred them — to make every entrant recruit others, multiplying leads.
detail: "Refer a winner and you win too": if a person you referred wins, you also win a grand prize. Each referral becomes another chance to win, so entrants have a huge incentive to bring many people. Example from Skool: if someone you refer finishes in the top 10 of a game, you also get prizes. Applied to the equipment case: two $50,000 packages, or two $25,000 packages — two big prizes either way. If you have the cash, doubling the incentive is worth it; giving away your own service keeps the hard cost of two prizes low.
numbers: Can double, triple or quadruple giveaway leads.

### MM-12#13 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:35
claim: Limit entries by time, by count, or both, and set the cap by how many leads your team can actually contact.
detail: Scarcity: run for a set time (e.g., 7 days), a set number of entries (e.g., 5,000), or both — he prefers both. Set the cap from capacity: if your team can only reach out to about 200 leads, raise qualifications and cap entries at 200 — which also raises each entrant's odds of winning and makes entry more attractive. If you cannot contact more than 200 within 7 days, cap at 200 entries and 7 days, whichever comes first.
numbers: Example caps: 7 days; 5,000 entries; 200 leads when contact capacity is limited.

### MM-12#14 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: promotions-launches | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:38
claim: Put a deadline on each of the three actions you need: entering, claiming the prize, and using it.
detail: Urgency at (1) entry, (2) claiming, and (3) using the prize — even the grand prize winner (scholarship, vacation) gets a time window to use it, because you want people to consume and start. This matters most for partial-scholarship winners, whom you need to turn into customers. Make all deadlines clear in the advertising. Once winners are known, tell them how long they have to claim and get their call scheduled within the next week if possible; once they know what they won, tell them how long they have to use it. He prefers hours for decision windows but has gone up to five days.

### MM-12#15 | SCRIPT | topic: money-models | also: sales-process, objections-closing | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:41
claim: On promotional-offer calls, say up front there are two ways to use the credit; offer it on the grand prize first, then the same percentage off a cheaper back-pocket product.
detail: At the start of the call, tell them they have two ways to use the partial scholarship/voucher/credit and you will help them find the one that fits. Offer the discount on the grand prize first. If they decline (e.g., cannot afford it), offer the same percentage discount on another relevant product, roughly half to a third of the price. Dentist example: full set of braces with $2,000 off is too much → "80% of your pictures show your top teeth, so let's do top teeth only for $3,000 and I'll take $1,000 off." Keep the percentage the same so they do not feel they lost part of their prize. Also: if no-shows for in-person appointments become a problem, stop mass notifications and contact qualifiers individually, telling them they need to claim their prize — more personalisation fixes it.

### MM-12#16 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv, pricing | fw: Free Giveaways | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:49
claim: In recurring-revenue businesses, spread the promotional discount across the longest commitment they will accept and auto-bill at full price afterwards — never as free months up front.
detail: First choice is to collect as much cash up front as possible: if they will pay the discounted annual amount in full, take it. Otherwise spread the discount over the longest period they agree to and set up a subscription that bills automatically at the normal rate after the discounted period ends. Avoid "the $2,000 covers your first four months and billing starts in month five" — he has the scar tissue; it does not work.
numbers: $8,000/year service − $2,000 credit = $6,000 → $500/month for 12 months, then $8,000/12 (~$667/month).
