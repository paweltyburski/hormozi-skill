### SC-05#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 2 Advertise: a solo founder with freelancers whose new customers come inconsistently; the fix is to let more people know about the business, every day.
detail: Headcount 1, role doer. A common mistake is copying what established founders do today instead of getting your hands dirty. You lead freelancers, not employees. Company count: within the ~30M US firms; no Census breakdown, the speaker guesses it is lower. Constraint → graduation step:
- Product: unreliable for paying customers → good enough for now, unscalably.
- Marketing: inconsistent leads → Rule of 100, daily.
- Sales: inconsistent conversions → a setting and closing script (CLOSER).
- Customer service: more complaints → Angry Boat and HEAR for upset customers, Epiphany Bridge for testimonials.
- IT: no money → free tools, a lead-capture site, a starter CRM.
- Recruiting: too much work for part-timers → convert them to full-time, post jobs.
- HR: contractors treated as employees → W-2 status.
- Finance: no tracking or tax plan → QuickBooks, quarterly tax savings, check the bank daily.
This is the last stage without employees.

### SC-05#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:57
claim: Valuable over scalable: first do whatever it takes for paying customers to get the result, then build a scalable version that delivers the same result.
detail: Optimize first for experience and result. Only later worry about the unscalable things you did (driving to pick someone up, going to the airport, and so on). A big error new entrepreneurs make is spending so much time making the offer scalable that they forget to make it valuable. At this stage the product's clear focus is to be actually good; ignore scalability entirely.

### SC-05#04 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: outreach | fw: Rule of 100 | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:21
claim: The Rule of 100 means 100 advertising actions every single day; the common failure is stopping advertising once you have enough customers.
detail: Lead flow is inconsistent at this stage because the founder generates the leads and starts and stops. The fix is consistent inputs: 100 dials, 100 outreaches, 100 texts or 100 emails. Do it every day, not every other day, not only when you need customers. Consistency matters most. Most people go wrong by stopping when they feel full, and soon wish they had kept going. Excess leads are a good problem you can solve later. Consistent generation means that when you open capacity, people already want to buy.
numbers: 100 actions per day.

### SC-05#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: outreach, content-brand, paid-ads | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:32
claim: The Rule of 100 applies to each of the Core Four advertising methods: warm outreach, cold outreach, free content and paid ads.
detail: - Warm outreach (one-to-one, people who know you): 100 reach-outs a day.
- Cold outreach (one-to-one, people who do not know you): e.g. LinkedIn filtered searches, followers of your friends on Instagram, friends of friends on Facebook, or a purchased list. Private channels: text, email, door knocking.
- Content (one-to-many, people who know you): about 100 minutes a day. Make the post, message people who liked or commented, and use the rest of the time to comment on other posts aimed at your audience.
- Paid ads (one-to-many, the most leveraged method one person can run): a small daily budget, plus about 100 minutes a day of creative work.
One presenter suggests screenshotting this as a reference for applying the rule to each method (speaker unclear).

### SC-05#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:07
claim: CLOSER is Hormozi's structure for sales calls: Clarify, Label, Overview past pains, Sell the vacation, Explain away concerns, Reinforce the decision.
detail: - C: Clarify what prompted them to act and attend.
- L: Label them with the problem, or with the plan you aim to solve it.
- O: Overview the pains and experiences they've had before.
- S: Sell the vacation, not the plane ride (outcome over process).
- E: Explain away their concerns once the offer is made.
- R: Reinforce the decision by convincing them it was a great choice and giving them a smooth handoff into the next step with customer service.
Simple mental model: where you are now, where you want to be, what you think is blocking you, and then how I can help you get past it.

### SC-05#10 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: Three Pillar Pitch | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:54
claim: Pitch in three pillars: boil success down to three things the customer needs, give each a one-line statement and an analogy, and keep the whole pitch to two or three minutes.
detail: Structure:
1. State the three things someone needs to succeed.
2. Make the core argument with an image: in fitness, fitness, nutrition and accountability are three legs of a stool. Drop any one and it falls: no accountability, you won't do it; bad eating, no weight loss; no training, you lose muscle and regain the weight. With all three, you cannot lose.
3. Explain each pillar with a one-liner and an analogy. Accountability: parents made you brush your teeth, and now you do it unprompted. External accountability built a habit that became internal.
Salespeople often pitch for 20 minutes when prospects do not care. Keep it short and spend most of the call on the prospect.
numbers: Pitch length ~2–3 minutes (vs. a typical 20).

### SC-05#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:41
claim: Deprivation drives people, so devote most of the call to walking through their earlier attempts and pains before pitching; this is the "pain cycle".
detail: Loop until every option is used up: What have you tried so far? How did that go? What did you like? What didn't you like? What else have you tried? Analogy: the hungrier someone is, the more eager to eat. Reminding them of their hunger (when they last ate, the aroma of cookies) boosts motivation. In business: "When was your lead flow last steady? What would it be like if it were?" Stretching the felt deprivation raises motivation to act.

### SC-05#12 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: CLOSER | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:53
claim: CLOSER translated into a sequence of questions: what made you take action, where you want to be, what is in the way, what you have tried, then the pitch, then handle concerns and reinforce.
detail: 1. Clarify: What made you opt in, engage, open my email or respond to my comment?
2. Label: So it sounds like you'd like to be here — is that right?
3. Overview: What has gotten in the way? What else have you tried? How did that work for you? (repeat)
4. Sell: the three-pillar pitch.
5. Explain: overcome the concerns that block a decision today.
6. Reinforce: affirm the decision and hand them into great onboarding.

### SC-05#13 | FRAMEWORK | topic: objections-closing | also: - | fw: CLOSER | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:38
claim: Concerns you have to overcome to get a decision today typically come in five kinds: time, stall, money, preferences (fit), and decision maker (authority).
detail: - Time: "I'm busy right now."
- Stall: "I don't like deciding quickly; give me a couple of days."
- Money: "This costs too much."
- Preferences: "I dislike this particular thing; I'd rather reach the result my own way."
- Decision maker / authority: the choice is passed to somebody else.

### SC-05#14 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: sales-team | fw: CLOSER | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:24
claim: In a two-step sale the setter runs only CLO — clarify, label, overview the pain — and hands off to the closer at peak hunger without offering any solution.
detail: Split CLOSER into CLO for the setter and SER for the closer. The setter asks about the problem and the goal, labels the gap and the obstacles they have tried, and overviews the pain. Just as the prospect is "starving", the setter says something like "I think you'd be a good fit. Let me set you up with John." Withholding the solution leaves them hungry, which raises the chance they show up for the closing call. The setter should not sell.

### SC-05#15 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: AAA | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:24
claim: Run the AAA loop — acknowledge, associate, ask — in between questions, so a scripted call feels natural, not mechanical.
detail: 1. Acknowledge: "Got it — so X, Y and Z are what you're dealing with, correct?"
2. Associate: "That actually puts you in the same boat as some of our best customers."
3. Ask: proceed to the next scripted question.
Repeat this cycle after every question.

### SC-05#16 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: objections-closing | fw: Angry Boat | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:04
claim: Only one person can sit in the "angry boat", so when a customer is furious, the rep should get even more upset on their behalf, and he expects the customer to back off and backpedal.
detail: Whoever is most upset occupies the boat. Example: a customer booked a workshop on the only date offered, then realized they could not come, and the next date is six months away. The rep responds: "I can't believe this. That's unacceptable. I'm so upset about this — I have to do something about it." He says the customer will start to back off, almost backpedalling ("No, don't fire anyone, it's not that big a deal"). The worst thing a customer can hear when upset is an indifferent "I don't know if we can do anything about that."

### SC-05#17 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: - | fw: HEAR | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:39
claim: De-escalate an upset customer with HEAR: Hear them out, Empathize and acknowledge, Apologize, Resolve together.
detail: 1. Hear them out. Let them vent and stay quiet, with only body language and short acknowledgments ("Okay. Oh my goodness.").
2. Empathize and acknowledge (the transcript says "emphasize"). Agree with their feelings and escalate past their upset: "I can't believe you had to deal with that... Do I need to fire somebody?"
3. Apologize. It fixes nothing, but taking accountability for the impact matters to them.
4. Resolve with the customer, not around them, so they like the solution. Offer options. In the workshop example: (a) a refund, framed as "I don't want to refund you because I know you want to do this"; (b) getting them into a date that works (e.g. December 12); (c) getting them into the next-level event, paid for from the rep's own paycheck in the example. Then ask which they prefer, or whether something else would be better.

### SC-05#18 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: operator-mindset | fw: HEAR | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:56
claim: Never back down from or cower before an upset customer; always confront the situation, because avoiding hard conversations is a missing skill at this stage.
detail: Many early founders do not deal with upset customers because they lack the skill of hard conversations. The HEAR approach works for hard conversations anywhere, including with a spouse or a friend.

### SC-05#19 | FRAMEWORK | topic: lead-getters | also: retention-ltv, sales-process | fw: Epiphany Bridge | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:41
claim: Capture testimonials with the Epiphany Bridge interview: walk the customer through internal and external struggle, skepticism, why they bought anyway, then external and internal victory.
detail: A new business will not keep growing without social proof. Interview order:
1. Internal struggle: what rock bottom felt like, and what they could not do (weight loss: not wearing a bikini, not looking in the mirror).
2. External struggle: objective "before" metrics (e.g. 250 lb, 3,000 calories a day, a size 45 waist).
3. Skepticism: their main concerns before joining.
4. What made them do it anyway: why us, why now? This draws out your differentiator and their timing trigger.
5. External victory: the metrics now (weight, waist, clothes).
6. Internal victory: their biggest win, what they can do now, and what pain is gone.
numbers: Example before-metrics: 250 lb, 3,000 cal/day, size 45 waist.

### SC-05#27 | DECISION_RULE | topic: hiring-talent | also: finance-exit | fw: IRS 20-Point Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:18
claim: On the IRS 20-point checklist, as the speaker recalls it (with some hesitation), five or more yes answers of 20 (about 25%) means the worker is probably an employee, who should be set up on W-2.
detail: Treating contractors like full-time employees is illegal in the US; other countries have their own employee status rules. As an employee you must withhold their taxes and pay payroll tax, Medicare and similar on top of their pay. Typical tests include whether they must show up at set times, attend regular meetings, wear a uniform and face customers. Going legitimate costs more. That is simply what running a real business costs.
numbers: 5 of 20 IRS criteria (25%) → likely an employee.
applies_to: US businesses.

### SC-05#28 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:04
claim: Everything in business is managing levels of risk, not eliminating it; be by the book whenever you can afford to, but recognize that a compliance cost that would sink the business is the bigger risk.
detail: Ignoring employment rules ("my mentor said don't worry about it") is a choice to carry risk; ask how big that mentor's business is. You can run a completely non-compliant business, but you carry the risk. Conversely, the most compliant way to advertise is never telling anyone you exist, which guarantees you go out of business. So weigh which risk is larger. The recommendation is to go by the book as long as you can afford it.

### SC-05#29 | CASE | topic: hiring-talent | also: finance-exit | fw: IRS 20-Point Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:27:39
claim: Misclassified contractors become a growing liability: one departing worker can trigger an IRS look that brings back taxes, penalties and interest across everyone.
detail: A year from now a "contractor" leaves, gets a tax bill, and claims they were an employee. If they meet five checklist points (fixed hours, regular meetings, uniform, customer-facing), they win. The IRS then collects the unpaid taxes with interest and penalties, and after finding one case it examines everything. With 10 people over two years, that is two years of back taxes across all of them. Liability grows with size, time and turnover. Hormozi's advice: relax, do not be a jerk, and move everyone onto W-2 (or replace those who will not switch), even though withholding lowers their gross pay and costs you more.
numbers: Illustration: 10 workers × 2 years of back taxes plus penalties and interest.

### SC-05#30 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:28:28
claim: New owners should pay taxes quarterly, with the amount set by a trustworthy CPA, so they never spend money that belongs to the IRS.
detail: New owners often forget they will owe tax and get hit with a large bill they cannot pay. You could pay annually, but without a CFO, controller or even an accountant it is safer to pay quarterly, especially if you tend to spend whatever is in the account. It also builds financial discipline. The caveat is a CPA who is not "sketchy". Also set up QuickBooks or an equivalent to track the money.
applies_to: Early-stage US businesses without finance staff.

### SC-05#32 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:30:41
claim: Before you have a P&L, check the business bank account every day; it builds a feel for the flow of money and makes you feel every expense.
