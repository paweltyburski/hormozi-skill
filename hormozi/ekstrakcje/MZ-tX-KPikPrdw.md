### MZ-tX-KPikPrdw#01 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: pricing, unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:00
claim: An Australian baby-health brand (about $3M, wholesale the majority, plus Amazon and D2C) wanted $5M but its margin had squeezed to 7%.
detail: About 1,000 stockists; pharmacies want 10% of what they sell returned as advertising, so cost rises with sales. Retail price had risen from 79 to 109 over years, but pharmacies undercut at 79 for a 2% margin, and Australian law only permits recommended retail pricing. Diagnosis: pricing and cost, not the team.
numbers: Revenue just over $3M; target $5M; margin 7%; ~1,000 stockists; 10% advertising give-back; RRP 79 to 109.

### MZ-tX-KPikPrdw#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:08
claim: With a thin margin, test a price rise by worst-case math: a 10% increase more than doubles profit, so even losing half the volume leaves you ahead.
detail: At 7% margin, 10% more price lifts profit from 7 to about 17 points of revenue. Losing discounting retailers also restores price integrity and control of D2C margin. He recommends boldness either way.
numbers: 10% price rise = more than 2x profit; worst case half the business lost still beats today's profit (his statement).

### MZ-tX-KPikPrdw#03 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:06:34
claim: Selling through a mass distributor costs you control of price; the owner dropped the largest pharmacy distributor to regain it.
detail: In three weeks they regained control of rogue distributors and pulled the product from the main pharmacy distributor, giving up thousands of units. Rationale: control of wholesale price allows D2C price increases.
