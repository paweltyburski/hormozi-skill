### POD-200710-01#01 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: money-models | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:49
claim: After speaking at a marketing event with nothing to sell, one gym owner paid $5,000 on faith, which led to the first paid gym launch.
detail: He told the owner he did not yet know what he would deliver but promised more than $5,000 of value. The owner was opening a gym, so he launched it: 191 sales at $600 each over 19 days, roughly $120,000. Seeing the speed of that result, he began offering to fill gyms for free in exchange for 100% of the upfront revenue.
numbers: Deposit $5,000; 191 sales at $600 in 19 days; about $120,000 earned.

### POD-200710-01#02 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: unit-economics, scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:36
claim: His done-for-you gym-launch model reached $300,000-$400,000 a month but had thin margins, high refunds and sales-headcount logistics that made it unscalable.
detail: Costs included flights, hotels, cars and meals for eight reps. Clients jumped from about 70 to about 270 members in 21 days and operations broke; two owners told members to refund in the middle of class, causing about $100,000 of refunds in a week. He needed owners able to take 100 or more new customers (about $50-$60k) to cover costs. Reps and gym launches had to match one to one or a rep sat idle.
numbers: Revenue $300k-$400k/month; refunds about $100,000 in a week; minimum viable intake 100+ members, about $50,000-$60,000 per launch.

### POD-200710-01#03 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: offers, money-models | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:14:12
claim: He raised the price of the training package through the day as each owner said yes: $6,000, then $8,000, $10,000 and $12,000.
detail: He kept raising the number after each yes and ended at 12 by the end of the first day; the next hundred sold at $12,000, then 2,900 buyers at $16,000. He cites Dan Kennedy: price is what you can say without cracking a smile. First month revenue $180,000; $10M in the first 10 months; the lowest-performing early student made $30,000 in 30 days, which led students to ask what else he offered.
numbers: $6,000 to $12,000 in a day; next 100 at $12,000; next 2,900 at $16,000; $180,000 first month; $10M in first 10 months; weakest early student $30,000 in 30 days.

### POD-200710-01#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:33:51
claim: Getting leads to show rests on four pillars: volume of outreach, personalisation, speed (two parts) and availability.
detail: Speed means time from opt-in to first contact and time to answer each question before the appointment, since slow answers signal poor service and cut shows. Availability, which his data team found to be the single strongest predictor of throughput, means having open slots to meet prospects. When he worked leads himself at his first gym he got about three times as many people in as one of his employees did.
numbers: Self-worked leads gave about 3x the showing of one employee.
### POD-200710-01#05 | BENCHMARK | topic: sales-process | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:34:38
claim: Lead-to-show rate differed 10x between his lowest and highest performers (5% against 45-50%), versus about a 2x differential from improving closing skill.
detail: He says he can double someone's close rate from 30% to 60%, which takes time and effort, but the show gap was a 10x differential in throughput. That is why he built software for lead nurture.
numbers: Show rate 5% lowest versus 45%-50% highest; close rate improvement example 30% to 60%.
### POD-200710-01#06 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: sales-process | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:35:23
claim: Test availability with arithmetic: do the closing hours on your calendar actually reach the member goal?
detail: Example: two hours on three days is six hours a week, only 6 of about 160 hours; with one in three closing, that is about two closes a week, which cannot produce 100 more members in 90 days. He compares it to a shop open only two morning hours.
numbers: 6 selling hours of about 160 per week; close rate one in three; goal 100 members in 90 days.

### POD-200710-01#07 | BENCHMARK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: outreach | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:37:44
claim: Texts are opened virtually across the board (98%+) while a new opt-in email opens about 30-40% depending on the offer and a list about 10%, so he says text gets roughly three times as many people to see the nurturing.
detail: He says replies are also much more likely by SMS. He doesn't email anymore, though he says email still works. Opt-in needs a disclaimer checkbox for pre-recorded calls and SMS; otherwise he says you get fined $150 in SMS.
numbers: SMS open 98%+; new opt-in 30%-40% depending on offer; list about 10%; fine $150 in SMS.
### POD-200710-01#08 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: sales-process | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:40:05
claim: Manual labour to work leads can cost $99-$150, more than the $10 lead itself.
detail: If one in five leads walks in, a $10 lead means $50 of ad spend per show but $150 of labour. His software gets on average twice as many leads to show, and the top 20% of users get three to four times the average. It took about two years to build, and it sent 1.5 million texts in the last eight weeks or so.
numbers: Lead cost $10; labour $99-$150 to work leads; 1 in 5 show; average twice as many shows, top 20% 3x-4x the average.
### POD-200710-01#09 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: lead-getters | fw: Implied Guarantee | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:41:59
claim: His done-for-you acquisition offer charged only for each person who walks in or books a call, not a retainer.
detail: Pitch logic: if a client is worth $500, would they pay the ad cost plus about $40-$50 per appointment, at roughly one third of an in-house salary and without the hiring and HR load. He had not offered any done-for-you service for four years before starting this eight weeks earlier.
numbers: Customer value example $500; fee added about $40-$50 per appointment.

### POD-200710-01#10 | TACTIC | topic: scaling-constraints | also: product-delivery, management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:28
claim: He beta tests each new play with at least 20 representative gyms, including bottom performers, and expects most tests to show no gain.
detail: Take winners and the bottom 30% of clients, since tests on only top performers are unrepresentative. Deliver the training exactly as it would ship to everyone (no extra one-on-one coaching), then track results at 30 days. He runs about two big conceptual tests a month and says two out of three show no market improvement, and split testing button colours usually changes nothing, so choosing the right lever matters.
numbers: Beta cohort at least 20 gyms, bottom 30% included; tracking after 30 days; about 2 tests/month; roughly 2 in 3 fail.

### POD-200710-01#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:49
claim: A market leader's best defence against copiers is to copy first: buy newcomers' courses, take what is good and distribute it, and keep content fresh.
detail: Information is high margin but loses value fast, so he released new plays every 14 days while competitors did quarterly, mostly recycled material. Ego makes former leaders refuse good ideas from others. His top tier was built around best-practice items sorted by lead generation, nurture, sales, fulfilment and ascension, then matched to who struggles with each.
numbers: New plays every 14 days versus quarterly for rivals.

### POD-200710-01#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:33
claim: Industry-specific masterminds yield tactics you can use tomorrow, while cross-industry ones yield more creative ideas.
detail: He gets more ideas from the general group and more immediate changes from the specific one. Returns depend on intent: he cites a rule of thumb from another speaker that only about 10% to 20% of attendees are real executors, while many come to talk candidly or for friends.
numbers: Real executors in a mastermind about 10%-20% (second-hand figure).

### POD-200710-01#13 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit, verticals | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:26:48
claim: Do not franchise until one location is profitable and you know lifetime value, acquisition cost, churn, page conversion and upsell levels.
detail: Spend about a year squeezing the model first; a struggling single gym owner wanting to franchise is a red flag. The model should differ in avatar, monetisation or acquisition, since acquisition is easy to copy. Acquisition-only franchisors who just learned ads often lack culture, leadership and back ends, so revenue per customer is too low. Once franchising, you become a coach to your owners.
applies_to: Prospective franchisors, especially in fitness.

### POD-200710-01#14 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:27:33
claim: Expect franchisees to execute at about 35% of your own level, and blaming them stops you from fixing the system.
detail: Instead ask what conditions you created that tolerated the poor result, how to improve training, onboarding and screening, and how to raise qualifications. Franchisor outcomes depend on unit production. Selling your first ten units to friends is easy, which is why many franchises stall at 10 to 20 locations when the model does not work.
numbers: Franchisee execution about 35%; typical stall at 10-20 locations.
