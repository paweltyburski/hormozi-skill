### POD-0372#01 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:00
claim: In the prior year he sold majority stakes in three portfolio companies, including two at a $46.2M valuation, to concentrate on one active business.
detail: One sale was a 75% stake in another company, another a 66% stake (the Gym Launch stake among them). He had been broadly against selling. The deciding reason was focus: acquisition.com, a company that builds businesses and takes minority stakes, became his single active business and the others were handed to an investment partner who would prioritise them.
numbers: $46.2M valuation on two stakes; 75% and 66% stakes sold; acquisition.com portfolio around $85M a year.

### POD-0372#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:34
claim: A sale lowers after-tax net worth, even though it lowers risk, because equity compounds untaxed and a sale triggers costs.
detail: Held equity has a market value and grows tax-free; liquidating adds transaction costs, taxes and payouts such as bonuses. So selling to "increase net worth" is a false belief; the real benefit has to be something else, such as risk reduction or freed attention.

### POD-0372#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:55
claim: Being out of the operation is what makes a company sellable; if the buyer thinks you are needed to run it, you will probably get some earn-out, seller financing and continued transition services.
detail: He had been removed from day-to-day for about a year before selling, was bored, and so none of the three sales carried an earn-out, seller financing or transition obligations.
applies_to: owners preparing a company for sale
### POD-0372#04 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:06
claim: Test whether a sale is needed by asking whether you can recreate the post-sale conditions while still owning 100%.
detail: He listed reasons for selling (legitimacy, a break from the gym-industry label, net worth, headspace) and checked each: legitimacy he already had, net worth would fall, and conditions could be matched by stepping out of operations.
### POD-0372#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:39
claim: Owner and CEO are different jobs, and unsold companies you still steer occupy attention even when your calendar is empty.
detail: Doing research, reading data and setting strategy is the CEO's work; a true owner, like a stock holder, does none of it. He compares remaining responsibility to a child who has left home: it still takes shower, walking and thinking time. His "one active business" belief is that a person can be actively running only one at a time.

### POD-0372#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:20:59
claim: In his own decision to sell, Hormozi reasoned from the opportunity cost of his headspace: being even 10% less effective at building something 100 times as big would outweigh the total value of the companies he sold.
detail: He argued that even being 10% less effective at building something 100 times larger would cost more than the total value of the companies sold. Framing: he felt he was buying back time with equity, and the cash was one of the least important variables. Ask: in five years, would I build bigger or smaller if I keep these?
numbers: 10% less effective on a business 100x bigger.
### POD-0372#07 | EVOLUTION | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: updates | ts: 00:08:32
claim: He still believes in "rich people sell, wealthy people never sell", but says he may sell minority equity positions in the future.
detail: He would not sell majority or decision-making power in acquisition.com because it is so core to who he is. Counsel from Grant Cardone was that rich people sell only when the party is over or interest has gone; his case was a greater interest in something else. Other high earners told him they wished they had kept the companies they sold.
### POD-0372#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:09:41
claim: The money from the sale made no felt difference, but losing the business was felt, especially where the founder's face was on the brand.
detail: He and his wife had already taken a large amount in dividends, so the proceeds did not change his life. Selling Gym Launch was emotionally harder than selling the other two because his thumbprint and face were on it, despite zero involvement. The strongest confirmation of the choice was the energy he felt building anew a week later.
numbers: About $45-$50M of dividends already taken from these companies before the sale (as stated).

### POD-0372#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:07
claim: His three hardest decisions each meant giving up something that confers status for something unproven, with risk shrinking each time.
detail: First, quitting a consulting job to open a gym; second, selling six gyms at age 26 to start Gym Launch; third, selling the portfolio stakes. Each had lower risk than the last because he had more skills, teams and prior businesses. Approval from parents or peers loomed large before and looked trivial after.
numbers: Six gym locations sold at age 26.

### POD-0372#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:22:42
claim: Make decisions from a position of needing nothing; a poor owner decides poorly.
detail: He says some owners run businesses without taking money out while growing; he never believed in that. Having cash lets decisions be made from long-term judgment, and he also noted that the sale itself brought more recognition than the pre-sale results did, despite the lower net worth.
