### LC-13#01 | CASE | topic: lead-getters | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:46
claim: Unable to afford $5–10K/month agency retainers, Hormozi paid a solo agency owner $750/hour to coach him while he ran his own ads, and it became one of his highest-return investments.
detail: He could run local gym ads but not national campaigns (retargeting, pixels). The agency owner said he did not sell his time; Hormozi asked what it would take. Terms: the expert watches over his shoulder, Hormozi holds the mouse, and every change comes with the reasoning — why this, why not that. Weekly calls for eight weeks, recorded, with notes and homework. By week six he knew everything the expert did; he kept two more weeks to be sure, then stopped. The skill later made him millions.
numbers: Agency quotes $5K, $7K, $10K/month; tutor rate $750/hour × 8 weekly sessions ≈ $6K total; independent by week 6.

### LC-13#02 | DEFINITION | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:10
claim: Agencies are service businesses that generate leads for you: you pay them to run ads, conduct outreach, or package and distribute your content.
detail: After referrals and employees, they are the third lead getter; they carry out the Core Four on your behalf so you personally do less.

### LC-13#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:10
claim: The standard done-for-you agency relationship tends to decay: strong start with a senior rep, then a junior rep, falling results, cancellation, and a new agency.
detail: Typical cycle: onboarding teaches you a little; the best person is put on your account and you get results; he is moved to a newer account and a junior takes over; results drop; you ask for the senior back, who fixes a bit while spread across accounts; eventually you cancel and repeat the ride with another agency. After his good first experience, most of his later traditional engagements went this way.

### LC-13#04 | DECISION_RULE | topic: lead-getters | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:45
claim: If you have no money, do the lead getting yourself; if you have money, an agency is viable — but used as a teacher, not a permanent outsourced department.
detail: This advice targets small and mid-sized owners, for whom $10K–100K on an agency is significant. Large companies using big ad agencies, and owners who never want to learn the skill, can reasonably use the traditional hands-off model. Hormozi says he is not anti-agency (he owns equity in agency software, ALAN); he prefers this model because he always wants to learn.

### LC-13#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: lead-getters | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: More Better New | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:49
claim: Use an agency when you want to add something new — a new placement, platform or method — because that is when your own risk of failing is highest.
detail: Agencies already know the basics you lack and can contextualize them to your business, so your learning curve shortens. They are motivated to show results fast, which beats your own trial and error. For him the time saved is worth the cost.

### LC-13#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:52
claim: Agencies can make good teachers because their business depends on doing what works right now, unlike many educators who have not done the work in a long time.
detail: Many educators have not done the work in years. Agencies are current practitioners with enough clients asking them to perform that they can transfer the skill; what they lack is a teaching structure, which the learning agreement supplies.

### LC-13#07 | SCRIPT | topic: lead-getters | also: hiring-talent | fw: Agency Learning Agreement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:52
claim: The agency pitch Hormozi uses: hire them for six months to do the work and teach it, pay extra for explanations, then move to a cheaper consulting retainer once your team can do it.
detail: Sequence of moves: (1) say you want what they do in your business but do not know how; (2) propose working together about six months so you learn how they do it; (3) offer to pay extra for them to break down why they decide what they decide and the steps they take; (4) explain you will train your team once you understand it; (5) when the team is good enough, switch to a lower-cost consulting agreement so they can still help with problems; (6) close with "are you opposed to this?" Clear expectations up front plus extra pay for the inconvenience gets many yeses; if one refuses, another accepts. He has used this with almost every vendor since.

### LC-13#08 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: management-ops | fw: Agency Learning Agreement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:57
claim: Set an internal deadline (e.g. off the agency by month six) and accept a period of paying both your team and the agency.
detail: The deadline is mainly for your own team — without it they get lazy and dependent. Working alongside the agency gets results sooner, and by the time the senior rep rotates off, your team can hold the results instead of watching them plummet. The cost of long-term ignorance of a new platform or method outweighs the short-term double pay. Negotiating these terms is normal; if someone will not, ask the next agency.

### LC-13#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Agency Selection Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:00
claim: Hormozi's checklist for picking an agency has ten signals.
detail: (1) Someone you know got good results with them (word of mouth — ask your network "who is great at Pinterest/Hulu ads?"). (2) Prominent companies you recognize got results. (3) A waiting list. (4) A clear sales process that sets realistic expectations. (5) No short-term hacks — long-term strategy and clear timelines for setup, scaling and results. (6) They state exactly what they need from you, when, and how they will use it. (7) They propose a regular meeting cadence and several ways to update you. (8) Updates in simple terms with clear cost-versus-results tracking. (9) They make a good offer by the value equation. (10) They are expensive — in his experience all good agencies are, but not all expensive ones are good.

### LC-13#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: product-delivery | fw: Agency Selection Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:41
claim: A waiting list is a probable sign of a good agency: when demand exceeds supply they are probably pretty good, and the best ones grow by word of mouth, usually constrained by operational scale.
detail: Small outfits running lots of ads for themselves are, in his experience, usually not very good.

### LC-13#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: management-ops | fw: Agency Selection Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:26
claim: Agencies are operations machines, so look for evidence of a tight ship in how they sell and onboard you.
detail: Good signs mirror the referral checklist: clear onboarding, clear qualifications for which customers they take, a reasonable dream outcome, fast early results, good communication, and telling you what they want you to buy next. An agency that says "this is what we can confidently commit to" and then meets it is run by a real operator. Bad operators at the start mean bad operators at the end. Silence is a red flag: "most clients don't want to talk to us" is not acceptable — you would meet anyone running marketing inside your business.

### LC-13#12 | EXAMPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: content-brand | fw: Agency Selection Checklist | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:11
claim: Make sure what the agency optimizes for matches what you want — a content agency chasing viral videos is not building your brand.
detail: Some agencies obsess over beating algorithms. Hormozi optimizes for the strongest brand, not views, so a pitch built on "we'll get you viral videos" is misaligned. If you only want eyeballs, that agency may fit.

### LC-13#13 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: lead-getters | also: offers | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:04
claim: Judge an agency's offer with the value equation questions before signing.
detail: Dream outcome — do they promise what I actually want? Perceived likelihood — how many people like me have they gotten it for? Time delay — how long will it take? Effort and sacrifice — what do they require of me, what will I give up, and can I sustain that long enough to see results? If they demand inputs you cannot provide, do not work with them; both sides will end up upset.

### LC-13#14 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:54
claim: Talk to five to ten agencies before buying, and keep comparing even after one agrees to your terms.
detail: Early calls teach you a lot; over time the gap between good and bad agencies becomes obvious, making you an informed customer. Compare candidates against the checklist and pick the best fit — none will be perfect, but you will be better off, faster, than doing nothing.
numbers: 5–10 agency conversations before deciding.

### LC-13#15 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:54
claim: When you like an agency that does not offer what you need, ask them who is as good as them at that specialty.
detail: Good agencies know other good agencies; these referrals have produced some of his best agency contacts.

### LC-13#16 | FRAMEWORK | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Agency Learning Agreement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:47
claim: Six steps for using agencies: decide, shop widely, use the learning agreement, set a deadline, run both teams, then step down to consulting and cut loose.
detail: (1) Decide whether an agency makes sense now. (2) Talk to many agencies to feel out the market; do not be cheap. (3) Use the learn-then-consult agreement. (4) Set a clear deadline that forces you and your team to learn. (5) Run both teams until your team regularly beats theirs. (6) Switch to discounted consulting until you feel you are teaching them; once you have surpassed them, end it. The logic: trade money to pay down your "ignorance tax" as fast as possible so you can enter new platforms, media types and methods sooner.
