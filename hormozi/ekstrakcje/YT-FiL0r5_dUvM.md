### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:45
claim: Harvard's brand rests on selectivity, no outcome promises, and not everyone passing, which most education businesses lack.
detail: Few applicants are admitted, a price is stated without a result guarantee, and students can fail. It is also in person, and the peer network is its most valuable offer on top of the brand. A business that admits anyone with a card cannot build the same brand. Their customers also leave after four years, a far longer horizon than a six-week programme.
applies_to: Education, coaching and course businesses.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv | fw: Continuity Offers | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:21
claim: Education sellers mistake a payment plan for continuity.
detail: A 12,000 programme with one year of access paid at 1,000 a month is not recurring revenue: people finish and graduate. That is a payment plan, not retention. Real continuity in education needs new courses or a curriculum each quarter.
numbers: Example 12,000 over 12 monthly payments of 1,000.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: product-delivery, retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:42
claim: Split your deliverables into column A (one-time value that expires once learned) and column B (consumables people need again and again).
detail: Teaching a skill is column A; the learner no longer needs the teacher afterwards, as with a university course taken once. Column B must be something that keeps producing value each month. He says education alone does not lead to continuity, which is why he looks for consumables. Most of Gym Launch's enterprise value, he says, did not come from education.
applies_to: Education businesses wanting recurring revenue.
### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: money-models | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:41
claim: Consumables are things the creator builds once and licenses repeatedly: tested ads, trending-product lists, property lists, communities.
detail: Gym Launch tests ads in representative markets with its own money, then sells the winners to thousands of locations at near-100% gross margin; new ads are needed monthly. A 3D-printing community teaches setup once, then posts a monthly hot list of trending products. A real-estate wholesaling community shares a monthly list of properties found by scouts. A community is itself consumable because of network effects, as are agency and SEO services.
numbers: Near 100% gross margin on resold ads after the initial test cost.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Big Head Long Tail | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:19
claim: Price the one-time value as high as the market allows, and price the consumable at the level people would pay if it were sold alone.
detail: The common mistake is to spread the one-time price into a monthly plan, for example 30,000 as 2,500 for 12 months, so people drain the one-time value and quit because the consumable is not worth 2,500. Ask: if I sold only the consumable, what price would people never leave at? That is the continuity price; as a hypothetical he suggests it might be around 100-500 against a 10,000 front end, though he says he doesn't know.
numbers: Example 30,000 split to 2,500 x 12; hypothetical consumable maybe 100-500 a month against 10,000 upfront value.
### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv | fw: Ascension Path | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:29
claim: Higher-level education (master's, PhD, certification levels) is a ladder, with a fraction moving up and prices set to reflect that drop-off.
detail: Harvard-style ascension: perhaps 20% of bachelor's students go on to a master's and about 10% of those to a PhD (illustrative figures). Expect a drop at each level and price accordingly.
numbers: Illustrative: 20% to master's, 10% of those to PhD.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:17
claim: Bolting software onto an education business to raise its value usually fails and distracts.
detail: He has not seen a from-scratch education-to-SaaS exit succeed; white-labelling is just affiliating. Software is valuable because of revenue retention, high gross and incremental margin and low operational drag at scale, not because of code. Software with six-month churn is valued like a service. If you want sellable, make the business about the sticky thing.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:03
claim: Education and media earn more cash today than similar-scale businesses, in exchange for less sellable value later; both are legitimate choices.
detail: A friend took a 70,000 salary for 8-12 years and sold a software firm for 250M; an education business might have paid about 10M a year but never sold. Hormozi's frame: delaying gratification has diminishing returns, so find a middle point (the "three marshmallow problem" of waiting for a third reward).

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: retention-ltv | fw: Continuity Offers | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:24
claim: The one education model with real continuity is continued education: recertifying people on new skills over a whole career.
detail: Where a profession must stay current, a membership that recertifies can compound and sell near a tech multiple, if you can show people stay for years and a brand proves future demand. Entry then requires a prerequisite, so finding such prospects is the new problem, and each level loses people.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers | fw: Trim and Stack | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:14
claim: His own rule of thumb: he wants each feature to be valuable enough on its own to more than justify three or four times the price.
detail: Calls, accountability and Q&A libraries are standard in education stacks and none solved churn. He would add as few features as possible and likely can delete some; the stack may be three or four things, two, or even one done well. He would add a service when people consume it again and again, such as repeated questions month after month, and then the service business itself will be more valuable than the education one if done well.
numbers: Feature value more than 3-4x price (his way of thinking).
### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#11 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Cohort Analysis | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:47
claim: Volatile customer groups (very small businesses, prosumers) churn however good the product is, so compare against realistic churn for the segment.
detail: Their passion and cash swing in and out, and the volatility passes to you. Facebook has high churn but better than other social platforms. Shopify is said to renew about 55-60% annually, yet those who pass a year stay long. Aim for a milestone after which nobody leaves; year one is a sales filter for diamonds.
numbers: Shopify renewal about 55-60% a year (as stated, unclear in audio).

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#12 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: Revenue Churn | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:20
claim: Average monthly churn of school communities is about 18%; 10% is about half of that, 5% means a 20-month average life.
detail: Lifespan equals one divided by monthly churn, so 10% gives 10 months and 1% gives 100. Track retention at month 12 and churn after it. High-end enterprise should renew at 80% a year or better; beginner markets churn more, and that comes with the segment.
numbers: 18% monthly average (school); 5% monthly = 20 months; enterprise 80%+ annual renewal.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#13 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery | fw: Cost to Acquire a Permanent Customer | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:21:07
claim: Measure the cost of a permanent customer: if ten education buyers give three service buyers and one never leaves, acquisition cost is tenfold.
detail: Compare permanent LTV to permanent CAC, not front-end numbers. The hard part is the feature stack and customer type that makes people stay at 12 months; after that it is math. Improve selection up front or activation so it is one in four instead of one in five.
numbers: 10 buyers to 3 service customers to 1 permanent; one-in-five improved to one-in-four.

### YT-FiL0r5_dUvM#14 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv | fw: Ascension Path | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:22:39
claim: Sell the next layer after the first problem is solved, because offer timing matters as much as the offer.
detail: Example: after teaching DM appointment-setting, the next need is people to do it for them: a placement service, a shared pool of setters, or employed setters at about 20% above cost and pre-trained. Selling that layer too early often fails.
numbers: Employed setters at 20% markup.
