### POD-0938#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing | fw: 80/20 Customer Analysis | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:15
claim: The 80/20 split nests: inside the 20% of customers who give 80% of profit, the same skew repeats.
detail: Applying it twice means a very small top slice of customers produces most of the aggregate profit, so a model must allow some customers to spend far more.
numbers: 64% of aggregate profit comes from the top slice inside the top 20% (about 4% of customers, implied).

### POD-0938#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:30
claim: Expect 99 of 100 prospects to decline the expensive product; keep it available for the rare large buyer.
detail: If your model allows it, expect most people (99 of 100) to say no to the expensive products and services. When the big buyer appears, the top tier is what lets you capture it.
### POD-0938#04 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Anchor Upsell | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:08:38
claim: Build from the top down: launch the expensive, scarce version first and move down-market later with a story that frames the cheaper product as access.
detail: Tesla's path (a $250,000 Roadster in tiny volume, then cheaper models) is the example. Anchoring high helps the brand narrative, and early margin per unit is much larger. The first product you sell need not be your base tier.

### POD-0938#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:31
claim: Pricing tiers that differ by $10-$40 give top-10% buyers no way to spend more; use up to four tiers with a real spread.
detail: Three tiers at $100, $129 and $139 per month all look alike to a rich buyer. In his illustration, adding one high tier moved profit from 10 to 90 (index, units unclear in audio). You need not serve everyone with every tier.

### POD-0938#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:24:18
claim: Think in absolute profit, not margin or unit volume: a few large sales can equal many small ones.
detail: One buyer paying $10,000 for something costing $2,000 is compared with 400 buyers paying $50 for something costing $25. As stated the figures are only roughly equal ($8,000 vs $10,000 profit). The point is the work and conversations needed to reach the same profit.
numbers: $8,000 profit on one $10,000 sale; 400 sales at $25 profit each = $10,000.

### POD-0938#07 | BENCHMARK | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:30:32
claim: Rules of thumb for starting price points by buyer type (services, not assets).
detail: Consumer impulse buy versus higher-ticket service, and for small businesses a cheaper and a mid-tier monthly price. Large companies can pay far more. Where to start: if you currently make nothing at zero, think what you would deliver for a price and start there.
numbers: Consumer impulse $500-$600; consumer higher ticket usually $3,000-$10,000; small business cheap about $400-$800 per month (roughly $500), mid probably $2,000-$3,000 per month.
applies_to: Service businesses choosing a first price
### POD-0938#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: objections-closing | fw: Price to Value Discrepancy | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:32:20
claim: Wealthy buyers judge the ratio of return to cost; less wealthy ones react to the absolute price.
detail: Told a $20,000 price, a rich buyer asks "for what?", while the other calls it expensive. The same sum for a share worth $800,000 or a new Lamborghini would be a bargain. Founder admits he had an emotional reaction to zeros until he saw this.

### POD-0938#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: sales-process | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:36:30
claim: A low price tells large buyers the seller cannot handle their size, so price and marketing filter who talks to you.
detail: He rules out any B2B vendor selling at $1,500 per month because it signals the vendor cannot serve a company his size, and prefers vendors at $20,000-$50,000 per month. Flash sales and discounts tell wealthy buyers the offer is not for them. Decide where to draw the line on customers.
numbers: $1,500 per month rejected as too small; $20,000-$50,000 per month trusted.

### POD-0938#10 | CASE | topic: paid-ads | also: unit-economics | fw: Lead Scoring | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:38:44
claim: In a book-launch ad test, leads optimized for purchase cost more but were worth far more than leads optimized for volume.
detail: Two campaigns: one optimized for lead volume, one for purchases. After the campaign, value per lead was measured. Higher cost per lead, much higher return per dollar. Cost per unit rises when serving richer buyers, but not proportionally to what they pay.
numbers: Volume leads: $5 each, worth $20 (4x). Purchase-optimized leads: $17 each, worth $189 (about 11x). Cost 3.5x higher for about 9.5x the value.

### POD-0938#11 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: paid-ads | fw: Lead Scoring | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:41:32
claim: Qualify and rank leads by wealth before the first call, for example by home value via a property-listing lookup.
detail: A home service company serves only homes above a set value and checks the address before talking. One can also run an API lookup on lead addresses and prioritise higher values. For B2C, find which household profile (age, family, zip code) forms the best customer set.
numbers: Example threshold: homes over $1 million.
applies_to: Home services and local B2C

### POD-0938#12 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:42:18
claim: Find your best customers in CRM, interview them about why they bought, and use their answers as front-end marketing.
detail: Offer ways to spend more so the data shows who does. Do not judge pricing by people who can never pay. Different buyers value different things: low-end buyers mostly want the cheapest. Delay the bottom 50% until you can serve them with automation.
