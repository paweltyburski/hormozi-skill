### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:46
claim: Wealth can be reached by four routes of rising leverage: V1 pure income, V2 income plus passive index investing, V3 income plus selling an owned business, V4 income plus borrowing against assets.
detail: He built this as his understanding of money improved, first imagining he would need to earn about $1.5B pre-tax to keep $1B. He says V2 feels easier than V1 because it combines income with public-equity investing. He uses a $100M/year business as the big example and then translates each route to a $10M goal.
numbers: $1.5B income to keep $1B in V1; about $67M a year of post-tax income from a $100M/year business, invested at 9%, gives about $1B after a decade in V2.
### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#02 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:00
claim: A $10M net worth is his reference goal because 4-5% a year on it supports a top-1% lifestyle.
detail: At 5% it yields about $500,000 a year in the US, and passive distributions can be taxed as qualified dividends at about 20% instead of income tax rates. He says the figure can be raised as inflation moves.
numbers: $10M at 4-5% gives about $400-500k a year; qualified dividends taxed about 20%.

### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#03 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:51
claim: Investing skill over ten years can be judged by comparing the final portfolio with what the income would have been before tax.
detail: Someone earning $1M a year at a 37% tax rate keeps about $670,000; invested at 9% for ten years that reaches roughly $10M, which equals ten years of untaxed income. So an investor has merely recovered the tax. Early years matter much more than late years because of compounding. He ignores living costs for simplicity. Elsewhere he says "67% tax rate" for the $15M-to-$10M example, which is inconsistent with 37% here.
numbers: $1M/year, 37% tax, $670k/year invested at 9% gives about $10M in 10 years.
### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:31
claim: Selling a company leaves a check that must be redeployed, so compare future returns to what the business itself earned.
detail: Everyone dreams of the check; nobody mentions that you then have to put it back into assets, and the question is whether you will beat the return of the business you just sold. Thinking one step ahead about what replaces the income can change whether to sell.

### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#05 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:39
claim: Real service businesses with owner earnings sell at roughly 10-14x, so a $10M-revenue business paying $1.2M a year can yield about $12M.
detail: He says HVAC companies are selling at 10, 12 and 14x, and also mentions dental offices and accounting firms as real businesses that can be sold in the right market, if the business truly is an asset. Capital gains tax then reduces the proceeds (his example nets about $10M from $12M).
numbers: HVAC multiples 10-14x owner earnings; $1.2M owner earnings at 10x gives $12M pre-tax, about $10M after tax.
### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#06 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:04:40
claim: An ESOP lets an owner sell a 33-49% minority stake to employees through bank financing, tax-free in the US, while keeping control.
detail: The bank lends to the employees, who pay the owner using the stock as collateral. The multiple is lower than a private-equity buyer would pay because banks price risk, not upside. Benefits: owner keeps the majority, gets liquidity, and a team with a stake tends to act like owners.
numbers: Stake sold 33-49%.

### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#07 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:06:57
claim: A dividend recap borrows against the business at roughly four to five times annual EBITDA, with the company, not the owner, carrying the debt.
detail: The owner takes the loan proceeds personally, the business repays over years, and the owner still owns the company, like paying off a house. Sensible mainly for a growing business with reliable cash flow. He is unsure whether the proceeds are tax-free.
numbers: Loan size about 4-5x EBITDA; $100M EBITDA could support about $500M.
applies_to: Growing, profitable companies with stable earnings.

### YT-6x3re5-Ms1E#08 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:07:49
claim: Going public lets an owner sell only 5-10% and then borrow against the remaining shares quickly and piecemeal, with such loans not taxed as income.
detail: Banks lend up to about 50% of the stock value, in chunks over 24 hours instead of a months-long process. Loans must be repaid and carry covenants, and the plan only works while the business keeps growing; if it shrinks the owner can lose everything. He sees the common claim about rich people borrowing against stock as ignoring this risk.
numbers: Float 5-10% of shares; loan up to about 50% of stock value (his arithmetic on a $1B stake is garbled, unclear in audio).
