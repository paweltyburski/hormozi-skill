### YT-QGcjweehrvU#01 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:53
claim: Estimate average customer lifespan from the share of a starting cohort that is gone 30 days later, then invert it.
detail: Take only the customers you had at the start of the month and count how many of those remain at the end; ignore anyone sold in between. The lost share is churn, and one divided by churn is the average lifespan in whatever interval you measure. Works with quarterly or yearly intervals the same way. Meant for small firms without a CRM that reports lifetime.
numbers: 100 customers at start, 90 of them left after 30 days = 10% churn = 1 / 0.10 = 10 months average lifespan.
applies_to: Smaller businesses with no CRM reporting lifetime

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: verticals | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:02:25
claim: A staffed-service gross profit calculation: staff cost per head divided over the customers one person can serve.
detail: Subtract the delivery cost (the employee) from the billed revenue of the customers that employee handles. Divide by customers to get gross profit per customer per month, then multiply by average lifespan to get lifetime gross profit. This is the number that has to pay for acquisition and everything else in the business.
numbers: $1,000/month customers, 10 per employee = $10,000 billed; employee costs $4,000/month; gross profit $6,000/month = 60% margin = $600 per customer per month; at 10 months lifespan, LTGP = $6,000 per customer.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#03 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:10
claim: Judging ad spend against lifetime revenue instead of lifetime gross profit can hide a business that earns almost nothing per customer.
detail: Pen example: sell at $10, cost $9, average buyer takes 10 pens. Lifetime revenue is $100 and the team cheers at spending $50 to win it, but lifetime gross profit is only $10, so $50 buys $10. He explains the confusion as inherited from Silicon Valley software, where gross margin is near 100% so revenue and gross profit are interchangeable; firms with people, inventory or manufacturing cannot treat them as equal.
numbers: Price $10, cost $9, 10 units = $100 revenue, $10 gross profit, $50 spent to acquire.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:06:50
claim: Worked CAC that includes labour as well as ad and software spend, giving a 5:1 ratio.
detail: Sum the monthly cost of the acquisition team and the ad and software spend, then divide by customers acquired in the same 30 days. Compare with the LTGP from the previous example. He says anything above 3:1 is fine and 5:1 is great.
numbers: 5 employees $50,000/month + $50,000 ads and software = $100,000 / 83 customers = $1,204 CAC; against $6,000 LTGP = about 5:1.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:27
claim: When evaluating a business to buy or invest in, a poor LTGP:CAC means he can toss the business out, while an exceptional one might make him willing to forgive some weaker metrics.
detail: Below the 3:1 floor he would not need to look at anything else and could automatically toss the business, because it is too constrained to scale. Exceptional ratios mean he might be willing to overlook other metrics that are not as strong. If you don't know these figures for a business you are buying, investing in or own, find them out.
### YT-QGcjweehrvU#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:08:27
claim: Return on invested capital for a repeatable unit such as a new location, tested after customer-level LTGP:CAC is already sound.
detail: Put every cost of opening one unit into a single figure (launch marketing, build-out, staff hiring, recruiter and job-ad costs). Then ask three things: how fast the money is paid back, what the one-year return is, and what profit it throws off at capacity. He wants at least 3:1 annual profit at capacity on the invested amount, ideally 5:1, which he justifies because his own time is involved, so a stock-market style 10% return is not enough for active work.
numbers: $100,000 to open a location; at capacity $500,000/year profit = 5:1; minimum asked 3:1 (a 300% yearly return). Example path: break even at month 6, pacing $250,000/year at month 12, $500,000/year in year two.
applies_to: Brick-and-mortar chains and other repeatable units

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:57
claim: Payback on a new location depends on the size of the build-out and on whether the chain has a launch strategy for a market where nobody knows it.
detail: A chain that is strong in one city and can drive to all its sites often has no playbook for a new city, and going from five to twenty locations is a different game. His aim is to open full: spend a bigger share of the opening budget on launch so the site reaches capacity as fast as possible.
numbers: Example: of a roughly $10,000 opening budget, put $40,000 (as spoken, figures unclear in audio) into the opening so the site is already at a $500,000/year run rate by month six.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#08 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing | fw: Payback Period | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:47
claim: Four ways to pull cash forward and shorten payback on a recurring-revenue customer.
detail: 1. Collect first and last month at signup. 2. Add an onboarding, enrollment, initiation or setup fee on top of the recurring charge. 3. Add an upsell, which is the same as a fee except that it is optional. 4. Have a third-party financing partner pay the whole amount up front and keep a fee for taking the risk. A business that breaks even on the first transaction is more resilient in a downturn.
numbers: Example with $600/month and CAC of $1,200 (earlier said $600, unclear in audio): break even in month 2; adding a $600 setup fee breaks even at the first payment and is $600 ahead after month 2.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:15:54
claim: Sales velocity times lifetime gross profit (or lifetime value) shows the revenue level a recurring business will settle at if nothing else changes.
detail: Units sold per month multiplied by lifetime value gives the steady-state monthly revenue. If current revenue is below it the business will grow without new effort; if above it, revenue will shrink, usually because delivery slipped and churn rose, or because earlier sales were higher, or a channel was lost. Averages over trailing 90 days, six months and 12 months are more reliable than daily numbers in small, volatile businesses.
numbers: 100 customers a month x $10,000 lifetime value = $1M/month ceiling. A firm at $500,000/month will double; one at $2M/month will fall to $1M.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#10 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:18:35
claim: Sales velocity divided by churn gives the number of customers the business will have to serve at its ceiling, which sets headcount and capacity needs.
detail: Pair it with the revenue ceiling: one tells how much money, the other how many customers must be serviced. If infrastructure is built for more customers than the ceiling, cuts loom; if the company is below the ceiling, plan how to scale without breaking on the way.
numbers: 100 new customers a month at 10% monthly churn = 1,000 customers at the ceiling; at $1,000/month each that equals $1M/month. Churn moving from 5% to 10% halves lifetime revenue; 10% to 5% doubles it.

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#11 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:19:22
claim: Opportunity size equals potential units times lifetime gross profit, divided by risk.
detail: The potential-units term is the reachable market; assume 100% buying as the upper bound. Risk is applied to both parts. For units: reliance on one acquisition method or platform, and key-person dependence. For gross profit: competitors undercutting, strength of position, premium sustainability, economies of scale versus shrinking margins at scale. He treats market trend as a risk lever too (e.g. rising fertility problems as a tailwind).
numbers: Example niche of 50,000 potential units (vegan powerlifting mothers of three, used as a joke example).

### YT-QGcjweehrvU#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:43
claim: Market size is very important but hard to know, so assess the knowable risks instead and expect winners to widen their audience over time.
detail: He borrows the Munger and Buffett split between important and knowable: market size is important but largely unknowable, because most large companies began with a narrow avatar and redefined it later. Amazon started with books and Facebook with college students. So a small niche now does not cap the outcome; focus on the risks you can actually observe.
