### MM-01#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: offers, leads-fundamentals | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:47
claim: The three books answer three sequential questions: what to sell ($100M Offers), how to get strangers to want it ($100M Leads), and how to get paid and paid more (Money Models).
detail: Money Models covers how to get customers to pay, buy more, produce profit, and fund advertising profitably so more people find out. Hormozi wrote it first as his "cookbook for making money", then realized readers needed advertising skills (Leads) and something to advertise (Offers) before they could use it. The course adds examples, math and cut chapters beyond the book.

### MM-01#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:20
claim: Document lessons while you grow, not at the end — details are forgotten, and there are not very many apprenticeships in business to pass them on.
detail: Hormozi frames education as the greatest legacy: otherwise everyone relearns and re-suffers what you did. Complex skills (deal making, surgery) are really bundles of simpler skills that are hard to quantify and often transfer through years of mentorship; his books and trainings try to mimic apprenticeship at scale.

### MM-01#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: money-models | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:25
claim: Most advertising is not profitable, meaning people lose money getting customers: even a 10:1 LTGP:CAC deal is worth taking but causes cash-flow problems if the profit arrives slowly.
detail: The problem the course solves is that people lose money getting customers. Without fast payback you must burn your own savings, take on debt or sell equity, or borrow from a bank to fund the wait.

### MM-01#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:59
claim: A 10:1 LTGP:CAC deal is still a cash-flow problem when the gross profit arrives a year or more later.
detail: $1,000 lifetime gross profit for $100 CAC means $10 back per $1 — always take that trade. But if the $1,000 comes in one payment 12 months later, you are out $100 plus delivery costs all year. If it comes as $100/year ($8.33/month) for 10 years, you are not even back to break-even until month 13. "Door three" is the fix: make more than the $100 CAC in profit within the first 30 days.
numbers: LTGP $1,000, CAC $100 → 10:1; $8.33/month payments → break-even month 13.

### MM-01#05 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: money-models | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:35
claim: Client-financed acquisition (CFA): the gross profit one customer generates in their first 30 days exceeds the cost of acquiring them.
detail: CFA adds speed to the equation — not only how much you spend and make back, but how fast. Payback speed can unlock all growth or sink a business overnight. The math sections were cut from the book because proofreaders found them hard to follow; the course keeps them.
numbers: Window: first 30 days; condition: 30-day gross profit > CAC.

### MM-01#06 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:26
claim: Hormozi's first gym grew on Facebook ads until the platform raised his cost per lead fivefold and he could no longer afford to fund acquisition.
detail: He signed a $5,000/month lease, slept in the gym, and was proud of about 18 memberships after week one. The gym grew to nine-plus employees and felt figured out — until lead costs jumped. He needed immediate upfront cash to pay the ad spend on his credit card without going into debt.
numbers: Lease $5K/month; ~18 memberships in week one; 9+ employees; cost per lead ×5.

### MM-01#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: operator-mindset, paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:05
claim: When ad costs rise, stop fighting what you cannot control and make more money per customer.
detail: Two types of owners: one complains about uncontrollable factors like rising CPMs; the other changes what they control. Better ads and hooks help, but fundamentally you can either afford the impressions or you cannot — so either go out of business or find a way to make more money per customer.

### MM-01#08 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: verticals | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:57
claim: Adding a supplement upsell let Hormozi's gym get paid to acquire members: about $100 profit per new member covered the cost to acquire them.
detail: At an airport layover he met a college acquaintance whose two supplement stores made $80K and $130K a month with no members; Hormozi reasoned that with a member base he could sell at least $20K/month. Nobody bought at first; after the first sale he averaged $200 of supplements per new member. That upfront profit paid back acquisition immediately, so he could spend far more on ads again, and the membership revenue became pure upside. The moral: making another offer made more money.
numbers: $200 supplement sale per member ≈ $100 profit; competitor stores $80K and $130K/month.

### MM-01#09 | BENCHMARK | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics, verticals | fw: Money Model | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:23
claim: By recombining pricing and the client journey, Hormozi raised gym marketing returns from 4–5:1 to 31:1+, and licensed the model to 5,000+ gyms (Gym Launch).
detail: Across Gym Launch locations, the average return on ad spend in the first 30 days is 4:1 counting only upfront cash — memberships excluded, so it is not a full LTV:CAC ratio; the whole membership still comes on top.
numbers: Own gyms: 4–5:1 → 31:1+ ($30–40 per $1 of marketing); Gym Launch: 5,000+ locations, average 4:1 upfront 30-day ROAS.
applies_to: Gyms and local service businesses.

### MM-01#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:23
claim: The aim of a money model is never to use your own money to acquire customers again, so growth is no longer limited by capital, contacts or bank terms.
detail: Design how you charge and sequence offers so that customers' own money — not banks or investors — finances acquiring the next customers.
