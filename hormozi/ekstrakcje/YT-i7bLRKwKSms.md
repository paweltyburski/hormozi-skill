### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#01 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:15:01
claim: The minimum LTGP:CAC ratio rises with the number of manual steps in lead generation, conversion and delivery.
detail: The software rule of 3:1 holds only when all three parts are automated. With one manual part, he says about 6:1; with two manual parts about 9:1; with all three run by people, over 12:1. The margin is padding for the costs that arrive as you scale.
numbers: All three automated 3:1; two automated 6:1; one automated 9:1; none automated 12:1 or more.
applies_to: Any company deciding how much ratio it needs before scaling.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#02 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:35
claim: Lead generation counts as automated only when it is one-to-many, such as content or ads, or a compounding word of mouth; manual outreach as the main source counts as a manual step.
detail: The test is whether you are limited by human labour. Manual outreach is fine as a method, but it means you need the higher ratio on the scale in the earlier atom.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:22
claim: Acquisition cost almost always rises over time, so today's CAC is probably your best ever.
detail: Reasons given: rising ad prices (CPMs), more competitors, and moving to colder audiences as spend grows (the platform shows ads to less interested people, so more impressions are needed per sale). Extra reasons the ratio needs cushion: layers of management add cost, later customers are less sold and may be worth less, and each new hire in sales, marketing or service performs below the veterans for months while the cost starts day one.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: sales-team | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:54
claim: A company at 3:1 that adds a sixth salesperson, a new marketer and new account reps at once can go from barely profitable to unprofitable.
detail: Each new hire is weaker than the first five until trained, conversion dips and the business is lumpy. The model must absorb that dip, because fixing it by only selling harder never scales.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#05 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit | fw: CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:34
claim: Work out CAC and lifetime gross profit with a one-year back-of-napkin method from bookkeeping totals.
detail: CAC: add last year's advertising, associated labour, contractors and commissions, divide by new customers (example 100 customers for $100,000 = $1,000 each). Lifetime revenue: revenue divided by total customers; multiply by gross margin (a $20 sandwich cost on a $100 price is 80%). Cost per customer: yearly cost of delivery divided by customers; subtract from lifetime revenue. A full year smooths good and bad months and is usually the most accurate.
numbers: Example CAC $1,000 ($100,000 over 100 customers).

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#06 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: verticals, unit-economics | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:39
claim: He funded each new gym by putting $5,000 in a bank account, pre-selling for 30 days and collecting around $100,000 before opening.
detail: The cycle: sign the lease, run $100/day of ads, get 10 leads, close 2 at about $600 each, so $1,200 returns the same day. Done daily, that yields about $30,000 profit in month one; spending $500/day instead produced up to roughly $100,000 in sales. The cash paid for equipment, flooring, sign, paint, lobby and sound system, so the gym opened fully outfitted and paid for; six weeks later customers roll into recurring membership and cash flow turns positive. He opened six locations this way in his early twenties.
numbers: $100/day ads = 10 leads = 2 closes x ~$600 = ~$1,200; opening cost slightly under $100,000; presale ~30 days.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#07 | BENCHMARK | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: Money Model | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:22
claim: The challenge cash sequence per customer was $600 on day one, ~$200 supplements within 48 hours and ~$2,000 for a year's prepay at week three, blending to about $1,000 in 30 days.
detail: Not everyone takes each step, which is why the blend is far below the sum. The standard competitor offer was $21 for 20 days, then conversion to a $99 monthly membership; about $100 to acquire a trial and one in three converted, so roughly $300 per $99 member who stays about six months.
numbers: ~$1,000 blended in 30 days versus $21; competitor ~$300 CAC per member staying ~6 months.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:08
claim: The business that earns more per customer wins the advertising auction; it can outbid rivals without cutting prices.
detail: He frames attention as a continuous auction; with higher 30-day customer value one can buy nearly all ad space in a market. It is not a network effect or predatory pricing, just a better model. A business that loses money acquiring customers must budget (for example spend $4,000 for 40 buyers), while one that profits at once spends as much as it can absorb.
numbers: Budget example: $4,000 to get 40 buyers of a $21 offer.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:50
claim: He limits customer-value measurement to 30 days because that is typically as long as most small businesses can handle on cash flow, which matches the interest-free period of credit cards.
detail: Know two numbers: gross profit a customer yields in 30 days, and what it costs to get one. Ideally the first exceeds the second. Without understanding the business math, owners keep blaming methods (Facebook, outbound, content) when the issue may be the model.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#11 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:24:22
claim: Optimise the ratio, not the lowest CAC: LTV $5,000 at CAC $500 beats LTV $1,000 at CAC $200.
detail: Scenario one returns 10:1, scenario two 5:1, even though the first customer costs more than twice as much. Owners who go for cheap customers broaden the funnel and fill it with poor ones; higher-value customers widen the ratio. Also keep CAC distinct from cost per lead, because very cheap leads may never buy.
numbers: 5,000/500 = 10:1 versus 1,000/200 = 5:1.

### YT-i7bLRKwKSms#12 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing, money-models | fw: Crazy Eight | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:21:14
claim: The same levers apply to physical goods, shown on a book and a table: price, cost, upsell, downsell, cross-sell, financing and payment terms.
detail: Book: raise price, buy at larger scale, negotiate shipping, AI support, hardback or bundled formats as upsell, a second different book as cross-sell, financing. Table: marble upgrade, cheaper wood or plastic downsell, chairs or covers cross-sell, warranty, financing. He says it is harder with physical products than services, and warns a downsell that moves buyers of the expensive item to the cheap one loses money; it must capture people who would not have bought.
applies_to: Product businesses (the table and book are his examples).
