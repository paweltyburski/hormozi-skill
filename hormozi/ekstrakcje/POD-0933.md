### POD-0933#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: hiring-talent | fw: Buy vs Build | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:40
claim: Advice to this caller, not stated as a general rule: a home builder (about $200M revenue) wanting land-development capability should consider hiring away the best developer's key people if the relationships move with them, or buying the whole company if the relationships are entrenched. General statement (offered as his hypothesis): from a buy-versus-build view, stripping out the talent while they keep the relationships and then rebuilding would be the most efficient route.
detail: Capital-allocation logic, illustrated by AI firms paying very large signing bonuses to a few key researchers instead of buying their company for billions. The test is how portable the inroads are (zoning, local relationships). Stripping the talent and rebuilding is the most efficient route when it works.

### POD-0933#08 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:52
claim: Before a raise, talk to about ten times more potential investors than you need to learn what each must believe, then cost out the believable items.
detail: His own practice for a planned round. List each investor's need-to-believes, discard unrealistic asks, price the doable ones; sometimes proving one small belief quickly lets you ladder into the next. Like finding customers and building what they want, without handing over the vision.

### POD-0933#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:45
claim: Good VCs value a startup by discounting the end-state value by how many assumptions still must prove true; fewer open assumptions, higher valuation.
detail: They only make money on very large outcomes, so each unproven assumption discounts that outcome. A big valuation step-up should be justified by retiring the riskiest assumption, leaving a capital constraint rather than an assumption constraint.

### POD-0933#11 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:22:48
claim: "Are this month's leads the same as last month's?" separates a lead-quality problem from a sales-discipline problem.
detail: If lead quality is stable but close rates swing widely, he wagers script adherence is low and treats it as sales discipline: training cadence, scripting and daily or weekly management. A change in lead quality would point elsewhere.

### POD-0933#12 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: sales-team | fw: Two-Step Four-Way Fit Interview | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:27:32
claim: Screen high-volume sales hires by sending the script on application, asking for a 60-second video of it, then a group drill with feedback.
detail: Mid-size teams stall when attrition equals hiring, so hiring needs a funnel. Steps: applicants get the script; they record about 60 seconds of it; promising ones join a group call where each says it, gets one note, and repeats; unprepared or defensive people are cut; the rest get a job-offer call (from ten, pick two or three). The militant process signals how the company operates and attracts winners.

### POD-0933#15 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: hiring-talent | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:43:22
claim: Equity provides four things (cash flow, sale dollars, risk, control); his approach to leaders is to ask whether they want any risk, and when they say no, to withhold control and offer only cash flow (profit share) and a sale bonus.
detail: If you never want to sell, he would not encourage giving real shares away: the only thing guaranteed in ten years is that you are still in the business. Profit share comes off the bottom line; a sale bonus may be taxed as income (US), but alternatives need valuations, gifting tax or buying in. Leavers get neither. Write the sale bonus as a share of cash received, not headline value (he says he was burned on this before; said to a caller from a Melbourne agency).

### POD-0933#18 | FRAMEWORK | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:55:42
claim: Three revenue wheels of equipment businesses: the machine, what it consumes, and the service around it.
detail: Same model as auto dealers, heavy equipment, printers and ink. Sophisticated players sell the machine at cost or a loss and earn margin on consumables and service. To this caller (cleaning-equipment seller, little repeat business): first focus was knowing about 90-95% of buyers are retained yearly on consumables, since they must buy them somewhere; that base becomes the annuity. Also to him: put customers on a subscription with the initial sale and ask what maintenance programmes are sold up front.
