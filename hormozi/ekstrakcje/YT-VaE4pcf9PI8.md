### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:42
claim: Hormozi's personal picture of a perfect business is "unique, expensive, sticky air": it costs almost nothing to make, sells for far more, is habit-forming so customers return, and cannot be copied.
detail: Based on Buffett's remark that the ideal business costs a penny, sells for a dollar and is habit-forming, with uniqueness added from his talk of competitive moats and share of mind. Uniqueness means a brand others cannot copy.
### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:34
claim: After buying Geico, Buffett raised its advertising budget about tenfold to take over mindshare, and it grew accordingly.
detail: Used as an example of share of mind as a moat.
numbers: ~10x advertising budget.

### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:25
claim: Do not feel bad charging far above cost: you charge what the market gives, and markets run on information gaps between seller and buyer.
detail: If a pen costing a dollar can sell for $100, take it. The best investor looks for about 100 to 1, which means 99% gross margin, not 80% or 90%.
numbers: 100:1 price to cost; 99% gross margin.

### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:14
claim: Each step up in gross margin multiplies profit: 90% to 95% roughly doubles profitability, and a dollar of price on a penny cost makes ten times the profit per unit of cost as on a dime cost.
detail: On a $10 cost sold at $100 (90% margin), reaching 95% needs a price of $200, not $105. He used this with gyms stuck near 66% margin (which he calls horrible) and a target of 80%, which looks like only 14 points.
numbers: 90% to 95% margin = ~2x profit; $10 cost: $100 to $200; gym margin 66% to 80% target.

### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#05 | CASE | topic: management-ops | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:08:42
claim: Berkshire Hathaway runs with roughly 20 employees by delegating responsibility to the businesses it buys, yet reported about $88B of profit in a year (as he states it).
detail: Hormozi cites the structure as a model of abdicating responsibility and looks to operators who show three traits.
numbers: ~19-20 employees; ~$88B profit.

### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:35
claim: The first operator trait is risk aversion: the richest people give up bigger upside to protect against ruin, and it is more important not to lose than to win.
detail: Buffett's point that anything times zero is zero; "you should only have to get rich once". He contrasts people who buy lotteries with near-certain downside. Not losing for long enough means you win by default.

### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:20
claim: The second trait is long-term thinking: the longer the horizon owners talk in (week and payroll, then quarter, then years), the wealthier they tend to be.
detail: Buffett likes family-owned businesses because families do not cut corners for quarterly results. A simple plan: raise income by $20,000 a year for five years to reach a top-1% income.
numbers: +$20,000 per year for 5 years.

### YT-VaE4pcf9PI8#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:12
claim: The third trait, unimpeachable character, compounds: always-clearing cheques and kept promises bring more deals, faster closings and a reputation that is a lasting advantage.
detail: Buffett forgives lost money but is ruthless about lost reputation. A 30-year record is worth more than a 10-year one.
