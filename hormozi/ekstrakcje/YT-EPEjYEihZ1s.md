### YT-EPEjYEihZ1s#01 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Net free cash flow is what an owner can take out after reinvesting enough to stay ahead of competitors and keep the competitive advantage; it is the first metric he has finance report for every entity.
detail: It is owner earnings, the number Buffett and Munger calculate. It forces focus on profit beyond what is needed to run and grow the business, and it avoids capital-heavy businesses (manufacturing and other heavy capex types) where profit must be sunk back in.

### YT-EPEjYEihZ1s#02 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:01:36
claim: Reinvesting every gym profit into another location left him asset-rich and cash-poor; selling six locations later brought about $200,000, roughly $40,000 a year.
detail: After four or five years and millions in revenue he sold the gyms as assets, then lost the proceeds in a failed venture with a partner and promised not to repeat it. Had he pulled cash out of the business each year he would have had far more.
numbers: ~$200,000 total for six locations (partnerships included); 4-5 years, about $40,000 a year.

### YT-EPEjYEihZ1s#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:00
claim: Do not plan on a future exit: 99% of businesses never sell and many exits are small, so take money out every month while growing.
detail: Operational risk (outlawed, forced to move, a blow-up) is always present. Growing fast for arbitrary reasons adds waste and risk and loses the cash flow you get paid for growing. Seeing real deposits each month tells you if the month was better or worse.
numbers: 99-to-1 odds against a sale.

### YT-EPEjYEihZ1s#04 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:41
claim: Set a fixed cash floor in the business account and sweep everything above it to the owner every month.
detail: Based on projected fixed costs, pick a non-negotiable line (for example $100,000 or $500,000) and take out all that exceeds it. He asked an entrepreneur he holds equity in, who was not taking profit, to do this.
numbers: example floors $100,000 and $500,000.

### YT-EPEjYEihZ1s#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:06:26
claim: Two metrics for buying or reinvesting: price-to-earnings (cheap price) and return on capital (business quality).
detail: Return on capital: spend $1 million to open one location earning $100,000 a year is 10%; spend $1 million on ten locations each earning $100,000 is 100%. Per a book he cites, buying low P/E and high return on capital beat the S&P 500 over about 25 years, though roughly half the picks tank because they are mispriced troubled companies.
numbers: 10% versus 100% return on capital; ~25 years outperformance; ~half of picks fail.

### YT-EPEjYEihZ1s#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:27
claim: Reinvest in the business only if you get the best return on capital you can; if you expect about 10% back, you might as well put it in the S&P and skip the mess.
detail: Wealth does not need to come only from the business or a sale; putting $500,000-$1M a year of profit in the S&P every year would make you really wealthy.
numbers: example profit $500,000-$1M a year.
