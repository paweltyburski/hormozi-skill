### YT-O1As2zxy0es#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:17
claim: By his knowledge, there are four ways to package a service business for scale: private chain, franchise, license, or software and tech-enabled service.
detail: Private chain: you own, fund and staff every location. Franchise: outside investors buy into a model. License: you sell the IP with fewer elements than a franchise. Software or tech-enabled service: only if software can do the work. For most service businesses it will probably be options 1 to 3. He owns businesses in three of the four.
### YT-O1As2zxy0es#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:05:31
claim: In his view, a private chain is worth considering when a new location costs little relative to its first-year profit and the service is easy to replicate.
detail: Loose rule of thumb he offered (he said he should probably put a percentage on it): a new site costing less than about six months of its first-year profit would make sense as a private chain. His example figure was under $40,000-$50,000 for a site making $400,000 a year in profit (the two figures do not match the rule exactly; unclear in audio). The second condition is low service skill, as specialist skill makes quality inconsistent.
numbers: Build-out under ~6 months of first-year profit; example $40,000-$50,000 vs $400,000 yearly profit.
applies_to: Single-location service businesses
### YT-O1As2zxy0es#03 | EXAMPLE | topic: verticals | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:58
claim: Insomnia Cookies fits the private-chain rule: tiny sites, cheap build-out, high margin.
detail: Locations of about 800 square feet needed only an oven and a window, relied on delivery, and sold cheap ingredients at high margins. He adds that luxury fashion brands began as owned stores for the same reason, though those are product businesses.
numbers: ~800 sq ft per location.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:51
claim: Scaling depends on productizing the service so variability and required skill fall.
detail: Subway works because anyone with the same recipes and process makes the same sandwich. If your own skill is the value, quality is inconsistent and the model does not scale.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#05 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:49
claim: A portfolio company switched from licensing to owning its locations and grew from $0.5M to $12M a year in 14 months.
detail: Its licensees were earning a lot with the system and opening cost little, so he decided to keep that revenue instead of giving it away. Later it ran 12 company-owned locations and opened one more each month.
numbers: $500,000 to $12M per year in 14 months; 12 locations; 1 new location per month.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#06 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:29
claim: A franchise has three elements (name, business systems, fee); a license has fewer, and using all three while calling it a license is an illegal franchise.
detail: Examples: CrossFit gives name and fee but no systems; a white-label model gives systems and fee but no name. Mislabelling can bring fines, taxes and shutdown.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#07 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:49
claim: Starting a franchise costs about $750,000 and takes one to two years to show profit.
detail: The upfront franchise fee of about $50,000 mostly pays for finding buyers through franchise brokers, so profit comes later from royalties on top-line sales. Enterprise value counts only the franchisor's royalty revenue, since franchisees own the sites.
numbers: ~$750,000 start-up (legal, setup, filings); ~$50,000 typical upfront fee; 1-2 years to profit.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#08 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:44
claim: As a rule of thumb, he says you usually need 100+ locations for a franchise to make sense, and 90-95% of franchises don't get past 100 locations.
detail: He says franchising is the start of a new journey, not the goal of a service business.
numbers: 90-95% stay under 100 locations.
### YT-O1As2zxy0es#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:43
claim: Franchise when each new location needs a lot of capital, so outside owners carry that risk.
detail: A McDonald's costs about $1M to open, so crowdsourcing the capital makes sense. If you are confident of winning and a site is cheap, own it instead.
numbers: ~$1M per site example.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:32
claim: Licensing suits easy-to-copy, offer-driven models where speed and early cash matter more than exit value.
detail: It costs almost nothing, has high margins and pays from day one, but is hard to defend and has low exit multiples unless licensees stay. Gym Launch licensed because he lacked capital and wanted cash flow; the offer was easy to copy, so speed to market share mattered.
numbers: 80%+ yearly licensee retention for an exit multiple of 6x or more.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:29
claim: Software gives the highest exit value but costs the most, takes the longest and pays little cash early.
detail: It only counts as software if yearly retention is 80% or higher; otherwise buyers value it as a service with tech. Without a technical co-founder, do not hand it to an outside dev team: he says he lost $2-3 million that way.
numbers: Retention 80%+ per year; development losses $2-3 million.

### YT-O1As2zxy0es#12 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:12:14
claim: To choose a vehicle, weigh the IP, cash flow needed, upfront investment, time to goal and the owner's risk appetite.
detail: As an investor he asks which of the four fits the IP and the model, how protectable it is, how structurally sound the returns are, and how much risk the founder accepts. Certain win and cheap openings point to private ownership.
