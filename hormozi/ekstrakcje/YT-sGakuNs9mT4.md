### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: sales-team | fw: CLOSER | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:50
claim: Sales tone has five levers: three constants (loud enough, slow enough, clearly articulated) and two variables (pauses and rising pitch).
detail: The constants maximise comprehension. He found common coaching terms such as curiosity, aggressive or laid-back tone vague and confusing, and points to these specific voice settings instead. Variation in rhythm appears naturally when the rep knows the script rather than reads it.
### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#02 | BENCHMARK | topic: sales-process | also: sales-team | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:35
claim: Speech rate should sit between roughly 135 and 185 words per minute; faster lowers trust, slower makes you sound unintelligent.
detail: Hormozi speaks near 185 and, for the book launch pitch, counted words to know exactly when the purchase link would drop. A steady rate also makes call lengths consistent instead of varying from ten minutes to ninety.
numbers: 135-185 words per minute.

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#03 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:29
claim: Memorise a script by reading it aloud repeatedly while blacking out one more word each pass, until you recite a blank page.
detail: Print two copies, one to correct against. Read the whole script clearly, then cross out one word, read again, cross out another, and so on, so total repetitions equal the word count. He calls the result "breathing" the script, which frees attention to listen, since the person who speaks most in a sale tends to lose.

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:28
claim: Treat the script as the standard no rep is above, because a team in which everyone says different words cannot be coached or improved.
detail: He says about 95% of sales teams lack this uniformity. Words in the script were refined by many calls and are known to close.
numbers: ~95% of sales teams lack a common script (his claim).

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#05 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:24
claim: Mark a script with only three signs: three dots for a short pause, a period for a full stop, and a question mark for rising pitch.
detail: A short pause draws attention to a point; the strongest combination is a long pause with a raised voice at the moment you ask for the sale, then silence. This replaces vague tone vocabulary and lets managers coach with precise language ("speak louder", "slow down").

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#06 | BENCHMARK | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:06
claim: Waiting up to eight seconds after asking someone to buy was linked to about 30% more closed sales in meta-analyses he recalls.
detail: The source is half-remembered (one possibly from Columbia, unclear in audio). The reason is that people need processing time and believe their own words more than yours. In his two-hour book pitch the longest pause came when he asked for the purchase.
numbers: Up to 8 seconds of silence after the ask; +30% sales.

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#07 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:05
claim: Manage tone most carefully at the close, where adrenaline pushes reps to speak too fast, and use clear articulation to slow them.
detail: Fast and loud sounds aggressive after a "no". Coaching language should be behavioural: "speak at a slower cadence" instead of "stop sounding nervous".

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:53
claim: The reps who close most are those who ask the most times, within the limit of not annoying the prospect, and objections mostly serve as a reason to ask again.
detail: Roughly 20-30% of sales are won after the first "no"; most of the selling occurs before the ask, and most deals are lost in discovery, not in the pitch or close.
numbers: ~20-30% of sales come after the first no.

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#09 | SCRIPT | topic: objections-closing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:14:20
claim: One universal close turns the stated reason against itself: the reason you can't buy is the reason you need this more than anyone.
detail: Structure: (1) when the objection is not a real logistical limit, repeat their reason; (2) say it is exactly why they need the solution (no time, no money); (3) for B2B, tie it to the problem you solve (too few customers, weak pricing); (4) ask again. He says there are seven such universal closes; this is the one given. Do not argue to be right.
applies_to: Consumer and B2B sales; time, money and decision-maker objections.

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#10 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:58
claim: In discovery, if answers are thin, alternate "can you give me an example?" and "can you be specific?" until a concrete fact appears ("pulling teeth").
detail: Then recap the fact, draw the inference ("sounds like a marketing problem, is that fair?"), ask what else holds them back, and repeat until you have two or three key points chunked up to things you sell.

### YT-sGakuNs9mT4#11 | SCRIPT | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: CLOSER | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:21
claim: Transition to the pitch by summarising the problems they named, getting agreement that solving them would grow the business, then matching each to a specialist.
detail: Steps: restate the chunked issues (inconsistent leads, thin margins); ask whether solving them would help growth; get a yes; ask permission to tell them about your offer; pair each problem with the relevant expert and ask whether that expert could help. Then present the pitch, make the invitation, and stay silent.
