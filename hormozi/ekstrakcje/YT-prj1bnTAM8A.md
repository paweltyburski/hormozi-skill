### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#01 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:01:11
claim: A small agency competing with a market leader should answer "you are not the leader" by contrasting the leader's assigned rep with the owner's personal attention.
detail: The owner served gym owners and was challenged for lacking a track record against Gym Launch. Hormozi's advice: the leader's pitch is a proven system and case studies; the small player says clients are a number there, handled by a checklist-following account rep, while with me you get my cell number. The real comparison is me versus their rep. Every position has an advantage, so play the cards you hold.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Rule of 100 | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:37
claim: Volatile sales come from too little volume; compress the advertising that takes four weeks to produce one customer into a single day.
detail: If you win one customer every three to five weeks, a fixed amount of advertising activity (yours, others', word of mouth) is behind it. Ask how to run that amount in one day to get one customer daily. More volume cancels luck and brings consistency.
numbers: One customer per 3-5 weeks as the starting point; goal one per day.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Core Four | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:08:24
claim: The "size of the pie" fallacy: most small businesses treat one method on one platform as their whole market, so they think new competitors are shrinking it.
detail: In their minds a competitor leaves them half the market, and two more leave a quarter. In reality customers can be reached by warm and cold outreach, content, ads, affiliates, referral networks, employees and agencies, and then by many platforms within each. Your little circle sits in a mostly empty field; the problem is that nobody knows you exist, so advertise more.
### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#05 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Rule of 100 | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:37
claim: Add commenting to the Rule of 100: leave the best comment on posts relevant to your avatar to borrow their views.
detail: Posts that attract your avatar act as free ads. Content and DMs are capped by platform limits, but comments are far less limited. A new channel starts from zero even for experienced owners. Be generous: praise and add to the author instead of tearing them down. Paid-ad budget per month should track what you can pay per customer.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#06 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:20

detail: Homework: state how you differ from the leader in a way nobody can dispute. Go one level deeper than David and Goliath: you do not compete with the whole Nike but with the one junior person who runs a single account; win by nibbling at the perimeter like the barbarians against Rome.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#07 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:15:14
claim: Two companies with equal sales can differ hugely: the one that keeps customers pays to acquire half as many.
detail: Company A sells 100 customers a year and keeps all of them, reaching 200 in year two and 300 in year three. Company B sells 100, 200, 300 but loses all it gained each year. By year three both sell 300, yet B acquired 600 customers against A's 300, and A keeps growing with 100 new ones each year, while B needs 400 sales just to keep pace.
numbers: A: 100/200/300 customers over three years paid for 300 acquisitions; B: 600 acquisitions.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:35
claim: Acquiring customers only gets costlier over time, because later audiences are colder, less suited and more price sensitive.
detail: Early adopters give way to laggards. Acquirers and investors look at remaining runway and ask whether customers can be kept and can bring more. Nail retention (keeping, satisfying and referring customers) before scaling; if you cannot, scaling only thins margins and increases volatility from reliance on paid ads.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Starving Crowd | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:23
claim: It is better to build something 100 people love than something a million people mildly like, and a named avatar makes that practical.
detail: Give the avatar a name (say Jenny, three kids, wants to lose weight) and ask what she would think at every decision. If unit economics are poor when small, they will not improve when bigger.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#10 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: product-delivery | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:48
claim: Brainstorm solutions with "yes, and" thinking and no cost limits, and fix the price only afterwards.
detail: Steps: define the dream; list every problem in the way (listen to prospects' excuses); name each solution; brainstorm every possible way to solve it without ever saying no (cook for them, a chef, a group chef, grocery trips, a recording); then trim and stack. Because price is set last, nothing is off the board yet.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#12 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:24:32
claim: In his experience, a change to a sales script, ad copy, landing page or process tends to cause about a 20% immediate drop in performance, so he wants changes he expects to gain north of 20%.
detail: Small improvements of around 5% often are not worth it: if you keep changing things, the team can sit permanently at a decrement because no one gets time to become good. He over-indexes on stability now and, for example, with an expected 30-50% boost it is probably worth the bet.
numbers: ~20% performance drop after a change (in his experience); change only if expected lift is north of 20% (example: 30-50%).
### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#13 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:26:11
claim: Changing a process that people must execute in a service business is very tough, and the performance drop shrinks as the team gets smaller.
detail: Different for a manufactured physical product or a one-time software change. Solo, there is almost no drop; translating a change to a 50-person team needs certainty that it is worth it. This is another reason to nail the offer before scaling.
applies_to: Service businesses with human-executed processes.
### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#14 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:23:45
claim: Promising an offer is easier than delivering it, and delivery timelines run far longer than planned.
detail: A task he thinks will take six weeks often takes six months or a year, so he takes on fewer new things than expected. If you cannot deliver the components of the offer, do not scale yet.
numbers: Estimate 6 weeks, reality 6 months to 1 year.

### YT-prj1bnTAM8A#15 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:27:20
claim: Your competition changes with growth in stacked layers: yourself, family and friends, employees, market shifts, then regulators.
detail: Layers add up rather than replace each other. Friends and family may resist new expectations, and some relationships end. Employees must be sold on your dream, and recruiting experts shortens learning by the years they already spent. At larger size, a shrinking market hurts you and regulators begin to target you (he cites FTC suits against Amazon, Fortnite and Reebok in the last year).
