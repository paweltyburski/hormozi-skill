### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: unit-economics | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:46
claim: Answer "why can't I do more?" in order: market, data, model, affordability, then manpower, and repeat the loop for talent.
detail: Check if the market is truly capped; if not, if you lack metrics; if so, get data; if doubts about the model, apply the "all businesses have hard parts" test; if you cannot afford more, decide whether leads, sales or lifetime value is low; if you have manpower limits, run the same questions on recruiting.

### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:46
claim: A real market ceiling is rare; if you can serve one market you can duplicate elsewhere, so test saturation with counts.
detail: A B2B firm serving chiropractors had 100 customers among about 50,000 chiropractors, so the problem is that nobody knows it, not a capped market. A true limit is a case like all customers in one tiny town.
numbers: 100 customers versus about 50,000 chiropractors.

### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:31
claim: Every business has a hard component, so switching business often just swaps one hard part for another; the hard part is what you are paid to solve.
detail: His CFO's line: after 38 years, all businesses have problems. Cleaning: customers are easy, reliable cleaners are hard. Fitness: staff are plentiful, but sign-ups are hard. Frame the hard problem by its payoff, such as a recruiting and training engine adding $10-50M of enterprise value.
numbers: Extra $10, $20 or $50M of enterprise value if the hard problem is solved (illustrative).

### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:53
claim: Most goals are reachable in decades rather than months, even in low-glamour models such as fast-casual restaurants.
detail: He uses his landlord's restaurant empire to argue that sticking with a plain model for 45 years can yield billions in sales, large annual cash take-home, and tax-advantaged real estate. A low-opportunity market also has less sophisticated competitors than AI-type markets.
numbers: Illustrative: 5 locations at $3M each at 27% net margin; large chain about $3.7B sales per year; about 45 years.

### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#05 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:17
claim: When an owner "can't afford more", isolate whether leads cost too much, too few leads close, or lifetime value is too low.
detail: Common hidden cause: a break-even front end with no back end, fixed by selling something further. Another: churn within three months, so acquisition cost is not the issue. Insurance example: lifetime value is set and about 30% of phone calls close, so work on creative quality and quantity, conversion rate optimisation and better lead magnets.
numbers: Close rate of 30% on phone (insurance example); churn within 3 months.

### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-process | also: objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:40
claim: If leads are cheap and lifetime value is good but sales fail, examine messaging, scripts, price, follow-up, training, onboarding and sales pay.
detail: This is the "middle of the sandwich" between lead cost and lifetime value.

### MZ-OuZMlb3U7Bs#07 | DECISION_RULE | topic: hiring-talent | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:27
claim: If demand and economics work and you lack technicians or coaches, treat it as manpower and ask the same questions about recruiting.
detail: Do you have recruiting metrics, is the talent pool real, is the offer to the worker compelling, and what do you earn per hire?
