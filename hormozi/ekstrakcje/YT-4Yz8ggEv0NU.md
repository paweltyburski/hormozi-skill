### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#02 | DEFINITION | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:32

detail: Trust can predict a good or a bad performance. A company with no history that looks and sounds like a scam is treated as one, so appearance matters before reputation exists. Build trust by keeping promises, ideally over-delivering, over a long period, like a friendship.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: content-brand | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:32
claim: Brand pays through four measurable effects: higher conversion, higher click-through, higher price, and more repurchases.
detail: Conversion applies at each funnel step: a stronger brand lifts clicks on the ad, page conversion and call close. Price: loved products can carry a premium. Repurchase multiplies the profit of the earlier gains. Together they lower cost per customer and raise lifetime value.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: content-brand | fw: Goodwill (Price-Value-Cost) | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:09
claim: A strong brand supports higher prices because customers pay a premium to cut their risk; consistency itself reduces risk.
detail: Buffett's test: needing to pray before a 10% price rise signals a weak business. Risk reduction is one of the three levers of value creation. McDonald's is cited as consistent, not great. In his illustration a business with the same costs charging 10% more roughly doubles its profit (assumes thin margin). You cannot over-deliver to everyone; aim to meet the most demanding expectations so more people are satisfied.
numbers: 10% price rise roughly doubles profit in his example (his illustration).

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: retention-ltv | fw: Give:Ask Ratio | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:56
claim: Give-and-ask runs through the whole business, not just content: a great product turns the ask itself into a give.
detail: Jobs framed it as deposits and withdrawals in a relationship each time someone buys and loves, or dislikes, a product. Hormozi therefore sends people to his books first, because value relative to cost is high. Media is a measurement of word of mouth; the brand is audience trust.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:16:40
claim: Audit your week by listing each activity and asking how it ties to how the business makes money; drop what is too indirect or drives nothing.
detail: Name inputs as concrete actions (write an email, film ads, make N outreach contacts, take N sales calls), not vague words. Growth comes from two outputs only: more customers or more lifetime value per customer. He cancelled recurring data-sharing meetings in a department he took over because the data changed no behaviour. A public-company CEO told him roughly one tenth of staff create most of the economics (name unclear in audio).
numbers: About one tenth of employees drive most economics (the CEO's view).

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#10 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:22:11
claim: Scale any input in three ways: automate it, hand parts of it to other people, or convert it to one-to-many.
detail: Delegate in chunks, not whole roles (e.g. someone else scripts and reviews while he only records). One-to-one acquisition such as cold outreach or one-on-one affiliate recruiting must be automated, staffed, or turned into one-to-many. Keep high-leverage activities yourself longer, such as allocating resources across portfolio companies. He argues every business can eventually be delegated if you find people better than you.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#12 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: management-ops | also: hiring-talent | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:26:05
claim: Three questions Hormozi likes to ask an employee: what do you do, what do we do as a business, and how does what you do help what we do.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#13 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:29:21
claim: Price every meeting: divide annual pay by 2,000 hours for hourly cost, multiply by attendees, and require several times that in return.
detail: A 10-person meeting can cost about $500; he says it should return roughly four to five times that or be skipped. Meetings multiply once staff meet each other without you. He lists three common time sinks: projects unrelated to the main objective (reorganising a project tool, redoing the website instead of split-testing headlines), meetings, and data that does not change action.
numbers: Annual pay / 2,000 = hourly cost; 10-person meeting about $500; target return 4-5x the cost.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#14 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:30:21
claim: The limits test for a metric: if it rising or falling would not change what you do, stop tracking it.
detail: Ask whether you are data-driven or data-distracted. Work backward from the inputs that drive output to the data that would change how you run them (a falling CTR changes hooks and targeting; tag counts matter only if they lead to money). Most businesses can find the key figures on the back of a napkin.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#16 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:38:19

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#17 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: product-delivery | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:41:03
claim: First mistake with referral programmes: using a better incentive to cover a mediocre product; people refer products they are proud of.
detail: Fix the product first. Incentives work best as a lubricant that moves a willing referrer from referring some friends to referring everyone, not as the thing that starts referring. He says he spent too long on crazier incentives in his early years, because early marketing success hid weak product economics. Marketing amplifies whatever you are, so it also tells the market faster that you are mediocre.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#18 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: - | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:43:35
claim: Second mistake: a weak incentive; a 7 out of 10 product with a 10 out of 10 incentive still generates referrals, but the reward must not be so big that people hand over the product.
detail: Most owners think the reward is strong when it is not (20% or half off a next purchase). A lifetime benefit would move people but must still require that the referral tries, buys and rebuys the product. Some mediocre products survive on exceptional affiliate payouts.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#19 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: retention-ltv | fw: Referral Rate minus Churn | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:47:19
claim: Referrals are not one per customer: about one in ten customers refers many, and you cannot tell which, so over-deliver for everyone.
detail: His first gym had a nurse among the first ten customers who brought about 20 more, including her hairstylist, friends and colleagues; it decided whether he made rent. Study what a super-referrer did by accident and repeat it deliberately. Referred customers also stay longer, so a product good enough to be referred improves both acquisition and retention.
numbers: 1 in 10 customers refers about 10; one nurse brought about 20 customers in the first two weeks.

### YT-4Yz8ggEv0NU#20 | DECISION_RULE | topic: lead-getters | also: scaling-constraints | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:48:52
claim: Third mistake: not reinvesting once a referral or affiliate system works; keep removing steps and adding training to lower the effort needed.
detail: After seeing 55% of School customers come from affiliates, the team decided to double down, making sharing easier and cutting activation effort.
