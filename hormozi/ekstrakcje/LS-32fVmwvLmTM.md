### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: unit-economics | also: money-models, paid-ads | fw: Money Model | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:01
claim: If ad spend cannot be scaled because lifetime gross profit per customer is too low, change the offer, move to cheaper channels, or (his preference) expand the money model to earn more per customer.
detail: Three levers stated in order: (1) a better offer improves the metrics; (2) channels with cheaper CPMs; (3) extend the sequence of offers so each customer is worth more. He prefers the third because businesses differ far more in what a customer is worth than in what it costs to get one.

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#21 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:47:34
claim: Three wrong ways to price: copy the market average, offer more for a bit less than that, or apply a fixed cost-plus markup; price on what the customer will pay.
detail: Averaging guarantees you are a commodity; undercutting it is worse. Cost-plus at least considers margin but is about you, not the buyer. Without comparables, price is speculative: a remote house is worth what someone will pay, with a floor set by what it would cost the buyer to build it and value added for the time saved. He prices mostly "on the back of a napkin".

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#22 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Point of Greatest Deprivation | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:48:45
claim: Willingness to pay changes along one customer's purchases and between customers, so he prefers non-linear pricing: onboarding or upfront fees when pain is highest, lower ongoing prices later.
detail: Ski-lodge beer: the first is worth ~$7, the second ~$3, the third ~$1, the fourth nothing; working through the totals, one beer at $7 beats three at $1 or two at $3. Recurring services face the same decline, and service businesses also front-load their work, so an upfront fee offsets CAC and matches deprivation. Between customers, $2,000 is trivial to a rich buyer and everything to a poor one, so tiers should signal who each is for.

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#23 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: verticals | fw: Point of Greatest Deprivation | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:50:57
claim: In gyms he replaced the free trial with a first purchase of $500 to $5,000 and a cheaper price afterwards, because sign-up day is when motivation and pain peak.
detail: Gyms paid to get people in the door, then gave the first visit away. He charged most at that moment and less for the ongoing community. His wider point: the reason people buy is often not why they stay (they come to lose weight and stay for friends), and those reasons carry different willingness to pay, so each phase can have its own price.

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#24 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:52:23
claim: The sweet spot on price is the one that maximises units sold × gross margin (absolute gross profit, not revenue); it is a curve with equal-money points on both sides, and for most service businesses involving humans he makes a strong argument for being the premium-price leader.
detail: If 10 customers earn what 100 would, scale the 10-customer business: once you can deliver to 100 of those, you are ten times as profitable, and he also alludes (loosely) to being able to advertise to those buyers and their willingness to pay. Treat price as a zone that moves with the market, not a single correct answer; only pure marketplaces such as Uber's surge pricing get close to perfect pricing. Tiers solve the rest.

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#26 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Anchor Upsell, Crazy Eight | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:56:26

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#29 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 01:04:06
claim: Fear lives in the vague, never in the specific: break uncertainty into concrete outcomes, drop those outside your control, and play the worst case to its end.
detail: The amygdala strongly changes behaviour but is poor at detail. Define failure (e.g. having to take a job again); play it out: friends, family or a shelter mean you will not starve, so you have unlimited attempts. Action alleviates anxiety. He also says success can be defined as continuing to try, as you would judge a child.

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#30 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 01:08:05
claim: Capture rather than manufacture content: record yourself delivering the service, then compress the key moments of a client's journey into short and long formats.

### LS-32fVmwvLmTM#31 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 01:18:31
claim: Hormozi defines trauma as accelerated, lasting learning from an aversive event; whether it is bad depends on whether the learned behaviour helps or hurts your goals, and the fix is to ask "what do I do instead?" and reward the new behaviour.
detail: A child burned by a stove learns permanently not to touch it, which is trauma by this definition and useful. Combat reflexes help in combat and may hurt at home. To change a harmful pattern, define the replacement action and build a reward loop around trying it. His wider point: vague terms like "running from problems" produce confused choices; ask what it means, how you know, and so what.
