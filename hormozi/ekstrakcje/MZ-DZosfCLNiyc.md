### MZ-DZosfCLNiyc#01 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:16
claim: His biggest financial mistake was building a general lead-nurture software company when a CRM built for gyms would likely have sold for far more.
detail: The software (Allen, decision trees on responses) sold for about $46M; he believes a gym-specific CRM would have fetched about half a billion. He admits the counterfactual of staying focused on gyms is unknowable and maybe even bigger.
numbers: Sold for about $46M versus an estimated $500M for a gym CRM (his estimate).

### MZ-DZosfCLNiyc#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:29
claim: Best companies feel expensive and still often yield higher returns than cheap "cigar butt" businesses.
detail: Beginner investors usually learn to prefer wonderful companies at a fair price over bargain ones, after the bargain hurts. He cites one famous investor's biggest regret as not investing in a later round of a company he seeded.

### MZ-DZosfCLNiyc#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:01
claim: A sharply higher price in a new round, even led by insiders, is usually fair if the risk profile changed materially.
detail: He recalls a venture-fund study (Google Ventures, a decade old) where such rounds were a strong signal. Example logic: a 10% chance of a billion-dollar outcome rises to about 50% once a network effect or virality is solved, so a fast 3-5x price rise is rational. The regret is emotional, not analytical.
numbers: Example odds 10% rising to 50%; valuation change of 3x or 5x cited for a payments company.
