### MM-03#01 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: CAC | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: CAC is every cost required to sell a new customer, divided by the number of new customers.
detail: Include ad spend, media buyer and creative payroll, software, sales commissions and salaries, and the managers of those teams. For paid media it is wise to track media CAC (ad spend only) separately from fully loaded CAC. With outbound, payroll is most of CAC; with content, it is the payroll of the team that makes, edits and distributes it. Multi-channel companies simply total all acquisition costs and divide by customers gained — you must know this to afford unlimited customers.

### MM-03#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: outreach, paid-ads | fw: CAC | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:45
claim: Three worked CAC calculations — outreach, content and paid ads — show the method: total acquisition spend ÷ new customers.
detail: Outreach: $3,000/month emailer + $200 software + 8 sales × $100 commission = $4,000 ÷ 8 = $500. Content: two media staff at $5,000 each + 10 sales × $100 commission = $11,000 ÷ 10 = $1,100. Paid ads (higher ticket): $4,000 media buyer + $20,000 ad spend + $1,000 software + 10 sales × $1,000 commission = $35,000 ÷ 10 = $3,500. Add costs you have (e.g. sales payroll) and drop ones you do not.
numbers: CAC $500 (outreach), $1,100 (content), $3,500 (paid ads).

detail: Working out your CAC is the first move toward reducing it.

### MM-03#04 | DECISION_RULE | topic: unit-economics | also: offers | fw: CAC | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:21
claim: Lower CAC mainly by making better offers; cutting labor or software costs is a minor lever Hormozi rarely touches.
detail: Better offers raise response rates, raise the share who take the next step, and lower CPMs because people respond to a better offer. Paying sales or outbound staff less, or buying cheaper software, also lowers CAC, but offer improvements are orders of magnitude larger, and cost cutting hurts morale for little gain.

### MM-03#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:53
claim: Use "free" and "new": they are the strongest two words in advertising.
detail: Keeping CAC low means getting the maximum sales from the minimum ad spend, i.e. converting as large a share of the audience as you can.

### MM-03#06 | BENCHMARK | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Penny Gap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:53
claim: The penny gap: in Dan Ariely's Hershey Kiss test, nine times more people took a free kiss than one costing a single cent.
detail: In that test even one cent cut the take rate sharply (free got nine times more takers); he uses it as an argument for giving something away free, especially when starting out.
numbers: Free vs $0.01 → 9× more takers.

### MM-03#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:43
claim: Give value away free, but do not discount products: he follows the Chick-fil-A model — free or full price, never in between.
detail: Answering the "free dilutes my brand" worry, he separates giving products away free from giving value away free (content, lead magnets, software tools). Chick-fil-A: if they mess up your order the meal is free; otherwise it is premium and full price.

### MM-03#08 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: offers | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:43
claim: If people will not take your free offer, there are only three reasons: they do not want it, they do not believe it, or the wrong people are seeing it.
detail: (1) Do not want it: change the thing or how you describe it. (2) Do not believe it: it sounds too good to be true. (3) Wrong audience: a free gym membership in Ohio means nothing to someone who is never there; fur coats to Arizonans in summer. Testing free gives clean feedback on which one is broken.

### MM-03#09 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:09
claim: Believability beats size of promise: "make an extra $500 a month" repeatedly out-pulls "make an extra $5,000 a month".
detail: A marketer ran an annual ad — "give me $100 and I'll give you $1,000 back" — and almost no one called, because nobody believed it. Hormozi has seen the $500 vs $5,000 split test won by the smaller promise many times, because more people believe it.

### MM-03#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:00
claim: Free gets more leads at lower cost, but the volume can overwhelm you and some takers will have no intention of buying.
detail: Without the staff or time to work all the new leads, the extra volume is wasted — which is solved with friction, not with a weaker offer.

### MM-03#11 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: sales-process, paid-ads | fw: Create Flow Monetize Add Friction | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:01
claim: Keep the strongest possible magnet and add friction to filter, rather than making the offer worse.
detail: Friction options: qualification questions, more steps in the process, tighter targeting, required consumption of material before the next step, longer ads. The goal is to get everyone who wants the thing to raise a hand, then let only qualified people with money through.

### MM-03#12 | CASE | topic: offers | also: verticals, sales-process | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:56
claim: "Free brings broke people" is a myth: across 20 gyms, free-offer ads produced more sales at the same close rate as paid-offer ads.
detail: Ten locations advertised without "free", ten with it, all else equal. The free group made more sales; close rates were identical. Strong salespeople closed both kinds of lead; weak ones closed neither. The only difference was much cheaper leads for the free group. Rich people like free things too — removing risk gets them to take a look, and a follow-up offer can convert them. He reran this whenever the objection resurfaced among gym owners.
numbers: 10 vs 10 locations across markets; close rate equal; lead cost much lower with free.
applies_to: Tested in gyms; principle applied generally.

claim: If you could make only one offer to convert prospects, make it free.
detail: Wading through poor leads and then adding friction beats an empty inbox and an empty calendar.

### MM-03#14 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: offers, paid-ads | fw: Create Flow Monetize Add Friction | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:27
claim: Advertising order of operations: create flow, then monetize, then add friction.
detail: Create flow: give the big, attractive thing away to get volume. Monetize: make sure the right people get to a sale, even inefficiently. Add friction: only then optimize with filters. You cannot optimize an empty inbox, an empty calendar or a funnel with no clicks.

### MM-03#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Attraction Offers | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:13
claim: Free and discounted are one continuum — 100% off is free — so every money model works with either.
detail: Wherever a construct says free, it can be $1; where it says $1, it can be $19. What drives engagement is getting something for less than it is worth; the gap between value and price is the arbitrage. A "$19 chiropractor visit" and a "free chiropractor visit" run the same model (00:22:38).

### MM-03#16 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: sales-process, verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:13
claim: If no-shows are expensive (dentist, lawyer, chiropractor), either use a small paid discount to get a card on file or keep it free but book the first visit with a cheaper staff member.
detail: A card on file improves show rates and allows no-show fees. Alternatively the free first appointment is handled by someone whose time costs less, who then books the visit with you.

### MM-03#17 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: promotions-launches | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:27
claim: Skip moderate discounts: 10–20% off rarely changes behavior, so it only gives away profit; use 50% or more on something whose value people already know.
detail: The only purpose of free or discounted offers is to get people who would not otherwise act to act. If the same number respond at 10–20% off as at full price, you just lose margin. He later shows how to give deep discounts without giving away the farm.
numbers: Avoid 10–20% off; use ≥50% off.

### MM-03#18 | FRAMEWORK | topic: pricing | also: paid-ads | fw: Four Ways to Display a Discount | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:34
claim: The same discount can be displayed four ways: percent off, amount off, relative equivalent (or portion off), and sale price beside a crossed-out retail price.
detail: Percent: "87% off the first visit" ($13 for a $100 visit). Amount: "$87 off", or "$181 off the first week, normally $210". Relative equivalent: frame the saving as something familiar — "save a steak dinner", "less than going out to lunch"; portion off belongs here, e.g. on a $100/month, $1,200/year plan, "4 months free" = "$400 off" (he calls it 25% off; the math gives 33%). Sale price: "$800" with "$1,200" crossed out. Test the formats against each other.
numbers: $1,200/year plan → $800 = $400 off = 4 months free (stated as 25%, actually ~33%).

### MM-03#19 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: pricing, promotions-launches | fw: Four Ways to Display a Discount | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:13
claim: Rotate discount display formats month to month to keep advertising fresh while the underlying offer stays identical.
detail: One month "4 months free", the next "$400 off", the next "$800, was $1,200": the market feels a new offer each month; internally nothing changes.

### MM-03#20 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: Four Ways to Display a Discount | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:02
claim: Showing a low dollar price works only when the market knows what the thing normally costs; for unique or intangible products, use percent off retail instead.
detail: A $29 chiropractic adjustment or $20 teeth whitening (even $100) reads as a bargain because people know the usual price is higher. A digital product, creator launch or anything nobody else sells has no market price, so "this price off that price" looks arbitrary, and a bare "$17 course" may seem cheap to some and expensive to others.

### MM-03#21 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: sales-process, money-models | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:55
claim: Discounts have three advantages over free: no bait-and-switch risk, some cash collected, and above all a card on file that makes a two-step sale seamless.
detail: Charge the smallest amount needed to capture and verify the card, so that in person the upsell is just "want to use the card on file?" Many believe discount buyers spend more because they arrive expecting to pay; Hormozi thinks that is mostly salespeople feeling more conviction, since his data showed equal close rates for free and paid leads.

### MM-03#22 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: offers, money-models | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:28
claim: Never discount the core service; avoid discounting anything with hard costs unless you really know what you are doing; break off a well-understood piece or create an extra, discount that heavily, and sell the rest.
detail: Offers have many components; take one feature off and make it 99% off (unbundling is covered in the $100M Offers course). Whatever you break off must be something people already understand the value of. The downside — bargain hunters — is small: they are already customers, easier to upsell than cold prospects.

claim: Attraction offers come first in the money model because client-financed acquisition needs demand, and free or cheap front ends create it.
detail: Without interest there is nothing to monetize. He ties this to the gap between CAC and gross profit and calls low-cost or free front ends a strong way to deal with it by driving lots of demand (the transcript wording here is garbled).
