### POD-0345#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:01:05
claim: A business has a hypothetical maximum revenue that follows from new sales per month and lifetime gross profit per customer, and comparing today's revenue with that ceiling shows whether it will grow, shrink or stay flat.
detail: Three variables: new sales per month, lifetime gross profit per customer, and hypothetical max revenue. If current revenue is above the ceiling, the business is on course to shrink; if below, it will grow until it caps at the ceiling; if equal, it is in equilibrium, with sales and revenue roughly unchanged month over month.
applies_to: Recurring-revenue businesses, used when sizing up portfolio companies.
### POD-0345#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:11
claim: A marketing agency selling a $1,000 per month service added 120 new clients a month with about 13% churn, so its ceiling was about 923 clients.
detail: Cost to serve was $100 per month, so gross margin was 90%. Churn came from roughly 50 clients lost out of about 380 active (about 13.1%). Ceiling = 120 / 0.13, about 923 clients, or roughly $923,000 per month (about $11M a year). At 380 clients they were far below the ceiling, so growth would continue but flatten as churn rose.
numbers: Price $1,000/month; cost $100/month; 120 new clients/month; ~13% monthly churn; ~380 active clients; ceiling ~923 clients.

### POD-0345#03 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: Revenue Churn | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:51
claim: If a business has been flat for months, its monthly churn equals new customers per month divided by current customers, so you can derive it without the owner tracking it.
detail: Average new sales over the last six months; if customer count has not grown, losses equal that average. Then churn = losses / active customers. From churn and margin you get lifetime gross profit per customer without asking the owner for a number they do not have.
numbers: Example: 100 customers at $1,000/month, 10 new per month, flat for six months, so churn is 10%.

### POD-0345#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:09:39
claim: At $1,000 per month, 80% margin and 10% churn, each customer is worth $8,000 gross profit, so a $1,000 acquisition cost is an 8:1 return and the move is to do more of it.
detail: If one channel is maxed out, the $8,000 is the budget available to buy customers elsewhere.
numbers: $1,000 x 80% / 10% = $8,000 lifetime gross profit; CAC $1,000.

### POD-0345#06 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: money-models | fw: Payback Period | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:11:43
claim: A $10 per month product with a 10-year life has 6:1 on paper ($1,200 vs $200 CAC) yet takes 20 months to pay back, which wrecks cash flow.
detail: Digital product with near-total gross profit. The ratio looks strong, but the money arrives slowly, which is why such companies raise outside capital. His answer is to design the offers so the customer pays back the acquisition cost sooner (the subject of his Money Models book).
numbers: $10/month x 120 months = $1,200; CAC $200; payback 20 months.

### POD-0345#07 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:49
claim: Cut CAC and collect prepayment up front (for example a first year at half price) so the first transaction already covers acquisition cost.
detail: Lowering CAC from $200 to $50 shortens payback to five months; selling the first year at 50% off brings $60 in cash up front against $50 of acquisition cost, leaving $10 positive on day one. Recurring billing at full price starts 12 months later.
numbers: CAC $200 to $50; first year at 50% off = $60 collected up front; +$10 net cash on the first transaction.
