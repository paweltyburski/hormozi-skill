### YT-k5-57282taI#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: other | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:50
claim: Trusting someone means making yourself punishable by them; four types arise from who is at risk and who does the punishing.
detail: Two axes: who takes the risk (you or the other person) and who punishes (the person or the environment). Examples: handing over a secret or information (the person could punish you), and giving or relying on someone's word, such as asking them to collect your child, or following their advice (reality punishes if they fail). Being trustworthy is the mirror case, when you hold the knife.

### YT-k5-57282taI#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: other | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:20
claim: Extend trust only if the person has a track record of not using what they were given and betraying you costs them more than protecting you.
detail: Two questions: has the person held earlier disclosures without using them, and is betrayal a bad deal for them. Trust is worth risking because the ceiling of a relationship depends on shared context, and more disclosure lets them serve you better. Short-term incentives (winning a fight) can tempt people to break it, so judge material disclosures one at a time.

### YT-k5-57282taI#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: other | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:57
claim: To be trustworthy, apply zero punishment: respond positively when something is shared and never use it against the person later.
detail: Trust builds through small disclosures that go unpunished; one betrayal erases years of reward. Thank people for sharing, keep it private, and do not pass it to others who may harm them. Reputation for loose lips ends trust.

### YT-k5-57282taI#04 | TACTIC | topic: other | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:34
claim: When asked "why don't you trust me?", answer with specific past instances in which a disclosure was used against you.
detail: Defining trust as punishability lets you point to concrete events instead of a feeling. The benefits of being trustworthy: people entrust you with more context, so you gain influence, and when your advice or word proves good they follow future advice more. Breaking your word for short-term gain effectively ends the relationship even if it lingers visibly.
