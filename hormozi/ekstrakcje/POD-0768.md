### POD-0768#01 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: scaling-constraints | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Theory of Constraints | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:49
claim: Instead of asking how to grow, ask "what would kill my business fastest?", rank the answers, and put most of your time on the top ones.
detail: A team exercise for stuck owners. Write down everything that could end the business and order it by speed of death; you can probably look at the top three. Hormozi suggests spending about 85% of time on those and delegating the rest. Avoiding the things that kill you creates a business that endures, and enduring businesses tend to keep growing.
numbers: Probably the top three threats; about 85% of the owner's time.
### POD-0768#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:45
claim: Teams, and owners, default to the easiest problems for fast wins, while the items that threaten the business most are usually the hardest, so a leader must assign resources to priorities.
detail: A threat that would take years to kill the company but is easy to fix gets done first, and weeks go by without touching the top item. Hormozi says this is not malice but human nature. He cites that most billionaires are self-made with the same time and little money, so the difference is how resources are prioritised and invested.

### POD-0768#03 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: management-ops | also: sales-team | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:34
claim: When someone gives a deadline, ask why it cannot be done by the end of today; he says that about two out of three times people answer they could do it today.
detail: In his example, the deadline was next Monday simply because that was conveniently when the next meeting was, not because of the real effort. If they cannot, the question lets you offer help clearing obstacles. In a small department he moved from a weekly meeting to a check-in four hours later; the person had been working on other things, so Hormozi made this the only task and the rest explicitly deprioritised, which relieved the person.
numbers: About 2 of 3 say they can do it today; follow-up meeting after 4 hours.
### POD-0768#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:06:24
claim: Five-step efficiency cycle (credited to Elon Musk): question the requirements, delete, optimise the mix, pull up timelines, then automate.
detail: Question why each task exists and how many resources it takes. Delete everything that is not a priority, and if unsure delete it, since it can be added back; the usual defence is "I was told to". Optimise by moving effort toward what gives the highest return. Shorten timelines. Finally automate, because the speed-up forces people to build templates and processes. Applies to a sales call, a video or a department.

### POD-0768#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: management-ops | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:06:24
claim: Colour grading consumed about a third of a video team's time, though nobody believed it drove a third of the video's success.
detail: He asked why it was done, how they know it raises views, and how much it costs, then asked whether it would rank first among the ways to kill a video. It would not, so the time should move to the video's big idea, hook and visual props. Reallocating resources beats paying for a new hire.
numbers: About one third of editor time spent on colour grading (as stated).

### POD-0768#06 | EXAMPLE | topic: management-ops | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:08:19
claim: With a 20-person media team and even effort across platforms, if two sources bring about 80% of views, shift about 80% of effort to them.
detail: Offered as a hypothetical, to show optimising the mix after deleting.
numbers: 20-person team; 80% of returns from two sources.

### POD-0768#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:06
claim: Moving from weekly to twice-daily check-ins gives about 20 deadlines a week instead of one and lets a team out-execute rivals many times over.
detail: He compares a company growing 20% a year with a rival growing 5x and puts that at 25 times faster (his arithmetic). Frequent check-ins are not micromanaging when people can see how their work links to results. Burnout comes from boredom without priorities or from heavy effort that gets no use.
numbers: 7 days to 4-5 hours per deadline; 1 versus about 20 deadlines per week; 7x speed from end-of-week to end-of-day.

### POD-0768#08 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: hiring-talent | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:07
claim: When asked to pull a deadline forward, a skill gap is solved by asking who else can do it faster and having that person teach the rest.
detail: Managers often skip translating priorities into actions and assume the report can break the task down. If the answer is that they cannot, check the five causes (did not know, how, when, not motivated, blocked) from the management diamond and remove blockers. Teaching raises the speed of all ten editors from one-in-a-day to all-in-a-day, then ask who could do it in four hours.
numbers: Example team of 10 editors; target of one day, then four hours.

### POD-0768#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:45
claim: Adding headcount guarantees communication cost and hierarchy, so ask first whether the same output can come from existing people through priorities.
detail: Owners debate a $5,000 monthly software subscription for months yet hire a $60,000 person without asking whether another route exists. Without close contact with a department, people generate work for themselves that does not help the business.
numbers: $5,000/month software versus $60,000 hire.

### POD-0768#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:18
claim: High demands are not overbearing if you clear the path and supply the skills, and managers should tell people why their task matters, since they often lack the full context.
detail: He argues people remember demanding leaders as the ones they did their best work under. Employees two layers down may know priorities only from a quarterly slide. Telling someone the job is important changes how they prioritise.
