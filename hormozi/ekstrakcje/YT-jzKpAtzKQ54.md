### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#01 | CASE | topic: unit-economics | also: verticals | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:01:32
claim: An ad agency bragging of a 5:1 return was earning zero gross profit per client once ad spend and account-manager cost were counted.
detail: The agency charged $2,000 a month, clients stayed five months (20% churn, so $2,000 / 0.20 = $10,000 revenue), and it counted $2,000 to acquire against $10,000 value. But $1,000 a month of the fee went to ad spend and each rep, paid $5,000, handled only five accounts, another $1,000 per account. Nothing was left. Hormozi found it by asking for the lifetime gross profit breakdown before looking at payroll.
numbers: Price $2,000/month; stay 5 months; ad spend $1,000/month; rep $5,000/month / 5 accounts = $1,000 per account; gross profit $0.
applies_to: Agencies and services where fees wrap in pass-through spend

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:40
claim: Exclude fixed costs (rent, non-delivery staff) from gross profit; only costs that rise with each extra unit belong there.
detail: Test: if one more unit sells, the extra gross profit goes to the owner or into reinvestment. Costs that exist regardless, such as rent or people unrelated to delivery, stay out of the figure.

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#03 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:58
claim: Service firms estimate delivery cost per customer as delivery payroll divided by customers, and model it as a step function.
detail: Service owners often treat all revenue as profit when five people deliver for 20 customers. Divide delivery payroll by customers, then model the cost at scale: if one more hire is needed for every set number of customers, each delivery person has a fixed maximum and the cost steps up. Physical-product owners usually see this faster (book printed for $10 and sold for $20 leaves $10).
numbers: Example: 5 people on payroll, 20 customers; payroll divided by 20 is cost of delivery per customer.

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#04 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:10
claim: Calculate CAC over a full year, not a month, to smooth swings from campaigns and sales days.
detail: Add a year of marketing and sales payroll, commissions and ad spend, divide by customers won. He calls it back-of-napkin but realistic. Monthly or weekly is fine for tracking.
numbers: Monthly example: $10,000 ads + $10,000 sales and marketing payroll = $20,000 / 20 customers = $1,000 CAC.

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#05 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:44
claim: A young business can still get a hypothetical lifetime value from one month of data on how many starting customers are left after 30 days.
detail: Take customers at the start of the month and count how many remain 30 days later; the lost share is churn, and price (or gross profit) divided by churn gives the lifetime figure.
numbers: 10 customers at start, 9 still there = 10% churn = 10 months average stay.
applies_to: Businesses under about six months old

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#06 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: verticals | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:06:16
claim: Starbucks and a local cookie promotion show how a huge LTV:CAC lets a capital-heavy chain grow with its own money.
detail: He cites Starbucks customer lifetime value of about $14,000 (as spoken, $14,099) from $5-$6 drinks over years, and says it has about 38,000 stores, nearly all company-owned. From a cookie-company campaign he ran: a free cookie with a drink, leads under $1, and one in ten leads walked in, so about $10 per visit. Against a $14,000 value that is far above 100:1.
numbers: Lead under $1; 1 in 10 leads visited = about $10 per visitor; against $14,000 LTV about 1,400:1.
applies_to: Local food and beverage

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: paid-ads, unit-economics | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:08:01
claim: A very high LTV:CAC lets you outbid every competitor for attention, which he calls an ethical moat.
detail: As you grow you hit colder audiences and costlier channels, so CAC rises. If the ratio is huge, CAC can double or triple and you still profit, so you can enter new channels and colder audiences. Ad platforms are auctions, so whoever can pay the most per click or lead wins the eyeballs. Business owners who do not know the figures stall around one to three million a year because they cannot say how much to spend or when it comes back.
numbers: Starbucks illustration: CAC rising from $10 to $50 would still be a very good ratio.

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#09 | CASE | topic: money-models | also: unit-economics | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:01
claim: A hair-extension educator had two front ends; he told her to advertise only the cheap one and make the coaching its back end, lifting LTV:CAC from about 30:1 to 75:1.
detail: Course for stylists at $2,000 cost about $67 to acquire (roughly 30:1); business coaching at $15,000 cost about $2,000 per sale (roughly 7:1) with fewer buyers. Since more people want the hair course and some also buy coaching, stop advertising coaching, sell the course first and ascend buyers into coaching, which then costs nothing to acquire. At a 20% ascension, $15,000 x 20% = $3,000 added to the guaranteed $2,000.
numbers: Course $2,000, CAC about $67 (about 30:1). Coaching $15,000, CAC $2,000 (about 7:1). With 20% ascending, LTV $5,000 and ratio 75:1.

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: money-models | also: paid-ads | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:55
claim: With several offers, advertise the one with the best LTV:CAC and stack the others behind it, unless the total addressable market is tiny.
detail: Which offer to advertise is a math question, not opinion. The saturation caveat rarely applies: selling 100 customers in a market of a million is far from saturation.
applies_to: Owners with multiple products or front ends

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#12 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: product-delivery, strategy-positioning | fw: LTGP:CAC | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:17:08
claim: A pool designer was told to focus on modular pools, which had six or seven variables, better margin, faster cycle and a larger market than resort pools.
detail: His four lines were commercial, resort, residential and modular pools. Modular was easiest to deliver, quickest from yes to installation, and had more buyers. Hormozi framed it as a math problem: higher LTV:CAC, larger market and faster loop, versus custom one-offs that are hard to scale. Find the "shower door" in your business: cheap, widely wanted, immediately understood, fast to deliver.

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads, sales-process | fw: CRO | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:23:58
claim: Build LTV from the back of the funnel forward, but cut CAC from the front of the funnel backward, because early-stage rates have more room to improve.
detail: For LTV start with the most expensive offer and work backwards, stacking profit. For CAC work front to back: raising click-through from 1% to 3% is a tripling, while a show rate of 60% or a close rate of 35% cannot triple. Exception: a clear outlier, such as a 5% show rate when the industry runs 60%, is a 12x gain to take first. Usually the biggest gains come from ads, offer, headline and team availability; with e-commerce or sales teams, from page CRO.
numbers: CTR 1% to 3%; show rate 5% vs 60% industry average (12x).

### YT-jzKpAtzKQ54#16 | TACTIC | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery | fw: Crazy Eight | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:51
claim: When applying the LTV levers to a product, pick the one that creates the least operational drag.
detail: For a book: raise price, buy printing volume or split stock across warehouses to cut shipping (price and cost first), cross-sell a second book, sell a summary booklet as the downsell. Asking existing buyers to buy a second copy takes far less work than writing a second book.
