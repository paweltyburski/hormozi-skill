### LS--HbwUdqUcEs#01 | DECISION_RULE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:44
claim: Hormozi's "minimum rule of 20%": changing a working process where people are involved typically costs about 20% of output at first (his observation), so a change should not be taken for a small possible gain such as 5%.
detail: Change has a fixed cost and an uncertain reward: from his own observation, changing a script, page or onboarding where people are involved typically drops output about 20% at first (retraining, practice, knock-on effects). Afterwards it may stay down, fall further, or recover to a slightly higher baseline (his illustration: +5%). Taking a guaranteed 20% decrease for a possible 5% increase is a bet he says not to take, though owners (himself included) keep making it. Later (00:23:03) he mentions an investor frame called ICE.

### LS--HbwUdqUcEs#02 | BENCHMARK | topic: management-ops | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:38
claim: A process left unchanged tends to improve on its own by roughly 1-3% month over month as staff get more skilled.
detail: Argument for limiting yourself to one or two material bets a year: if nothing is changed, people keep getting better at their jobs, so output creeps up 1-3% a month. Restricting the number of big changes forces each one to be worth it. He says his most profitable periods were when the business was boring and the team repeated the same work, not when he was changing the most. Strategy should start from the goal ("what do I want to eat"), not from what is already in the fridge.
