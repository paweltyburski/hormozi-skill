### POD-0839#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:06
claim: Selling to very small businesses forces a fork: templatized, high-volume and cheap, or custom and priced for a stable up-market client.
detail: Most firms end up in the middle: custom promises without scalability, so customers pay little yet expect fully custom work and get upset. A tech-first approach is what the cheap route needs.

### POD-0839#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:48
claim: Very small businesses are volatile, and in bad months they cut costs, including yours.
detail: Easy to acquire in large numbers, hard to keep. If they churn, fixing churn tactics and activation points helps but may miss the real cause: this avatar will not stay. Going up market means pricing to the client's worst month.

### POD-0839#03 | BENCHMARK | topic: retention-ltv | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:03:39

detail: Hormozi says he would double-check the figure; it is best in class for this segment. It works because adding a customer costs little.
numbers: About 50-60% retention at month 12 (to be confirmed).

### POD-0839#04 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:29
claim: An acquaintance's agency served small businesses at $200-$300 a month and kept clients about 38 months.
detail: He built the whole cost base around cheap delivery of nuisance tasks (review optimisation, replying to DMs) that owners know they should do. Price was low enough that one extra customer a year paid for it, so cancelling felt stupid. Lifetime value was about $11,400 ($300 x 38).
numbers: $200-$300/month; average stay 38 months; LTV about $11,400.

### POD-0839#05 | BENCHMARK | topic: pricing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:04
claim: Most agencies selling to small businesses typically charge $1,000-$3,500 a month; they can sell it, but cannot keep it.
detail: Hormozi's rough scale check: 1,000 sales a month at about $11,000 LTV would be a $100M-plus business. He says that at these prices small business customers typically leave around three to four months later, so teams feel they only onboard new customers.
numbers: Typical $1,000-$3,500/month; churn around 3-4 months; 1,000 sales/month x about $11,000.
### POD-0839#06 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:14
claim: Gross margin steps from 80% to 90% to 95% each double the operating leverage.
detail: Cost share 20% to 10% is half the cost per dollar; 95% against 80% gives four times the leverage. Hormozi wants at least 80% for services and prefers 90-95%. Templatized low-price versions can hold margin with one rep on 300-1,000 accounts; custom ones one rep per about five.
numbers: Minimum 80% gross margin for services, preferred 90-95%; 80 to 90% = 2x leverage; 80 to 95% = 4x; one rep per 300-1,000 accounts (templated) vs about 5 (custom).

### POD-0839#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:02
claim: Scaling ads into a churn problem raises CAC, lowers margin and makes volatility painful.
detail: Colder markets cost more and are harder customers. A 20% dip is small at 10 sales a month, but at 100 sales it leaves three or four reps idle; 40% (100 to 60) is serious when payroll was built on constant selling.

### POD-0839#08 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: strategy-positioning | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:54
claim: If your team feels like all it does is onboard people who leave in three or four months, it's probably because your model, services and prices do not match the avatar.
detail: The decision: either change services and price, or change the avatar (and maybe also price). Up market means custom and more money; down market means cheaper, templated, with high gross margin kept.
