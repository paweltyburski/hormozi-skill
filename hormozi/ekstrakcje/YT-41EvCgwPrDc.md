### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#02 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv | fw: Big Head Long Tail | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:03:05
claim: Split what you sell into one-time value and consumable value, price the first as a larger up-front fee and the second as a smaller recurring charge.
detail: Information businesses carry a lot of one-time value (a course) and little recurring value, so charging a flat monthly price for the course makes customers feel the value has gone once learned, which is why they cancel. Recurring things worth billing monthly include community, accountability or support, hosting or software, and ongoing ad help. Matching price to each kind of value keeps the price-to-value gap positive for longer.
numbers: Illustrative split: about $5,000 up front, then $200-$300 per month for the consumable parts.
applies_to: Education, information and media businesses

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#03 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing, retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:13
claim: Billing cadence alone changes churn sharply: roughly 10.7% a month on monthly billing, 5% on quarterly, 2% on annual (cited from a ProfitWell study).
detail: Across memberships, extending the billing interval cuts monthly-equivalent churn. Using price divided by churn for a $100/month price: monthly billing gives lifetime value near $950, quarterly (billed $300) about $2,000, annual (billed $1,200) about $5,000. Roughly five times the value per customer from the same product.
numbers: Churn 10.7% / 5% / 2% per month-equivalent; LTV at $100/month about $950 / $2,000 / $5,000 (extrapolated to monthly).

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:11:56
claim: Churn rises with billing frequency because each charge triggers a look back over only the period since the last charge.
detail: Customers judge "what have you done for me lately" across the window between bills. With monthly billing a great month followed by a weak one leads to cancelling; with annual billing the whole year, good months and bad, sits in the window and the deal looks strong. So lengthen the window as far as the customer will accept. He adds that people who can pay up front may simply be stronger buyers, so cadence is not the only cause.
numbers: Annual renewal said to be about five times more likely than monthly retention.

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:10:12
claim: Worked comparison of $12,000 sold as $1,000 a month versus $5,000 paid up front for the year.
detail: The monthly-billed version is typically cancelled around month four or five, so its customers are worth only $4,000-$5,000. The $5,000 up-front version may close at the same rate because the price feels lower, and about 80% renew, equal to 2% monthly churn. He computes monthly equivalent $416 times 50 (1 / 2%) as roughly $20,800 lifetime. Renewals also stack on new sales each year, which is what makes the business compound. He would take the lower annual price.
numbers: $12,000 at $1,000/month: lifetime about $4,000-$5,000. $5,000 annual at 2% churn: about $20,800; about 80 of 100 renew after a year.

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#06 | EVOLUTION | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: updates | ts: 00:13:59
claim: He used to preach selling for as much as possible and doesn't believe that as much anymore; he would rather pitch something people will keep paying for.
detail: Earlier he obsessed over making people stick; now he bills at the level a customer will keep paying on their worst month, not the most they will agree to today. The bi-directional price and conversion effect still applies (more may buy at $1,000 down than $5,000 up front), but personally he would rather have the much higher-quality customer in the most optimized billing cycle.
### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#07 | EVOLUTION | topic: strategy-positioning | also: retention-ltv, verticals | fw: - | novelty: updates | ts: 00:14:39
claim: If he started again he would pick products people already do not cancel, instead of engineering stickiness into a product that people drop.
detail: His early career went into getting people to pay repeatedly; he now says to look for categories with structurally long customer lives and enter those. Examples of rarely cancelled categories: alarm systems, insurance, payment processing, banking, cable, mobile phone service. Gym memberships are cancelled constantly, and he calls that structural because people do not want to work out.
applies_to: Choosing a business or product line

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#08 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: pricing | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:51
claim: Ask whether your growth depends only on selling more new customers every month, which makes the business collapse when one channel dies.
detail: He says the vast majority of small businesses stay poor because they think growth only means more customers sold each month. If the YouTube channel, ad account or domain deliverability (for outreach) breaks, a business whose customers do not plan to keep paying dies immediately. Find products customers keep buying, sell them at a price they will keep paying, and extend the look-back window.
### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: money-models | fw: Four Ways to Display a Discount | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:24:45
claim: A typical setup is annual billing at about 16% below monthly (buy 10, get two free), with quarterly as an option if a full prepay is too heavy for buyers.
detail: Annual customers might have about five times the lifetime value, which after the 16% discount would probably come closer to 4.5x. Most businesses have monthly plus quarterly or annual; pure quarterly can be a middle ground. Beyond the discount, remove bad things and add status: a named VIP tier tied to the annual plan, with two or three perks such as priority session times, no waiting in line or a special session.
numbers: Annual typically at 16% below monthly; 5x LTV less 16% discount is probably closer to 4.5x.
### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: pricing, money-models | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA) | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:26:42
claim: Annual billing also front-loads a year of cash, so even half the conversion pays for much more acquisition.
detail: If half as many customers choose annual but pay a year at once, upfront cash is still several times the monthly-billed version, which lets you bid harder for customers.
numbers: Half the conversions at 10x upfront cash = 5x the cash collected upfront compared with monthly.

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: objections-closing | also: pricing, retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:26:42
claim: A contract is worth only the creditworthiness of the signer, so use it for enterprise buyers, not consumers.
detail: In the US, consumers escape contracts by cancelling cards or ghosting, and card networks tend to side with them on chargebacks. In Europe contracts hold more. Big consumer firms mostly skip contracts because buyers weigh the contract when buying but not when cancelling, so it adds friction on the front end without removing any at the back. A contract holder does stay longer, but compare that with conversion lost and spend the effort on product quality, targeting and picking a product people do not cancel.
applies_to: Consumer vs enterprise subscription sales

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#12 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:09
claim: Put a consumer price index clause in every continuity contract, and apply it only every two or three years.
detail: The clause lets you raise price with inflation without renegotiating, but he would use it less often because customers dislike price changes.
numbers: Update every 2 to 3 years, not yearly.

### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#13 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:09
claim: Keep raising price until conversion rate times price stops growing the revenue (or gross profit) per conversation, and test low to high.
detail: Compare revenue per call across price points ignoring hard costs, or use gross profit if costs matter. Raising a price over time is easier to justify (more demand, better delivery) than lowering one; if you must lower it because costs fell, existing customers should get the saving, and if you overshoot, he backsteps and refunds the difference or gives a bonus to the people who paid the higher price.
numbers: 100 calls: 10% close at $10,000 = $100,000 ($1,000/call); 30% close at $5,000 = $150,000 ($1,500/call).
### YT-41EvCgwPrDc#14 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:29:28
claim: Report results to the customer cumulatively over the whole relationship to lengthen the look-back window.
detail: Show total clicks, hours saved, pounds lost or money made since the start (Instacart shows total savings), so a bad month sits inside a good total. He admits most customers do not care much, so the lift is a few points; the bigger lever is picking a product that does not get cancelled.
numbers: Example: 35,000 website clicks delivered over six months.
