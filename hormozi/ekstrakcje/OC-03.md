### OC-03#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:48
claim: After market, the next reason an offer fails to convert is a wrong price — so pricing is the second component of the offer.
detail: Even in the right market a wrong price blocks conversion or profit. Three parts: (1) price-to-value discrepancy, (2) virtuous versus vicious cycle of price, (3) correlation of price and perceived value. The fix is usually to raise value and price, not lower price.

### OC-03#02 | FRAMEWORK | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv | fw: Price to Value Discrepancy | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:46
claim: People buy, and keep buying, only while the value they perceive stays above the price; the moment value matches or falls below price, they cancel or never come back.
detail: When value equals or drops below price, recurring customers cancel and one-time buyers give no repeat purchases or referrals. Most businesses sit at or below that line, so they get one sale per customer and must keep finding new ones. Two ways to widen the gap: (1) lower price, "a one-way ticket to failure"; (2) raise value, above all perceived value, via niching, positioning, risk reversal and other offer components. Price can only go to zero; value is unlimited.

### OC-03#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: Price to Value Discrepancy | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:43
claim: Owners who say "we're great, we just can't find customers" are probably average or mediocre, he says; repeat business requires being exceptional, not merely above average.
detail: He says average puts a business at or below the price-value line, and that getting repeat business takes being remarkable, so good that people talk about you. He adds that this is difficult, which is why most businesses do not make money.

### OC-03#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: - | fw: Price to Value Discrepancy | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:33
claim: The $50,000 burger shows that price is relative to the buyer: two hours of a hedge-fund earner's time and two hours of a minimum-wage worker's time buy "equivalent" purchases.
detail: At a smoothie shop earning $6.75/hour, Hormozi paid about $13 for a burger, about two hours of his life. A magazine story told of a hedge-fund person paying $50,000 for a burger; earning about $20–30M a year (about $25,000/hour) it also cost two hours. Lesson: prices can go almost infinitely high for the right buyer; discounting stops at zero.
numbers: $13 burger at $6.75/hour ≈ 2 hours; $50,000 burger at ~$25,000/hour ≈ 2 hours.

### OC-03#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: Price to Value Discrepancy | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:17
claim: The goal is to sell "dollars at a discount": the customer must feel that each dollar spent with you returns more than anywhere else.
detail: Buyers care about what they get per dollar. The offer should make their money feel like the best investment available, so work on value, not price cuts.
quote: You're selling dollars at a discount.

### OC-03#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Vicious Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:17
claim: The common way to set prices — copy the market average, undercut slightly, add a bit more — locks a business into a "more for less" spiral that ends in no profit.
detail: Typical steps: (1) look at the market; (2) see what everyone offers; (3) take the average price; (4) go slightly below; (5) provide what competitors do plus a little more; (6) end at more for less. Repeating 5 and 6 means giving everything for nothing; margins shrink while overhead grows. Competitors being copied are mostly broke. You cannot win by doing it better, only differently.
quote: Every one of these competitors that you're looking at is poor.

### OC-03#07 | FRAMEWORK | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv, product-delivery | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:04
claim: For clients, a higher price raises their emotional investment, perceived value and results, lowers their demandingness, and gives you more revenue per customer to spend on fulfilment — a self-reinforcing loop.
detail: Raising price: (1) emotional investment up; (2) perceived value up; (3) results better; (4) demandingness down; (5) revenue per customer for fulfilment up, so a better experience and higher perceived value. Lowering price runs it in reverse. The people who pay least are the biggest pain.

### OC-03#08 | FRAMEWORK | topic: pricing | also: sales-team, hiring-talent | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:51
claim: For the business, a higher price raises profit, self-perception, perceived impact, service levels and sales-team conviction; a lower price erodes all five.
detail: Lower price: profit falls; you see yourself as a low-cost provider; impact perception falls with worse client results; service drops (less money for people and talent); you or sales lose conviction and prospects sense it. Higher price: profit up; you are a premium category of one; clients get results; excess margin funds talent and experience; deep conviction. Past pricing mistakes can be reversed.

### OC-03#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:02
claim: Price itself communicates value: in a taste test, the same wine was rated worse, better and best purely according to the price on the bottle.
detail: Tasters got three wines labelled low, medium and high price and rated them in line with price, though all were the identical wine. He concludes that charging more also makes the product better, and suggests owners who do not do it are probably afraid of what would happen.

### OC-03#10 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: finance-exit | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:41
claim: Warren Buffett's See's Candies raised prices faster than inflation every year and turned a roughly $24M purchase into about $1B of free cash flow.
detail: Bought decades ago for about $24M (unclear in audio). Prices were raised every year by about 5–6%, a faster rate than inflation; boxed chocolates were price-inelastic, selling at $40–60 a box. About $1B net free cash flow after reinvestment, with higher profit and perceived quality from changing a number.
numbers: ~$24M purchase; ~$1B net free cash flow; price raised ~5–6% a year (faster than inflation); $40–60 per box.

### OC-03#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:41
claim: Price so far above the market that buyers must stop and conclude it cannot be the same category of solution — not slightly higher, dramatically higher.
detail: A little more expensive invites comparison; much more forces the prospect to conclude it is something different, a category of one. Checking you charge what it is worth is one way to fix an offer's value.

### OC-03#12 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery, verticals | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:41
claim: Gym Launch entered a market whose most expensive competitor charged $5,000 with a minimum price of $16,000 and a next tier of $126,000, and used the extra margin to deliver better results.
detail: Instead of being a bit pricier than the $5,000 competitor, Hormozi set a $16,000 minimum and a $126,000 next offer, far outside the market. The margin let him reinvest $5,000, a competitor's whole fee, into the client experience. Better results, higher perceived value and more referrals followed.
numbers: Competitor max $5,000; Gym Launch minimum $16,000; next tier $126,000; ~$5,000 per client reinvested.
applies_to: Licensing / B2B coaching for gym owners; any service that can reinvest margin into delivery.

### OC-03#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv, product-delivery | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:45
claim: When the client must take action to succeed (do-it-yourself or done-with-you), Hormozi says you ethically have to get them emotionally invested in the outcome, which ties to his point that a higher price raises emotional investment.
detail: The more clients invest, the more committed, so better outcomes. To help someone as much as possible you have to get them emotionally invested in the outcome, especially in do-it-yourself or done-with-you services; his earlier virtuous-cycle point is that a higher price raises that investment.
applies_to: DIY and done-with-you services, coaching, education, programs requiring client effort.

### OC-03#14 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery, retention-ltv | fw: Virtuous Cycle of Price | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:31
claim: Raising the quality of your prospects raises the quality of your product, and a higher price is the barrier to entry that raises prospect quality.
detail: A higher price filters in the most committed; more succeed, so the product is better in practice. You also have more money per client, perceived value is higher, and nobody price-compares you. More margin than anyone else is the "winning combo".
