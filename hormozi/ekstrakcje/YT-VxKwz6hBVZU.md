### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:58
claim: Separate the goose (what creates value) from the eggs (the sellable assets it produces), and plan the exit around selling eggs.
detail: Owners often try to sell the whole value-creating machine, which is valuable but not sellable. Deciding which part is which lets you build toward an exit on purpose. His example: Acquisition.com is the goose he does not really plan to sell (some goose-level transactions are possible); the portfolio companies are the eggs.
### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#02 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:00
claim: An event-space owner who sold his venues and then taught others should bundle his students into a sellable roll-up.
detail: He rented a venue, ran 2-3 events a week at a day rate above rent, and sold three locations, then started an education business. Advice: standardise students early on colours, systems and prices, then offer the top 10% a chance to roll up and sell in groups, while the teaching business keeps feeding new students. That is the work a roll-up buyer would do, done in advance.
numbers: Groups of 20 to 50 students per roll-up; top 10% of students; one roll-up every other year.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#03 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:03:00
claim: A teacher of Amazon-store building has an unsellable education business (key-man risk, volatile acquisition), but his students' faceless stores can be sold.
detail: His coaching was not recurring or sticky and depended on his face. Advice: bundle the students' stores into one asset. Faceless, turnkey stores let an investor compute cost, yield and return, so building more of the sellable thing takes less effort than fixing the unsellable one.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#04 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:08
claim: Two questions locate what is sellable: does anything depend on you, and which part of your business or your customers' businesses is sticky or recurring without needing a face?
detail: Apply the second question at three levels: customers, service lines and the whole company. Owner dependence lowers value.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:07
claim: Micro gyms and personal-training studios rarely sell because of key-man risk, whereas franchise restaurants and accounting firms have clear exit markets.
detail: Helping Subway-style chains would give an exit path because franchises trade constantly; accounting firms sell thanks to high revenue retention. The customer segment you serve shapes what you can eventually roll up.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#06 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:18
claim: Check whether your business type is sellable by searching for M&A activity in the category and comparing those deals with your company.
detail: Search, for example, coaching business M&A activity. Little activity suggests no market. If there is some, find what differs from yours and orient the model toward what buyers have shown they like. Treat your company as a product and the acquirer as the customer.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#07 | DEFINITION | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:07:14
claim: A franchise exists when name, system and fee are all present; doing all three without complying is an illegal franchise.
detail: US rule as he states it. Business-help firms pick two: name and system without a fee; name and fee without systems (CrossFit); or systems and fee without a unified name, which Gym Launch did.
applies_to: US companies teaching others how to run a specific type of business.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#08 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:14
claim: Gym Launch was sellable because the key man was gone, revenue recurred, retention was decent and there were several acquisition channels.
detail: At sale he had left delivery and ads and a leadership team ran it. Gym Launch was then much bigger than he was; today he is bigger than a company like that, which is why that route is closed for a brand tied to him.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:08:32
claim: If you are bigger than the company, you are its key-man risk, so you can only sell a minority stake or raise at the holding-company level.
detail: A minority sale lets you take chips off the table or fund an acquisition; it is a liquidity event, not an exit. He would sell perhaps 5-10% of Acquisition.com at a high valuation. Parallel: Beast Industries raises at holdco level so the channel is not sold.
numbers: Sell about 5% to 10% of a holdco at a high valuation.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#10 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:10:36
claim: A media company with a stable of talent becomes sellable only by funnelling impressions into an owned product or by selling ad space.
detail: Option one: all talent promotes a reoccurring consumable (hair gel, a dip). Option two: sell advertising space. He favours the incubator route (reach is the goose, products are the eggs). Creators' fame outruns their cash, so selling about 10% can pre-fund inventory and logistics, since a bad launch damages the only reputation they have.
numbers: Stake sold to fund the launch: about 10%.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#11 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:10
claim: An accounting-firm coach should assemble roll-ups of 10 to 20 firms and exit one every one to two years, not sell the coaching business.
detail: Accounting suits M&A: high margins, sticky clients, large lifetime values, and acquisition is the only hard part. Nobody buys the coaching business, but buyers want combined firms. The organiser keeps a slice for assembling the deal.
numbers: Roll-up of 10-20 firms; exit around $100M; organiser's share 20% to 30%.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#12 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:04
claim: Investing has a volume premium: a larger combined profit sells at a higher multiple because big money pays to do fewer deals.
detail: Firms selling alone at about 4x earnings might sell together at about 12x; after giving the organiser 30% of that, each still gets roughly double.
numbers: About 4x earnings alone vs about 12x combined; minus 30% leaves about 8.4x.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:41
claim: Local rivalries mean nothing to the eventual buyer, so collaborating with competitors usually earns more than fighting them.
detail: He told gym owners they competed with the couch, not each other (about 11% of Americans hold a gym membership, his recollection, unclear in audio). Private equity buyers of fragmented industries remove egos and merge firms for cost synergies and market share.

### YT-VxKwz6hBVZU#14 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: lead-getters | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:17:05
claim: A dental association's group-buying co-op beat its own dues, then its top 11 dentists were rolled up and sold for $120M.
detail: Each dentist spends $20-30k a year on consumables; pooled buying saved more than the membership fee. The private-equity buyer also bought the association, seeing its other members as a five-year pipeline of acquisitions. The eggs were worth more than the goose, and the buyer took both.
numbers: 700 dentists; $20,000-$30,000 yearly consumables each; top 11 sold for $120M.
