### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#01 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: A neuropathy-clinic marketing firm doing $5M a year was advised to roll up its own customers rather than just sell more services.
detail: The firm serves 160 of roughly 2,000 US neuropathy clinics, each earning $1M-$5M a year. Hormozi's view: niche B2B agencies and info businesses already do the integration work of a roll-up (shared marketing, prices, packages) and stop short of monetising it. Offer owners a bigger exit through a combined group, and take an equity slice.
numbers: 160 of ~2,000 clinics (~8%); average-based clinic revenue total ~$320M a year; clinic example $2.5M revenue, $1M profit.
applies_to: Niche B2B agencies and info businesses with many similar customers

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#02 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:11
claim: Pitch roll-up participation by comparing a single practice's sale value with its value inside a national group, and ask for 20-30% of the uplift.
detail: Physicians often want out; a practice alone might sell for about $3M, while inside a national group it might fetch about $10M. He would ask a 20-30% slice, from only 10-20 of the 160 clinics. If their combined profit reached $10M-$20M, the exit could be $100M-$200M, and the founder's reputation and pricing power change.
numbers: $3M solo vs ~$10M in group; ask 20-30%; target 10-20 clinics; $10-20M combined profit gives a $100-200M exit.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: verticals | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Sales to Fulfillment Continuum | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:45
claim: Teaching a customer to do marketing yourself works for low earners, not for high earners, who pay someone instead.
detail: For gym owners, teaching marketing and sales was weaker than for a person earning $35,000 a year, because an owner earning around a million can hire cheaper help. Hormozi would choose the licensing/coaching model again. He did not do a roll-up because group buyers for service businesses did not exist in that market; where buyers exist, a roll-up would be the model.
numbers: Example earner: $35,000 per year versus $1M per year.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#04 | CASE | topic: retention-ltv | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:43
claim: A permanent holiday-lighting installer with no revenue retention was told to add seasonal campaigns that bring buyers back.
detail: Owner: one location, local Meta ads, ~$5,000 average ticket, customers split between older and keep-up-with-neighbours buyers, aluminium track with LEDs under the soffit. Hormozi's questions: who is the customer, what is the product, ticket, channel, locations, margin, CAC. Advice: sell temporary lighting for two or three seasonal events a year, check the lights each visit, and sell upgrades as the old set ages.
numbers: Margin ~60% before costs, ~51% after (as stated); CAC $300 against a $5,000 ticket; 2-3 seasonal campaigns a year.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:09:14
claim: Reoccurring revenue (repeat purchases without a subscription) counts as reliable, and proving it with data raises company value.
detail: Recurring means a membership or subscription; reoccurring means customers buy again regularly, as with Coca-Cola. A lighting firm can show that, say, half its customers buy something every year (upgrade, repair, seasonal lights), and that data makes the business more valuable.
numbers: Illustrative target: 1 in 2 customers buys again each year.

claim: A Missouri house flipper dependent on foreclosures should learn paid acquisition and study the biggest wholesaler before expanding geographically.
detail: Flipper: $15M of house sales, $3.7M project profit, $2.5M after team costs; no advertising; 95% foreclosures plus word of mouth; market covered within a four-hour drive; eight years in. ### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#07 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:38
claim: Many competitors in a market means many buyers, so long as you become number one.
detail: When told "my market has lots of competition" he replies that this shows demand. He also tells the flipper not to restart the learning curve in a new business after eight years of paid-off ignorance. Before reinventing, look at the biggest national version of your business: it probably has spare margin from vertical integration, and its owners are often gone, leaving managers who run it poorly.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#08 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:33:11
claim: To plan growth, study the biggest version of your business, count the steps between you and them, and treat your skill gaps as the opportunity.
detail: Ask what they did, what their model and brand look like, whether they are vertically integrated, and what skills you lack. The energy-drink example: Red Bull outsources everything except marketing and distribution, so for a ready-to-drink company those two matter most.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#09 | CASE | topic: retention-ltv | also: sales-process | fw: Revenue Churn | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:16:54
claim: A virtual CPA firm keeping about 70% of revenue was told its onboarding and communication, not the product, drive cancellations.
detail: Firm: serves businesses in 50 states, $2.5M last year heading to about $3M, profit $900K heading to ~$1.1M (about 30% margins on expensive labour), minimum fee $1,950 per quarter. Hormozi doubted "we already taught them the tricks" as the reason for leaving. He suspected a gap between the sale and the first weeks, plus weak touch points.
numbers: Revenue retention ~70%, target above 80%; minimum fee $1,950 per quarter.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: objections-closing | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:00
claim: What customers say when leaving ("I got the value already") is usually not the true reason.
detail: Leavers give a socially easy line. Look at the experience: gap between what was sold and the first days and weeks, and the cadence of contact. Clients who barely feel anything happening assume the hidden work is sloppy too.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#11 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: GROWTH Onboarding | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:10
claim: Push frequent status messages about work done on the client's behalf, so they see that you are working and cannot be undercut on price.
detail: Send short notes with no action required: what ran today, what moved to the next step, then another two days later. Capture detailed notes on each client in the sales handoff so they do not feel a stranger is handling their money. If clients cannot see work, they pick the cheaper bidder; if they see effort, they assume you are safe.
numbers: Example: a client paying $8,000 a year for tax work would walk if the firm clearly knew nothing about their business.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: Cohort Analysis | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:25:57
claim: A software business is a product business: retention decides everything, and marketing only serves to improve the product until growth is passive.
detail: Asked by a former high-ticket-education marketer with a technical co-founder, Hormozi says his own early mistake was assuming his experience in marketing made him a marketing company. If customers do not stay, marketing is irrelevant. Once the product retains, push hard.

### YT-bgBIO6nZawg#14 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Cohort Analysis | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:27:00
claim: Find the cohort that never churns, profile it by psychographics, demographics, geography and activity, then aim marketing and onboarding at those traits.
detail: Example pattern: the first six months lose most customers while the retained group's curve is flat. Study who they are, where they are, what they believe and what they did. Use ads to call out these beliefs, show up where they are, and steer new users to repeat what successful ones did. Early on, spend on marketing mainly for feedback on who sticks.
numbers: Example: ~80% churn in six months, then a flat curve for the remaining ~20%.
