### LC-04#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: outreach, content-brand, paid-ads | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: There are only four ways one person can let others know about anything: warm outreach, cold outreach, posting content, and running paid ads — the 2×2 of audience (warm/cold) and communication (one-to-one/one-to-many).
detail: One-to-one to warm = warm outreach; one-to-one to cold = cold outreach; one-to-many to warm = posting content; one-to-many to cold = paid ads. Hormozi calls it the "value equation" of the Leads book. Since these are the only four things you can do yourself, diagnosing a lead shortage becomes simple (not easy): which of the four are you doing, and how much? Leveraging other people comes later and builds on these. The course goes from simplest (warm outreach) to most complex.

### LC-04#02 | DEFINITION | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: - | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: A warm audience is anyone who knows you and has given you permission to contact them; a cold audience is strangers who have not — other people's audiences reached through platforms.
detail: Warm: friends, family, followers, current and past customers, contacts. The relationship runs one way — they must know you, not you them; the more you advertise, the more people know you without you knowing them. At the start, the easiest warm audience is people you also know. Cold: lists and attention belonging to someone else. Facebook users gave Facebook permission, not you, so you use it as a proxy; once they opt in they become your lead and warm up.

### LC-04#03 | DEFINITION | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: outreach | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:53
claim: Outbound means first contact with a prospect happens in a private, one-to-one channel; inbound means it happens publicly — and an automated email blast is still outbound.
detail: Private: voicemail, email, text, DM. Public: billboards, podcasts, posts, ads, any content. Someone who sees a billboard and calls is still inbound; what counts is where first contact happened. A blast to a list, or cold email, is one-to-one outbound — automation just makes it fast.

### LC-04#05 | CASE | topic: outreach | also: verticals, retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:35
claim: Hormozi's first business came from warm outreach: messaging every contact about a free 12-week online transformation won six clients, who all later converted to paying — about $4,000 a month.
detail: The offer: full coaching (nutrition, training, grocery lists) for 12 weeks online, free to him, in exchange for a $500–1,000 donation to a charity of their choice — skin in the game (plain free would also have worked). He asked every phone and platform contact "do you know anybody who'd be interested?" Six said yes, tried hard because they knew him, and had his cell number. The business needed only an LLC, a bank account, payment processing and a way to communicate — the last mattered most. He asked for referrals; after 12 weeks he asked them to pay as he went full-time, and all converted, replacing his consulting income.
numbers: 6 clients; $500–1,000 donation; 12 weeks; ~$4,000/month after conversion.

### LC-04#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: outreach | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Core Four | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:19
claim: Warm outreach (one-to-one with people who know you) is the cheapest and most reliable way to find customers, and for a first business he says it might be the most profitable and easiest start; done properly it need not feel "icky".
detail: Do it, make money, then "graduate" to other methods; with skills and money you can start with any of the four. MLMs gave approaching friends a bad name by not teaching people to behave like normal humans; he says the method below shows how to approach people for business without being a weirdo.

### LC-04#07 | FRAMEWORK | topic: outreach | also: - | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:19
claim: Warm outreach runs in eight steps: get your list, pick a platform, personalise, reach out to 100 people a day (three times each), use ACA, make the offer by asking who they know, make the easiest offer in the world, then start again at the top.
detail: After the steps come raising prices from free to full and keeping the list warm. Each step is detailed in its own atom.

### LC-04#08 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: - | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:19
claim: Step 1 — build your list: put every phone contact, social follower and email contact from every account you have used into one sheet; most people have 1,000–3,000.
detail: "Everyone has a list" — even 200 Instagram followers count. Count the names; that is your starting lead count. With no contacts at all, go where attention is and build some. Hormozi did this again a decade later when starting his software company.
numbers: Typical person has contacted ~1,000–3,000 people.

### LC-04#09 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: - | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:37
claim: Step 2 — start on the platform where you have the most contacts; you will work through all of them, and someone reachable on two platforms is almost two leads.
detail: Platforms change; people paying attention does not. People have communication preferences — Hormozi ignores calls but answers texts — so using several channels for one person is normal and raises response.

### LC-04#10 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: - | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:23
claim: Step 3 — personalise: spend about 30 seconds finding what is new in the person's life and open by asking about it, not by pitching.
detail: Check their social media and business, search their name, then ask: "saw you had a baby / moved cities / played guitar this weekend — how was it?" Dale Carnegie's first rule: people love talking about themselves.
numbers: ~30 seconds research per contact.

### LC-04#11 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: operator-mindset | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:40
claim: Step 4 — reach out to 100 people every day by any channel, contacting each up to three times, as you would if you genuinely wanted to reach a friend.
detail: Email, calls, DMs, SMS, voicemail. Trying to reach your mom, you would text, call, and text again hours later; acting like you want to talk to them, not working them like a lead, gets human responses. You are only asking about them, not selling. On rejection fear: the first message takes ages, the second two minutes, the third 20 seconds — "everything must be hard before it can be easy."
numbers: 100 reach-outs/day; 3 attempts per person.

### LC-04#12 | SCRIPT | topic: outreach | also: sales-process | fw: ACA | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:27
claim: Step 5 — when they reply, use ACA: Acknowledge what they said, Compliment them (tied to a positive trait), then Ask a question that moves toward your offer.
detail: Acknowledge: restate it to show you listened (mirroring, not weirdly) — giving attention and approval, which with affection are what reward people. Compliment: praise it and label a trait (two kids plus full-time accounting → "super mom, so hard-working"); later your offer can be tied to that trait, which people act to keep. Ask: steer toward what you sell — therapy: "do you get time for yourself?"; fitness: "do you get workouts in? how do you prep food?"; cleaning: "does anyone help around the house?" You will be bad at first and better after a thousand reps. Listening is itself value — people pay therapists about $100/hour for it — and builds liking and trust, which drive buying.
quote: Acknowledge, compliment, ask the next question.

### LC-04#13 | SCRIPT | topic: outreach | also: offers, lead-getters | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:20
claim: Step 6 — make the offer by asking whether they know anyone who would want it, not by pitching them; this avoids the "ick" while covering all four value-equation elements.
detail: Prefer offering the lead magnet. Structure (in your own words, over one to three messages): (1) "Do you know anyone who [struggle] and wants [dream outcome] in [time]?" (2) "I'm taking five case studies free — that's all I can handle" (implied scarcity). (3) "I want testimonials" (reason why). (4) "I help them get [outcome] without [what they hate]; I guarantee it, or I work with them until they do." (5) Two proof stories: Sarah with the same struggle (ideal prospect) and Tom on his first try (it's easy; Hormozi cited a client who spoke no English yet followed the meal plan). (6) "Does anyone you like come to mind?" — if no, "anyone you hate?" breaks the ice. Usually they say they are interested themselves. You are asking about friends and offering something free, so nobody feels pitched.

### LC-04#14 | SCRIPT | topic: outreach | also: offers | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:03
claim: Short offer version: "I help [ideal customer] get [dream outcome] in [time] without [effort and sacrifice]. I've done this many times — here are some examples."
detail: Fill-in-the-blanks for email, text, DM, phone or in person. Example: "Do you know any business owners who'd want my book on leads? It's free." A "tell me more" means they have shown interest.

### LC-04#15 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: outreach, sales-process | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:03
claim: Eleven ways raise a prospect's perceived likelihood of achievement; weave in as many as you genuinely have without overwhelming.
detail: (1) Proof you got the result yourself. (2) Proof people like them got it — stronger, more is better. (3) Volume of happy reviews. (4) Relevant certifications, degrees, accreditations. (5) Numbers, stats, research (e.g., average portfolio company triples profit in 12 months). (6) Experts vouching — borrowed authority. (7) A new mechanism they have not failed with. (8) Celebrity endorsements. (9) A guarantee with real consequences. (10) Describing their pain precisely. (11) Live demonstration. With enough proof, nobody asks "how do I know it'll work?" — you ask "why do you think it won't?" Starting free is how you collect that proof.

### LC-04#16 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: verticals, lead-getters | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:06
claim: Turn online reviews into a physical display — Hormozi framed a couple hundred five-star reviews floor to ceiling in his gym lobby.
detail: He screenshotted reviews from Yelp, Google and Facebook, framed them and covered the lobby, so every visitor saw them. Existing proof was simply emphasised where decisions happen.
numbers: ~200+ reviews.
applies_to: Local brick-and-mortar businesses.

### LC-04#17 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: lead-getters | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:12
claim: Borrow authority only with permission: serve a respected person free and ask up front whether they will vouch for you if you do a great job.
detail: An endorsement lends brand authority when you have none. Never imply an endorsement you lack (e.g., a photo passed off as one) — customers and that person will hate you. Ask before, never spring it afterwards; it is also a good route to early testimonials.

### LC-04#18 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: objections-closing | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:12
claim: Present a different vehicle than the one the prospect already failed with — a new mechanism restores belief.
detail: Someone who failed with meal plans can believe in a counting system without a plan: "I failed with that, not this."

### LC-04#19 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: - | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:08
claim: A guarantee raises belief most when it carries real consequences for you — the bigger the "or else," the more they believe.
detail: "I guarantee it" alone is weak; prospects need to know what happens if you fail to deliver.

### LC-04#20 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: sales-process, content-brand | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:18
claim: Describe the prospect's pain so specifically they feel you are inside their head — they will then believe you have the solution.
detail: This is why founders who suffered the problem often build good businesses around it. Feel-felt-found mini-script: "I used to feel this way… then I found this… here's what happened… you can too."

### LC-04#21 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: sales-process | fw: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement | novelty: canon | ts: 00:27:10
claim: A door-to-door salesman removed trodden-in gum from Hormozi's gym turf, named nearby gyms that had bought, and sold him a $100 bottle of cleaner in about a minute.
detail: He spotted the stain, cleaned two or three "impossible" spots, then listed gyms across the street that had bought; Hormozi asked the price himself, though it probably cost ~$1 to make. If you cannot demonstrate in person, send a video or run it as an ad. For complex services, demonstrate receiving the value: an agency sends a recording of a client getting a lead call and asks "could you handle a call like this? How many?" — the prospect is choosing quantity, past doubting it works.
numbers: $100 product sold in ~60 seconds.

### LC-04#22 | SCRIPT | topic: outreach | also: offers, retention-ltv | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:25
claim: Step 7 — the easiest offer in the world: you are only taking five, so you can give full attention for brag-worthy results, free, if they use it, give feedback and leave a great review if deserved.
detail: Structure: limited number → why results will be great → free → three conditions → "Does that sound fair?" Offer it to the contact or their friend.

### LC-04#23 | PRINCIPLE | topic: outreach | also: product-delivery, operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:25
claim: Working free for your first clients gets you reps making offers, forgiveness while you are bad, practice to improve, and reviews and feedback you would not otherwise get.
detail: (1) Offering free help calms nerves about asking. (2) People forgive mistakes far more when they have not paid. (3) You improve only by doing; guinea pigs work out the kinks and you get the better deal. (4) Free clients far more readily leave reviews, testimonials and honest feedback. Drop ego: the customer decides whether you are good.

### LC-04#24 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:31:02
claim: When someone starts giving feedback, thank them immediately and ask for more, so they keep telling you the truth.
detail: Most people have been punished for feedback and hold back. Respond with curiosity ("thank you; what else could we do?"), admit fault ("I'm new, it was probably bad; will you give me the chance to do better?") — almost everyone helps someone who owns a mistake. A tip not in the book.

### LC-04#25 | PRINCIPLE | topic: outreach | also: lead-getters, unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:32:07
claim: Free customers make money three ways: they convert to paying, they refer paying customers, and their testimonials attract paying customers.
detail: The return is high but delayed; take the long view.

### LC-04#26 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: outreach | fw: Hinge Method | novelty: canon | ts: 00:32:07
claim: Hinge method: when a customer names a friend, get a three-way introduction — ideally taking their phone, adding yourself, and sending a group text with a photo of you both.
detail: Trust partially transfers; the friend is likelier to reply and less likely to ghost with their friend watching. Typically they side-message "is this guy legit?", hear yes, then reply, show up and buy. Remotely, get a three-way DM or group chat intro. Far better than cold-messaging a name. Make it easy for the customer to introduce you.

### LC-04#27 | SCRIPT | topic: retention-ltv | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:33:28
claim: At the end of the free period ask if they want to continue; if not, ask what would make it worthwhile, then "if I did that, would you continue?"
detail: Yes → paying customer. No → they tell you what to fix and commit if you fix it; even if they still decline, you improve for the next person and surface hidden costs.

### LC-04#28 | DEFINITION | topic: offers | also: product-delivery | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:38
claim: Hidden costs are the non-monetary costs of using your product — effort, time, meetings, tasks — and removing them raises value at the same price.
detail: A free video editor still loses clients who must attend weekly calls, give feedback and record content. Fixes: use existing content, send a weekly report instead of meetings, ask only for approval. Hidden costs are the inverse of the value equation (effort, sacrifice, time delay); find and remove them fast.
quote: Doesn't cost them money doesn't mean it's free.

### LC-04#29 | PRINCIPLE | topic: outreach | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:35:46
claim: Step 8 — start again at the top of your list: circumstances change, so a message ignored months ago can land once they are in the market.
detail: Working 1,000–2,000 contacts a few times takes a couple of months. Someone fine in a dress 12 weeks ago now feels "fluffy" — her "taxi light" is on when your DM arrives. Timing also matters day to day: she may have meant to reply while getting kids out of the car. Reach out more times in more ways and do not take silence personally.

### LC-04#30 | DECISION_RULE | topic: pricing | also: outreach, scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:37:01
claim: Start charging once people refer others or you hit capacity: go from free to 80% off for the next five, then 60%, 40%, 20% off, then full price — and keep raising about 20% every five clients until enough still say yes and the roster is full.
detail: After full price, keep raising every five (later every 10 or 20) while capacity is constrained. Exact steps matter less than committing to the process. Reaching capacity creates genuine scarcity, which converts much better; you feel abundant, stop taking bad clients, and can honestly say you cannot go lower.
numbers: Free → 80% → 60% → 40% → 20% off → full, per 5 clients; then +20% or more every 5.

### LC-04#31 | SCRIPT | topic: pricing | also: retention-ltv | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:38:06
claim: Once paying clients arrive, tell free clients: "I have people paying me; I'd rather keep working with you — would you be willing to pay?"
detail: Capacity forces a real choice. Yes converts them; no frees a slot for someone waiting. Either way you win.

### LC-04#32 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: offers, objections-closing | fw: Pre-Paid Post Guarantee | novelty: canon | ts: 00:38:53
claim: Reserve the guarantee for customers who pay everything up front, and once they say yes, ask which they prefer: a lower price today, or a full refund of everything paid ("all your money back")
detail: Guarantees boost sales by shifting risk onto you; limiting them to prepaid buyers is believable because people who pay up front are more committed, so you can stand behind their results. This suits prepaying 12 weeks or six months of service, or bulk stock. The question (credited to Dr. Kashey) is asked only after agreement: "pay less today" means a payment plan with no guarantee, while "all your money back" means paying in full with the guarantee. The majority go for paying in full.
numbers: $2,000/month × 3 ($6,000) no guarantee vs $6,000 upfront with guarantee — majority pick upfront.

### LC-04#33 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: content-brand | fw: Nine-Word Email | novelty: canon | ts: 00:40:59
claim: Keep your worked list warm by giving value repeatedly and occasionally sending Dean Jackson's nine-word email: "Are you still looking to [desire]?"
detail: Lots of value interrupted by a small CTA ("…buy your dream home / get more leads / tone your arms?"). Since that means talking to many at once, it leads into content.

### LC-04#34 | FRAMEWORK | topic: outreach | also: operator-mindset | fw: Warm Outreach Steps | novelty: canon | ts: 00:41:51
claim: Warm outreach checklist: you, giving your first five away free, to contacts you already have, in the first four hours of every day, 100 personalised attempts a day with two follow-ups each, until you have customers.
detail: Where: messages, calls, mail, SMS. Why: customers or introductions. How: personalise, then ACA. Hormozi's note: you can get good enough at almost anything in about 20 hours of focused effort; most people spend years delaying the first hour.
numbers: First 4 hours daily; 100 attempts/day; 2 follow-ups; ~20 hours to get good enough.

### LC-04#35 | BENCHMARK | topic: outreach | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:42:37
claim: Expect roughly 1–2% of warm reach-outs to end in a purchase; about 80% will not reply, and a 2,000-contact list holds about 20–40 customers — enough for most people to replace their income.
detail: Of 100 reach-outs about 20 reply, some take the free offer, and 1–2 buy. Do not judge tiny samples: from 20 reach-outs, 1% is 0.2 people.
numbers: ~20% reply; 1–2% buy; 2,000 leads → 20–40 customers.
