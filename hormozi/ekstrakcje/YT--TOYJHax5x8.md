### YT--TOYJHax5x8#01 | BENCHMARK | topic: content-brand | also: other | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:00
claim: The first book sold 200,000+ copies in a year with no publisher, no ads and no audience at launch, spreading by word of mouth.
detail: He treats organic sharing as the test of quality: if readers do not pass it on unprompted, the product is not good enough to be worth promoting. He writes 4-5 hours a day before starting his normal working day.
numbers: 200,000+ copies in first year; 20-25 hours a week on the book; ~1,000 hours per book; the leads book went through 8 drafts, ~600 pages written to leave ~200.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:31
claim: A good book is a complete solution to one narrowly defined problem, not a list of hundreds of problems.
detail: Pick the single problem the reader should be rid of, then break the solution into three to six big buckets. Each bucket is backed by a personal experience that shaped the belief. Anything that does not serve the single point is cut. The book is meant to be the thing people recommend when someone says they suffer from that exact problem.
numbers: 3-6 solution buckets per book.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:17
claim: Story skeleton: setting, character, desire, struggle, eureka moment, victory, resolution, told on an external and an internal track.
detail: The external track is what a camera in the room would see; the internal track is what the person feels. Weave both, because a victory can feel empty. Use stories to carry the lesson behind each framework (example: a charity event where items worth under $10,000 sold for $100,000 taught scarcity).

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#04 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:05
claim: Show instead of tell, use all five senses, and include only the details that matter.
detail: Describe the scene so the reader infers it (the Stephen King style example: a pale band on a finger where a ring used to be, instead of saying "his ex-wife walked in"). The same holds for copy and ads: demonstrate rather than assert. Test a detail by asking whether changing it (deer versus buffalo on the wall) alters the story; if not, drop it. Be vivid only about things that matter.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:49
claim: Stakes and struggle are what make a story interesting; the same events become gripping once something real is at risk.
detail: His example retells ordering food when hungry; it is dull until he adds being diabetic, alone and close to passing out. Game shows put real money on the line for this reason; poker with meaningless chips would be boring.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:07:00
claim: Seven newsworthiness factors make a story interesting: recency, impact, prominence, proximity, conflict, unusualness and update potential; you don't need all of them, and having two or three in one story makes it much more interesting.
detail: Recency: tie to fresh events or trends. Impact: bridge why it matters to the reader and tie it to the action you want. Prominence: involve known people. Proximity: closeness to the reader. Conflict: tension and unresolved mystery. Unusual: set up an expectation, then break it. Updates: repeated follow-ups, mostly a news trait.
### YT--TOYJHax5x8#07 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:47
claim: For local ads, name the nearest small place in the headline rather than the big city.
detail: His example is writing the suburb (Towson, Maryland) instead of the city (Baltimore); proximity makes people more inclined to listen.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#08 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:36
claim: Place a story wherever the instructional content starts to feel heavy, then return to the framework.
detail: Book wireframe: one big problem, supporting arguments leading to it, a story for how he learned each point, and a framework per chapter. Stories give context for why the chapter's framework is useful.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: content-brand | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:09:36
claim: When the draft hits a logical inconsistency or contradicts his own experience, he goes back, adjusts the assumptions and rewrites from the start.
detail: A model must be both useful and valid. A framework is just a documented version of the mental checklist people already use; its validity is how many different circumstances it still works in. He tests it in new business situations and, if it fails, adjusts it to cover both cases, until he cannot find a way it is wrong. The larger the flaw, the more he restarts.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#10 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:05
claim: Edit from 12th-grade to third-grade language so readers spend their effort on concepts, not decoding words; use a free readability checker for instant feedback.
detail: Rules: cut any sentence whose removal keeps the meaning; prefer shorter sentences and words; remove adverbs (usually a weak verb: "ran" not "walked quickly"); use simple tenses ("wanted" not "would have liked to"). If it cannot be made simple, the writer does not understand it yet. Ideation takes the longest, then the first draft, then heavy editing.
numbers: average American reads at roughly 5th-7th grade level; target third grade.

### YT--TOYJHax5x8#11 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:43
claim: Send the draft to four to eight ideal readers who will be honest, then make a third draft and send it to a copy editor.
detail: Pick people whose opinion you value but who do not revere you. Reading rule borrowed from Stephen King: varied feedback means it works; if everyone says the same thing, change it.
numbers: 4-8 early readers.
