### POD-0548#01 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing, offers | fw: 80/20 Customer Analysis | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:23:42
claim: A generic PR company with high churn was fixed by serving only the small slice of clients who bought the top package and stayed.
detail: The company sold to small business owners and had a strong sales engine but heavy churn. About 85% of customers left within three or four months. The remaining 15% bought the most expensive package and stayed. These were founders wanting fundraising coverage. The company rebuilt itself around that niche, cold-emailed only that narrow window, got higher reply rates because the message was specific, and raised prices about 10x.
numbers: 85% of customers gone in 3-4 months; 15% bought the top package and stayed; prices raised about 10x; reply rates rose after narrowing.
applies_to: Service business with a good sales engine but high churn.

### POD-0548#02 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:39:29
claim: The same skill earns about 100x more when sold to a client ten times bigger, because the fee scales with the value created.
detail: Take conversion-rate optimisation done for e-commerce stores. A 20% lift on a store doing $1M a year adds $200K and a 10% cut is $20K. The same 20% lift on a $100M business adds $20M and a 10% cut is $2M. The work is the same; only the client changes. He ties this to "solve rich people problems, they pay better" and says the barrier is mostly belief that bigger deals are possible.
numbers: $1M store: $20K fee; $100M business: $2M fee (10% of value created from a 20% conversion lift); ratio 100x.

### POD-0548#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:32:36
claim: Talent stacking: skills multiply each other's value when combined, and each skill kind of requires the one before it.
detail: Example chain: a person good at math adds bookkeeping, then a CPA, then tax and insurance, then capital and debt markets and M&A (now a CFO), then selling (now a rainmaker). ### POD-0548#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:45:59
claim: Leverage is output relative to input, and income moves up by orders of magnitude as you climb four types: labour, media, capital, technology.
detail: Definition: leverage is the gap between what you put in and what you get out; people who earn less than they want are usually in low-leverage work. Order of types: labour (work for someone, for yourself, then others work for you), media (make once, license endlessly), capital (money works without your time), technology (build once, a million use it). Stack as many types as possible, and also how much of each (a bigger podcast has more media leverage than a small one). Credits Naval Ravikant for the four types.

### POD-0548#12 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: other | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:20:22
claim: Dream outcomes with more relative status are worth more; he illustrates each value-equation variable with weight-loss examples.
detail: For men, making money is a bigger dream outcome than losing weight, so it commands more. Likelihood: a surgeon with 10,000 five-star reviews beats a newcomer even if slower. ### POD-0548#14 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: sales-process | fw: Grand Slam Offer | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:28
claim: His first sales lesson, before he opened a gym, was to build an offer a prospect would feel foolish refusing, and improve it whenever yeses are too few.
detail: At a 2013 workshop on Facebook ads for gym owners, a marketer told him the "secret to sales" in one line. He had never sold anything. It became the rule in every business: ask how the offer can be made more valuable, and if too few say yes, upgrade the offer. He ranks the offer as the highest-leverage lever because it touches pricing, profit, marketing, sales and delivery, yet is the hardest to change.
quote: make people an offer they'd feel stupid saying no to
