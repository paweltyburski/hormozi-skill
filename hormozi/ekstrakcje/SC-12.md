### SC-12#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: strategy-positioning | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 9 Capitalize covers 250–499 employees; the founder becomes chairman, growth plateaus in the main product line, and you graduate by making big bets in product, brand and people, financed by professionalized finances.
detail: Constraint → graduation step per function:
- Product: changing the cash cow makes it worse, yet growth needs new products → buy (M&A) or build (R&D) the next rising star.
- Marketing: channel attribution gets hard, platform and regulatory compliance bite → brand-first omnichannel, blended ROAS across products, compliance process.
- Sales: too many lead types and products for one salesperson → specialize by product, lead, process; route by product or territory.
- Customer service: support for varied offerings breaks under volume → triage to specialized reps.
- IT: old stack broken by volume → division-level stacks, enterprise software (ERP).
- Recruiting: top talent ignores recruiters → founder recruits directly.
- HR: churn rises, unclear who is needed → strategic workforce planning.
- Finance: not credible enough for outside capital → prep for sale.

### SC-12#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: hiring-talent | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: At 250–499 employees there are about five layers below the founder, organized as divisions whose leaders run whole product lines or revenue streams.
detail: Ideally as few layers as possible; at this size you sometimes simply end up with five. Division (revenue) leaders report to a CEO who reports to the founder as chairman, or directly to the founder, depending on structure. The company becomes a company of companies (see the divisions atom).

### SC-12#04 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Buy vs Build | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:08
claim: When changes to the successful core product (the cash cow) start making it worse, stop tinkering and find growth by buying or building the next rising-star product, planning a year or more ahead.
detail: A loyal five- to eight-year customer base usually wants the product left alone; heavy changes hurt it. Keep routine fixes, but the next order-of-magnitude change has to come from a new product or service. Buy = mergers and acquisitions; build = research and development.
numbers: Planning horizon 1+ years.

### SC-12#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: product-delivery | fw: BCG Growth-Share Matrix | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:08
claim: The Boston Consulting Group matrix classifies products by market growth and market share into stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs.
detail: - Stars: growing market and growing share — the rising stars to bet on.
- Cash cows: high share in a market that is no longer growing much; prints money month after month — typical core product at this stage.
- Question marks: uncertain bets.
- Dogs: low share of a non-growing market — usually a past idea that did not work.
Do not try to turn the cow into a star; bet on the next star.

### SC-12#06 | TACTIC | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:33
claim: To pick the next rising star, think like an investor: study market trends, adoption of new technology, the services customers will ask for tomorrow, and underserved geographies.
detail: Questions: Where will the market be in a couple of years? What are adoption rates of new tech? What service lines do customers ask for today, and what will they ask for tomorrow? Which country or region is underserved — e.g. a company dominant in the US and UK might expand into Mexico or Latin America. Position yourself to be there when demand surges.

### SC-12#08 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: RICE | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:53
claim: Choose big bets for the highest risk-adjusted return using RICE: reach, impact, confidence, and cost.
detail: Reach of the market or product; impact the solution should have; confidence that you will achieve it; cost of the bet in time, money and resources.

### SC-12#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Buy vs Build | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:53
claim: Decide buy versus build by asking how core the capability is and how much speed matters; building tends to give better long-term results, while buying can make more sense when speed, licenses or assets matter.
detail: Favor buying when: it is not core and is simply a good return on capital; it can be cross-sold to existing customers; it brings cost savings; it turns you into a platform company (Waste Management grew by buying trash route after route); there is a race where one or two years' head start matters; licenses or regulatory approvals take years; you want its underlying assets or IP. Favor building when it is core to your future and speed is not critical. Both paths have horror stories and unicorns.

### SC-12#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: Buy vs Build | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:01
claim: Treat an acquisition as buying a product for your business and know exactly which part you are paying for — customers, team, product, potential, or list.
detail: As with pruning features, not every piece of the acquired business will be useful. Be clear about why you are buying and which piece you want most.

### SC-12#11 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: unit-economics | fw: Buy vs Build | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:47
claim: A friend buys retiring advisors' books of business because acquiring hundreds of wealth-management clients at once costs less than CAC times the same number won through marketing.
detail: With an illustrative CAC of $10,000 per client, buying 500 clients in one deal for less than that total beats winning them one by one through events, conferences and speaking. Compare CAC × customer base with the purchase price. Cross-sell synergies often fail to materialize; the best leading indicator is that the two companies already share customers, so the deal just formalizes it.

### SC-12#13 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: finance-exit | fw: Buy vs Build | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:24
claim: When building, hunt teams that have already done it — hire a competitor's number two and the people they know, paying large signing bonuses, which is often cheaper than buying a company.
detail: Questions for building: Who has already done this? Do we understand the space, or must we bring in experts? Is the ramp-up delay acceptable? Pros of building: the same culture. Cons versus buying: speed, cost and risk. Acqui-hiring a functioning team from another company is a middle path.

### SC-12#14 | DECISION_RULE | topic: paid-ads | also: content-brand | fw: Blended ROAS | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:29
claim: When channel attribution becomes unreliable and platform and regulatory compliance become real risks, shift to brand-first omnichannel advertising judged on blended ROAS across all products, and install a compliance process.
detail: With many channels and products, crediting a sale to one channel is hard, and a big company with money becomes a target for lawsuits; platform rules and regulation also become an issue.

### SC-12#15 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:17
claim: All advertising carries risk, but the biggest risk is going out of business from not advertising; you are usually okay if you state facts, tell the truth, substantiate claims and refund when asked.
detail: Lawyers will list every risk; keep risk number one (survival) first, unless the way you do business itself endangers the company. Distinguish platform risk from regulatory risk: some platforms are stricter than the law, so know whether a breach means an account shutdown, a fine, or jail.

### SC-12#16 | TACTIC | topic: paid-ads | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:02
claim: Have legal counsel audit all marketing and turn it into a compliance checklist that every ad, email, piece of content and phone script must pass, signed off by a manager before release.
detail: At this size you may need a full-time compliance person reviewing ads, emails, content and sales scripts for promises that cannot be kept. Hormozi's best version: the marketer submits the completed checklist to their manager before deploying. Reinforced over time, it becomes simply how the company advertises. Boring, but necessary once you have money and are a target.

### SC-12#17 | CASE | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: Give:Ask Ratio | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:38
claim: After 15 years of decline, New Balance's new CMO flipped the budget from 70% direct response to 70% brand; losses deepened for 18 months, then from month 19 sales shot up into a major resurgence.
detail: The 70% went to top-of-funnel brand awareness, associations, collaborations, partnerships and endorsements; 30% to bottom-of-funnel direct-response offers. Hormozi heard the split secondhand from someone who spoke to the CMO.
numbers: 70/30 brand/DR; ~18 months of losses; turnaround at month 19.

### SC-12#18 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: Give:Ask Ratio | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:23
claim: In his New Balance example a brand shift took about 18 months to start paying off (he expects years, not months), and a ratio of roughly three pieces of giving to one ask works for a mature platform, while growth calls for even more giving.
detail: New Balance (a big budget, he assumes) still lost money for 18 more months after switching to 70/30 branding, then turned at month 19; he says not to expect a two-month thing, but years. He says the 70/30 split (heard second-hand) is close to the give:ask ratio he teaches, which he calls four to one (three content, one ask), seen on mature media: three units of content per commercial (TV minutes, YouTube, the Facebook feed's three posts then an ad). Platforms in growth mode give even more — TikTok ran no ads for about three years. His interpretation: people had already seen so many asks that they needed 18 months of giving before the ratio shifted back.
numbers: ~18 months to payback; ~3:1 give:ask; TikTok ~3 years without ads.

### SC-12#19 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: Give:Ask Ratio | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:03
claim: Allocate roughly three-quarters of the ad budget to giving — content, giveaways, near-free tools, or for consumer brands endorsements and collaborations.
detail: Example: with $1M a month in ad spend, about $750K goes to content, giveaways and tools or products given to the base for almost nothing; for D2C, endorsement deals (a LeBron-type deal) and collaborations with creators who match what your customers resonate with.
numbers: $1M/month budget → ~$750K give, ~$250K ask.

### SC-12#20 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: unit-economics | fw: Blended ROAS | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:50
claim: Judge marketing on blended ROAS: direct-response ROAS looks spectacular only because brand spend creates it, and cutting brand to chase it drains goodwill.
detail: A direct-response team may report 20:1; including brand spend the true return may be 4–5:1. The temptation is to double down on direct response. That is how a new public-company CEO can look brilliant for a year: stop the giving, pull revenue forward, empty the audience's goodwill into the company's bank account — then face 18 months of giving to refill it. Dig the well before you are thirsty; over-deliver before the ask. Track top-of-funnel and bottom-of-funnel spend separately, then blend.
numbers: Reported DR ROAS 20:1 vs blended ~4–5:1.

### SC-12#21 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:21
claim: Founder-led companies think much longer term than professionally managed ones; if you have excess profits, spend the extra cash on long-term branding if you want something that lasts, and when in doubt, go long.
detail: Founders will give for a quarter, two quarters or a year; managers judged on quarterly results tend to extract profits and move on, which is what gives some private equity its bad name. If you want something that lasts, put the extra cash into long-term branding.
quote: When in doubt, go long.

### SC-12#22 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:21
claim: Add as many channels as possible, entering each brand-first: pave the way with goodwill, then follow with direct-response asks.

### SC-12#23 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: retention-ltv | fw: Cost to Acquire a Permanent Customer | novelty: canon | ts: 00:18:08
claim: Calculate the cost to acquire a permanent customer — one who survives past the point where churn stops — and use it as the back-of-napkin route to much larger scale.
detail: Example: a $100/month software product; one in three free trials converts, and one in three customers survives to month 12–24, after which they basically never leave. A permanent customer therefore costs nine trials. For physical products, the permanent customer is the one who keeps buying for life (Coca-Cola, a candy brand, Gymshark). With that number, reaching $1B or $10B becomes a math problem: how many millions to push enough trials through to create that many permanent customers.
numbers: 1/3 trial→customer × 1/3 customer→permanent = 9 trials per permanent customer; $100/month example.

### SC-12#24 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:41
claim: When there are too many lead types and products for any one salesperson, specialize reps by product, lead type and process, and route leads by product or territory.
detail: Best-leads-to-best-closers should already be done. Next, certain leads go only to closers who know how to sell them: a direct-to-consumer line and a wholesale line need completely different prospects, messaging, salespeople and processes. Each specialty gets its own type of rep, compensation plan and training.

### SC-12#25 | EXAMPLE | topic: sales-team | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:29
claim: A D2C brand might run separate sales teams for nano-influencers, mega-influencers and wholesale, each with its own leads, pipeline and process.
detail: Leads sometimes cross over — a nano-influencer brings in a wholesaler — so the earlier categorization and role specialization let you route it and capitalize.

### SC-12#26 | PRINCIPLE | topic: sales-team | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:17
claim: The main reason to specialize salespeople is to shorten training.
detail: A rep facing six processes, six lead types and twelve products would take forever to ramp. Recruit specifically for one lane (e.g. wholesale), train only for it, and give it its own compensation and channel. Divisions then grow their own structures.

### SC-12#27 | TACTIC | topic: sales-team | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:10
claim: Use territory leads and match reps to buyers they sell best to, and design separate sales processes by deal complexity.
detail: Matching can be cultural: buyers from the South often respond better to reps from the South. Processes differ by ticket: low ticket — one-call close; mid — two calls with a sales aid between; enterprise — five or more calls with multiple stakeholders to confirm integration. Each is effectively a separate operation.

### SC-12#28 | DECISION_RULE | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:59
claim: Leila: when support for many new offerings breaks under volume, triage customers to CS reps specialized by product or tier.
detail: A rep serving buyers of a $5,000 product does not need enterprise training. Route each customer type to reps trained on that product using CRM tags, customer segmentation, and phone and email routing. Benefits: shorter training and faster responses.

### SC-12#29 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: hiring-talent | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:39
claim: Tiered support creates a career ladder for CS reps, which extends the lifetime value of your talent as well as your customers.
detail: Reps start on the entry product; strong performers move to product two (example: about $15,000 more per year), then to enterprise accounts, where they can nearly double their starting pay. Ascending reps doubles as a retention system for staff.
numbers: ~+$15K/year at tier two; enterprise ≈ 2× starting pay.

### SC-12#30 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:25:50
claim: Prevent transfer loops and repeated explanations: standardize CRM notes for seamless handoffs and route every contact channel by rules.
detail: Without triage a customer explains a missed delivery, gets transferred, explains again, and spends 20 minutes reaching the right person. Standardized notes let any rep see history when a customer cross-buys or ascends, so customers never repeat themselves — the biggest failure in big companies. Customers reach out by email, social DMs and phone; integrate the tech so each channel routes by rules.
numbers: ~20 minutes lost in transfer loops (example).

### SC-12#32 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:44
claim: Leila: when volume breaks the old tech stack, give each division its own tech team and stack, and you probably need to move from a CRM to enterprise software such as an ERP.
detail: Many companies still run the CRM chosen at 50 people. Symptom: the system cannot handle logins for the different products and owners, so people share credentials. Move to a customizable ERP (e.g. NetSuite, Zoho) that serves every department and product. Each IT function gets its own "czar" (marketing, sales, CS), with inquiry processes and SLAs to prioritize.

IT departments fail mainly by spending time on things that do not matter. ### SC-12#34 | DECISION_RULE | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:33:10
claim: When the best talent stops responding to recruiters, the founder must become the head headhunter and sell top candidates on the vision personally.
detail: The needed people are highly specialized and courted by every company at this level. Nobody responds like they do to the founder or CEO of a large fast-growing company: they have seen your name or ads and open the door out of curiosity. Early on the founder sells customers and teammates; now they sell top talent on a vision big enough to contain the candidate's own career vision. This cannot be outsourced.

### SC-12#35 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:34:33
claim: Recruiting top executives is courtship that can take months or years, because they are rebuilding their whole life around a new mission.

He says Steve Jobs repeatedly cited 12–18 months to get top people from competitors; Zuckerberg took Sheryl Sandberg to dinner for about a year (frequency recalled only as maybe every two weeks, unsure). ### SC-12#36 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:36:05
claim: The founder recruits only rainmakers — not frontline or ordinary managers — and treats them as partners: beyond a certain level, nobody works for you; they work with you.
detail: Recruiters still fill other roles. Founder targets bring contact books that open new distribution or geographies, key stakeholder relationships, or whole teams that follow them. They have many options and recruiters in their inbox daily; they are doing you a favor by building your company. They are mission-driven, still hungry, and want you to match their intensity, intelligence and drive. Hormozi says many founders would spend the time to close a $10–20M contract, whereas closing one such leader to build a department or product division could create ten times that kind of wealth for the company.
numbers: Such a leader could create ~10× the value of a $10–20M contract (Hormozi's estimate).

### SC-12#37 | DECISION_RULE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: Strategic Workforce Planning | novelty: canon | ts: 00:38:25
claim: When churn rises and you no longer know who you need to grow, run strategic workforce planning: projections for people, as you do for finances.
detail: Start from company objectives (the what) and work out the who: which roles, how many, how fast; who moves; who will leave given the churn rate and how to backfill; who can be promoted; where to hold backups (leave, pregnancy, resignation); where hyper-specialization will be needed. Hormozi says he is doing a 12–18 month workforce plan himself, with the example of 315 people now and 500 needed in 18 months to hit goals, and where they sit in the org chart.
numbers: 12–18 month horizon; example 315 → 500 people.

### SC-12#38 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: Strategic Workforce Planning | novelty: canon | ts: 00:39:57
claim: Color-code the future org chart green, yellow and red to see where to promote, upskill or hire.
detail: Green: an internal person ready to be promoted. Yellow: someone in the seat but weak. Red: a missing department or leader — e.g. a whole team routed to someone competent who should not be leading it. Many reds means heavy hiring; clusters of yellow mean upskilling. Managers rate their own people red/yellow/green, since the founder can no longer know everyone's performance. The chairman then decides like an investor: train up, bring in or move around.

### SC-12#39 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: hiring-talent | fw: RACI Matrix | novelty: canon | ts: 00:40:55
claim: Have each team build a RACI matrix — responsible, accountable, consulted, informed — to expose gaps and misallocated people for workforce planning.
detail: Responsible: does the task. Accountable: owns completion, usually the direct manager. Consulted: subject-matter experts not directly involved (Layla is consulted on recruiting without running it). Informed: those who receive the output, e.g. CEO, CFO, leadership. It reveals one person who is R for everything, leaders who are only C or I and underused, or a manager with too many direct reports. Do it per team, not for the whole company; teams hand results up for org-wide planning.

### SC-12#40 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: Prep for Sale | novelty: canon | ts: 00:42:23
claim: When your finances are not credible enough for others to lend, invest or partner, run a "prep for sale" to make the company sale-ready — whether or not you intend to sell.
detail: For big buy-or-build bets, sometimes you need outside capital, or at least it accelerates them. Prep for sale is not trimming to show margins; it is cleaning everything up so you can double down on the opportunity. Treat the business as a product for the marketplace and make it more appealing — which also makes it more valuable to own.

### SC-12#41 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: Prep for Sale | novelty: canon | ts: 00:43:10
claim: Prep-for-sale steps: quality of earnings, a "banker audit", cleaning house on people, third-party audits, a review of legal structure, hedges, and debt relationships.
detail: - Quality of earnings report.
- Banker audit: ask investment bankers what is wrong with your business. They want to sell it later for a commission, so they give free, candid consulting from an investor's and a lender's viewpoint.
- Remove people who no longer contribute.
- External audits beyond internal ones.
- Legal structure: an LLC or S-corp may not suit a minority deal or dividend recap — ask lawyers.
- Hedging of key input costs; debt relationships.

### SC-12#42 | EXAMPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: Prep for Sale | novelty: canon | ts: 00:44:00

detail: Before selling a house you fix the door, power-wash the walls and stop the squeaks, then think it is not so bad after all. A portfolio CEO being pursued by investors said during prep that, once the remaining fixes were in, he could not see why he would sell.

### SC-12#44 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:45:50
claim: Companies buying huge volumes of a key input can hedge its price to smooth profit.
detail: Ray Dalio describes such a hedge for McDonald's beef; Starbucks faces the same with coffee beans. A few cents per pound swings profit heavily. Take a position that pays if the input price rises, offsetting higher costs; if the price falls, you lose the bet but gain margin. Relevant only for big purchases or large liabilities.

### SC-12#45 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: Prep for Sale | novelty: canon | ts: 00:46:53
claim: Professionalized finances open debt from institutional lenders and strategic partners, funding big bets without selling the company.
detail: Lenders care only about downside — how reliably you will keep earning to repay with interest — not your upside. Debt can fund an acquisition, carving a team out of another company, or big brand sponsorships (e.g. $20M a year). Alternatively sell a small stake to a strategic partner: e.g. someone buys 10% and adds distribution into 10,000 retail stores, giving cash, lower risk and expansion.
numbers: Example sponsorships ~$20M/year; strategic deal 10% stake + 10,000 stores.

### SC-12#46 | PRINCIPLE | topic: scaling-constraints | also: operator-mindset | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:49:02
claim: The stage-9 constraint is a plateau, and the answer is a big bet — in people, product and brand — to work out how to 10× from here.
detail: Typically many years into the business, you may need to shake things up again. The course does not cover 500–1,000 employees because Hormozi and Layla have not operated there yet (Acquisition.com is just crossing that threshold across its portfolio); he vetted the earlier framework with friends at or above this size. For most bootstrapped owners, reaching this stage is already an extraordinary success.
