### SC-06#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: scaling-constraints | also: hiring-talent | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Stage 3 Stabilize (1–4 employees): the workload now exceeds the founder's resources, and you graduate by getting help — hiring the first team and giving each function basic structure.
detail: Role: trainer of the first full-time team. Only ~24% of businesses have even one employee besides the founder, and there are only seven "Magnificent Seven" giants among 30M businesses, so you are probably doing better than you think. Constraint → graduation step:
- Product: volume creates cracks → fix the top complaint, or whatever raises activation most.
- Marketing: colder leads buy slowly → long-term nurture by email and content.
- Sales: time wasted on bad leads → qualification, outsourced setting and calendaring.
- Customer service: new customers feel lost → GROWTH onboarding.
- IT: unused paid tools → cut them and build a basic pipeline.
- Recruiting: poor applicants → job descriptions and résumé review.
- HR: taxes not withheld → payroll provider.
- Finance: informal payments, missed bills → bookkeeping, AR/AP, collections.
numbers: ~24% of businesses have at least one employee beyond the founder.

### SC-06#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:40
claim: With no time to fix everything, force-rank the product complaint list by return on investment; if unsure, simply fix the number one reason customers cancel or leave.
detail: Take the list of friction points and hidden costs collected at earlier stages. Rank each fix on three things:
1. How big an effect it will have on the product.
2. The investment it needs (time and resources).
3. The likelihood it works.
This mirrors the value equation: you look for the fix that creates the most value for the business by creating it for the customer. Shortcut if ROI is unclear: ask why people leave, and fix the top reason first. Example: if customers leave because you are too slow to respond, find a way to respond faster, whether a platform, better visibility or a hire.

### SC-06#03 | DEFINITION | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: Activation Point | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:29
claim: Activation is the point at which a customer first receives value, or engages with the product in a way that gets value; it can be defined by outcomes that occur and by behaviors the customer takes.
detail: Long term you reverse-engineer activation and push customers to it. Two lenses, from Gym Launch:
- Outcome: the gym owner made a sale in their first week from the leads Gym Launch generated. Owners who hit this were very likely to stay and keep paying.
- Behavior: attending the first call with a Gym Launch coach, who set up their ads and coached them through working the leads to a first sale. Behavior is the leading indicator of the outcome.
In the simplest form, Gym Launch's activation was the first coach call, the first time the customer engaged with the product.

### SC-06#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: management-ops | also: product-delivery | fw: Impact vs Investment Matrix (RICE) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:42
claim: Sort possible improvements on a 2×2 of impact against investment; the easy high-impact, low-investment box is usually already used up, so the real opportunity is the best item in the high-impact, high-investment box.
detail: The presenter calls this the RICE framework (reach, impact, confidence, expense), shown as an impact-versus-investment grid:
- High impact, low investment: obviously do these first. Because it is so obvious, most have already been done; if one appears, do it immediately.
- High impact, high investment: the big, hairy problems. This is where the real gains are; do this box better rather than doing more of it.
- Low impact, high investment: costs a lot, returns little.
- Low impact, low investment: small "golden BB" tasks. Delegate them once you have a team.

### SC-06#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: Impact vs Investment Matrix (RICE) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:08
claim: Many founders stay stuck doing quick B-minus and C-plus tasks for the feeling of momentum; the best founders ruthlessly pick the single biggest high-impact, high-investment problem and solve it.
detail: Quick tasks such as redesigning the website feel productive and give a fast feedback loop ("six things done today"), but can do nothing, and for weeks or months nothing substantive changes. What has made the biggest difference to how fast Hormozi's companies grew is ruthless prioritization: of the three items in the high-impact, high-investment box, pick the one with the biggest impact. It is usually a big, hairy problem with many variables, touching two or three departments. Those problems are the opportunity to set yourself apart from others in your space.

### SC-06#08 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: content-brand | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:44
claim: Once your warm network is used up, add long-term nurture by email: at least one value-driven blast to the whole list each week, ideally three segmented emails a week.
detail: Early customers came from people who knew you. That pool is exhausted, and new leads are colder and buy more slowly. Nurture expands your warm pool. Email cadence: the minimum is one blast a week to everyone, to show you are alive and stay top of mind; the extreme (best) is three segmented emails a week. Keep it value-driven.

applies_to: Stabilize stage (1–4 employees).

### SC-06#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:37
claim: His nurture emails pretty much all follow one structure: subject, a quick hit of value, tactical value about what you sell, a light call to action, and a PS that rewards the click.
detail: 1. Subject line.
2. Quick hit of value that rewards them for opening (Hormozi uses a favorite quote).
3. Tactical value related to what you sell.
4. A light CTA: "By the way, if you want more help with this / a personalized plan, here's X."

### SC-06#13 | FRAMEWORK | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:22
claim: Content ideas have five origins: far past (life stories), recent past (the previous week), present (whatever is on your mind), trending topics, and manufactured stunts.
detail: - Far past: stories from your life.
- Recent past: review last week's calendar, your conversations, customer issues. Something interesting occurs in any business within seven days; even how you handled a problem has value.
- Present: what you're currently thinking about. Jot quick ideas as they arise (Hormozi uses tweets).
- Trending: interest-rate news, an algorithm shift, a new law affecting your industry. These earn outsized views briefly.
- Manufactured: a deliberately extreme undertaking, e.g. "Two years living on $100 a month — what that did to me." It takes the most effort, but done well delivers the most views and growth.

### SC-06#15 | TACTIC | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:32
claim: To stop wasting time on bad leads, define whom you will not sell to: who cannot succeed with the product, and who your worst, most time-consuming customers are.
detail: Qualifying means deciding whom not to sell. Look at the requirements someone must meet to use your product at all. Look at your worst customers, the ones who eat the most customer-service time and are a pain. They often do not meet those requirements. Turn those requirements into qualification criteria, used on a set call or an automated application form. Then outsource setting and calendaring so your time goes to qualified prospects.

### SC-06#16 | EXAMPLE | topic: sales-process | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:32
claim: Gym Launch qualified prospects as small fitness gyms with at least three employees and at least 30 clients, because anyone else could not run its systems and complained loudly.
detail: Early systems were built for small gyms. Big gyms, yoga, barre and meditation studios, and solo personal trainers were a poor fit: loud, time-consuming and saying bad things. Fewer than three employees meant not enough help to execute the system. Fewer than 30 clients meant the signature "paid in full" play could not work. That play gets existing clients to prepay for a year, creating cash to reinvest in marketing. The offer was broadened over the following 10 years, but at the start these rules protected results and reputation.
numbers: Minimum 3 employees and 30 clients.

### SC-06#17 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: - | fw: BANT | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:08
claim: BANT — budget, authority, need, timing — is a decades-old IBM qualification checklist that still works.
detail: - Budget: they have the money.
- Authority: they can make the decision.
- Need: what you sell matches their situation. Someone without a gym has no need for Gym Launch, and a gym at full capacity has none either, unless it is opening another location.
- Timing: they want to start now.
Ask questions about who they are and what you need to know to close the right people, on the setting call or in an automated application. The presenter dates BANT to IBM about 50 years ago.

### SC-06#19 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: GROWTH Onboarding | novelty: canon | ts: 00:16:41
claim: Onboard new customers with GROWTH: set goals and expectations, give a personal success path, optimize the first experience for quick wins, integrate into their workflow, track with feedback loops, and hand off to ongoing support.
detail: 1. G — Goal setting: clear expectations and what they want to achieve.
2. R — Personal success path: the steps to the goal (the transcript says "allocate time"; the letter's word is unclear in audio).
3. O — Optimize the first experience: quick wins and immediate value, reaching activation fast.
4. W — Workflow integration: fit their operations (B2B) or their life (B2C), adding as little new work as possible.
5. T — Track and feed back: regular loops to monitor, recognize, reward and relay, on a set cadence. Book the next meeting before the current one ends (BAMFAM, "book a meeting from a meeting").
6. H — Handoff to ongoing support: once they succeed, move to long-term support, and make sure they know where to ask questions.
It echoes the CLO of CLOSER: changing behavior works the same way whether you want people to buy or to act.

### SC-06#22 | FRAMEWORK | topic: sales-process | also: management-ops | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:25
claim: At 1–4 employees, build a basic sales pipeline that tracks each step from opt-in to cash, even if the tools are duct-taped together.
detail: Stages and metrics to track: opt-ins, scheduled, showed, offer made, closed, cash collected, and backouts (cancellations after the sale), plus the conversion between steps. Each step should have a description and a metric. You cannot improve without knowing your baseline. Add or adapt steps to your business later; a more integrated system comes at a later stage. First, cut unused tools and extend trials or discounts on the ones you keep.

### SC-06#23 | FRAMEWORK | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: Job Description as Ad | novelty: canon | ts: 00:21:18
claim: Write a job description like an ad: a hook headline, values that retain the reader, an explanation of the role's main duties, and a clear "what's in it for you".
detail: 1. Hook/headline: speak to the candidate's dream job and how you see this role differently, i.e. why apply here rather than to the job across the street.
2. Retain: the company's values, why they matter, and how they show up in the role every day.
3. Educate: the main activities and the top three responsibilities, and how the role affects overall company growth.
4. Reinforce what's in it for them: pay, benefits, paid time off, working environment, awards (e.g. best place to work).
Early on you only have an external funnel for customers; now you are building an internal funnel for talent. They are called job ads for a reason. Making candidates jump through hoops with an ugly posting gets nobody.

### SC-06#25 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: - | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:23:07
claim: Review résumés against what you want (proven achievements, relevant experience, transferable skills) and what you do not want (irrelevant experience, too much experience, wrong skills).
detail: Want:
- Past achievements backed by objective data showing they actually did it, not just claims.
- Experience relevant to the business as it is now and to this job.
- Skills that transfer.
Do not want:
- Irrelevant experience.
- Too much experience.
- The wrong skills for the role.
Better job ads bring many more applicants, so you need this filter to sift them.

### SC-06#27 | PRINCIPLE | topic: hiring-talent | also: scaling-constraints | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:59
claim: Over-experience is a real hiring risk: someone from a much larger company brings practices that do not fit, so hire for your current stage and the next 12–18 months.
detail: A strong job ad can attract overqualified people. Someone from a company at stage 7 (50–99 people) will bring stage-7 practices into a stage-3 business and throw things off. Under-experience means they cannot do the job; over-experience imports big-company habits. Look for experience matching where you are now and where you will be in 12–18 months, and ignore anything beyond that for now.
numbers: Hire for the current stage plus the next 12–18 months.

### SC-06#29 | TACTIC | topic: hiring-talent | also: finance-exit | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:27:07

### SC-06#30 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: - | fw: $100M Scaling Roadmap | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:13
claim: Once volume grows, set up four basic finance processes: correct billing, a collections process, money set aside for fixed bills first, and one inbox for all incoming bills.
detail: The problems: informal pay, forgotten bills, customers billed wrongly. A late payment to a core vendor can halt delivery. AR (accounts receivable) is what customers owe you; AP (accounts payable) is what you owe vendors. Processes:
1. Billing: enter every client contract correctly. Hormozi says they have lost a lot of money here.
2. Collections: someone tracks declines and chargebacks and pushes customers to pay on time. Customers will happily get the service free if nobody follows up.
3. Set money aside first for fixed and recurring bills: rent, plus core software, which is effectively rent for an online business.
4. One inbox for incoming bills (e.g. ap@ or finance@yourcompany.com), so invoices get forwarded and handled in batches.
Also set up payroll processing and basic bookkeeping.
