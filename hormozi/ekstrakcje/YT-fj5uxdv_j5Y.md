### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#01 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: management-ops | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:00:00
claim: When someone asks you to approve an action, Hormozi will almost always respond by asking "what problem are you trying to solve?"
detail: People often act on a vague emotional discomfort. Example: a proposed employee engagement programme collapses when it turns out there is no retention or hiring problem to fix. The question tests whether the person has thought the proposal through before you commit resources.
### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:56
claim: Lack of focus is often intellectual laziness: people drop the task they do not know how to solve and do something they can solve.
detail: Owners usually know on some level what must happen, but switch to tasks that make them feel competent without changing outcomes. Staying on the hard task, while incompetent for an extended time, is the cost of getting what you want.

### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#03 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:02:30
claim: When you want something done, ask "what would it take?" rather than "can we?", because it assumes success and makes people state requirements.
detail: A bare "can we" invites a no based on someone's partial view of the business and plans. "What would it take" lets the answer surface (a hire at about $100K that brings an extra million, or reprioritising other work) so you decide whether it is worth it. Walk down the ladder: a billion dollars? a million? what actually?
numbers: Example: a $100,000 hire expected to add $1,000,000 in the year.

### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#04 | EXAMPLE | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:04:30
claim: "What would it take" works for any stretch target, such as speed, acquisitions and volume.
detail: Examples he uses: doing it in half the time, delivering in 24 hours instead of 30 days, buying a given company, doing a deal, getting someone to start today, persuading an owner to close one business and go all in on another, and lifting content from 10 to 20 pieces a day.

### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#05 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:10
claim: Ask "what do you want to have happen?" to cut through the emotion behind a proposal to the outcome wanted.
detail: Example: a proposal to hold quarterly meetings in person. The stated outcome ("more fun") is then probed for whether engagement actually changes output. He asks not to attack but to hear the other person's thinking.

### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#06 | SCRIPT | topic: management-ops | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:07:12
claim: Probe a claim with three questions in sequence: what does that mean, how do you know, and so what.
detail: Define the term (happier employees = higher well-being scores), then test how it would be measured (before and after survey), then ask what business outcome follows (does it fade, does output rise?). Final check: what else could we do with that time and energy for a higher return? He believes one-off perks do less than a work environment that rewards effort and removes poor performers. The questions also reveal the depth of a team member's thinking, and he is comfortable being asked them back.

### YT-fj5uxdv_j5Y#07 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: management-ops | fw: More Better New | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:32
claim: Questioning proposals often leads to "maybe we shouldn't do this", which he usually sees as the right answer: keep resourcing the proven return instead of a new maybe.
detail: With options A, B and do-nothing, doing nothing is often right: put resources behind what is known to produce. A proposal may come from emotional discomfort with the present rather than evidence. Asking "what would it take" tends to create more divergent thinking, with people trying to solve for success and reverse engineer it.
