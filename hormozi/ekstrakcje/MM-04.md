### MM-04#01 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: - | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Gross profit is price minus cost of goods sold — what it costs you to deliver the thing.
detail: Sell a meal for $10 that costs $9 to deliver and gross profit is $1. It is the second of the three acquisition numbers, after CAC.

### MM-04#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads, strategy-positioning | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Most businesses think they need cheaper leads when they actually need to make more per customer — CAC is often fairly similar across competitors, customer value is not.
detail: Across many businesses in the same industry, CAC for a given avatar sits in a tight range; the huge gaps between companies come from how much each customer is worth. That is the difference between whales and minnows. Improving ads sometimes matters, but less than people assume.
numbers: Typical competitor CACs e.g. $2,500 vs $3,500; rarely ~$6,000 except for new entrants.

### MM-04#03 | CASE | topic: unit-economics | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:56
claim: A competitor selling the same number of units per month as Hormozi made $300K a year in profit while Hormozi made $17M — about 56 times more.
detail: At one of his first consulting days he helped someone in his own space (he does not see the world as competitors). The only major difference was how much each business made per customer.
numbers: $300K vs $17M annual profit; same monthly unit volume; ~56×.

### MM-04#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads, strategy-positioning | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:01
claim: Gross profit is the arms race of business: whoever makes a customer worth the most can outspend everyone for attention — an ethical monopoly.
detail: Competitors may still operate, but to be heard they must outbid you on every channel, and your better model lets you outspend them. Dan Kennedy's line: whoever can spend the most to acquire a customer wins. Hormozi's precursor version: whoever makes a customer worth more to their business than competitors can wins — the input that makes the outspending possible.
quote: Gross profit is the arms race of business.

### MM-04#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: money-models | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:20
claim: The $50,000 burger: to someone earning ~$50M a year it cost the same in relative terms as Hormozi's $13 burger — high customer value makes expensive traffic cheap.
detail: Working at Smoothie King, he read about a Wall Street buyer of a gold $50,000 burger. Treat the burger as traffic and income as lifetime gross profit: that buyer could outbid him about 3,700 times over in the auction of attention. Costs can only fall to zero, but lifetime gross profit per customer can rise without limit, so work both sides — cheaper acquisition and more valuable customers.
numbers: $50K vs $13 burger; ~$50M/year income; ~3,700× spending power.

### MM-04#06 | EXAMPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery | fw: Gross Profit | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:07
claim: Gross profit for a product is price minus manufacturing and shipping; for a service it is price minus the per-client share of delivery staff.
detail: Product: $100 widget − $20 to make and ship = $80. Service: clients pay $3,000/month; one account rep costs $6,000/month and serves 10 clients = $600 per client; gross profit $2,400 per client = 80% margin. Many service owners do not know these costs.
numbers: Widget $80 GP; service $2,400 GP on $3,000 (80%).

### MM-04#07 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery, scaling-constraints | fw: Gross Margin | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:59
claim: A service business — local or national — needs at least 80% gross margin to scale: if delivery costs $100, sell for $500.
detail: This is gross, not net. Rent, admin, insurance, utilities, repairs and advertising still come out of it, so an 80% gross margin business might net around 30%. Below 80%, scaling a service business is very unlikely.
numbers: ≥80% gross margin; ~30% net margin at 80% gross.
applies_to: Service businesses.

### MM-04#08 | DEFINITION | topic: unit-economics | also: finance-exit | fw: Gross Margin | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:32
claim: Gross profit is the dollar amount and gross margin is the same thing as a percentage of price; both include only variable costs that rise with each unit or customer sold.
detail: $80 gross profit on $100 = 80% gross margin. Rent, admin staff and utilities do not go up per sale, so they are excluded; they are paid from gross profit to arrive at net profit. Test: if I sell one more unit or client, what extra cost do I incur?

### MM-04#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: product-delivery | fw: Gross Margin | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:48
claim: Delivery staff feel fixed but are variable in chunks — allocate a fraction of their cost to each client in gross profit.
detail: You pay a delivery person the same up to their capacity (20 clients in his example), but past that you must hire another. Fractionalize their cost per client rather than treating it as overhead.

### MM-04#10 | FRAMEWORK | topic: unit-economics | also: money-models | fw: More Better New | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:36
claim: Without changing price or cost structure, raise gross profit with More, Better or New.
detail: More: sell more quantity or duration — buy in bulk, buy again tomorrow. Better: sell a higher-quality version — organic instead of regular burger, personal instead of large-group training. New: sell an additional thing — fries and a Coke, which can then be supersized. The rest of the course expands on these.
