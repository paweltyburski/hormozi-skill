### OC-02#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Starving Crowd | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: An offer may fail to convert not because the offer is bad but because it is being sold into the wrong market — so the market is the first thing to check.
detail: First work out who you are talking to. A great offer in the wrong market still underperforms. When Hormozi works with a business, he examines market fit before offer or sales skill.

### OC-02#02 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Starving Crowd | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:47
claim: Picking the right market ("finding a starving crowd") has four parts: the three levers of success, the four market variables, the three main markets with sub-markets, and niching down.
detail: 1. Levers of success: market, offer, persuasion, ranked by importance. 2. Four variables checked before entering: massive pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing. 3. Three main markets (health, wealth, relationships) and a sub-market inside one. 4. Niching ("the riches are in the niches"), especially for businesses under about $3M a year, really under about $10M.
applies_to: Especially businesses under $3M–$10M annual revenue (niching).

### OC-02#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers, sales-process | fw: Three Levers of Success | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:33
claim: Three levers determine success, in order of importance: the market, then the offer, then your ability to persuade; you check them in sequence and only move to the next if the current one is at least neutral.
detail: Rate each lever plus, neutral or negative. 1. Market (most important): some markets are so imbalanced that having the product sells out (toilet paper or masks early in COVID); then change nothing. Most are normal; if plus or neutral, move on. 2. Offer: with no sales in a normal market, suspect it. A great offer means nothing else matters; a normal one depends on persuasion; a terrible one probably fails. 3. Persuasion (least important): needed only with a normal market and a normal or subpar offer; slow to develop. So fix market and offer first. Markets usually win: a good persuader in a terrible market does worse than a bad persuader in a great market.
quote: Markets will usually win.

### OC-02#04 | DEFINITION | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: Three Levers of Success | novelty: canon | ts: 00:02:20
claim: A "normal" market is one growing at roughly the same pace as the population; Hormozi made all his money in normal markets and simply avoids dying ones.
detail: He first says the economy, then corrects to population. He never had a "gangbusters" market, so booming is a bonus, not a requirement. Jump on a booming opportunity; otherwise just stay out of shrinking markets.

### OC-02#05 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Four Market Variables | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:04
claim: Hormozi checks four variables before entering a market: the customers are in massive pain, they have purchasing power, they are easy to target, and the market is growing.
detail: 1. Pain: they must desperately need it, not find it nice to have (hot dogs at 2 a.m. in a college town as bars let out). 2. Purchasing power: they must have money. 3. Easy to target: platform targeting, associations, rentable lists, or others who aggregated attention; reaching only adjacent people (nurses instead of doctors) fails. 4. Growing, or at least not shrinking. Rule: if a market fails any one, find another that meets all four.

### OC-02#06 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Four Market Variables | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:04
claim: A friend with a strong resume-improvement offer for job seekers failed because the people he reached were unemployed and could not pay — a purchasing-power failure.
detail: He was good at improving resumes, but the people he found lived on unemployment benefits. Job seekers are not inherently unworkable; the way and place he found them delivered people without money. A good offer cannot overcome missing purchasing power in the audience you actually reach.

### OC-02#07 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Four Market Variables | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:08
claim: A strong entrepreneur selling a well-designed performance offer to local newspapers could not grow because the newspaper market was shrinking about 25% a year.
detail: The friend's software company sold advertising services to local newspapers with a good performance-based, zero-down, revenue-share offer, yet could not grow. Newspapers had shrunk about 25% a year for about nine years (halving every three years), so 25% growth only kept him flat. Hormozi also argues for outside advisors who spot the big obvious thing.
numbers: Newspaper market shrinking ~25%/year for ~9 years; 25% growth only offsets decline.

### OC-02#08 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Health Wealth Relationships | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:46
claim: There are three main markets — health, wealth and relationships — reflecting the core problems of the human condition; nearly every business sits inside one of them.
detail: Do not just pick one; find your sub-market: a specific avatar with a specific problem solved in a specific way. Wealth sub-markets: real estate investing, stock market, advertising, sales teams, creating offers. Health: nutrition-based, subdividing into keto, intermittent fasting, Weight Watchers points, Nutrisystem meals. Often you only need to repackage an existing approach.

### OC-02#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: offers | fw: Starving Crowd | novelty: canon | ts: 00:07:31
claim: Position the business around helping a distinct avatar fix a particular problem via a distinctive method that defeats their main objection.
detail: Formula: a unique avatar, a very specific problem, and a unique method that reverses their biggest objection. The prospect hears that it concerns them, recognises the problem, and the unique mechanism answers "is this one of those?". Describe your sub-market using these terms.
quote: Help a unique avatar solve a specific problem in a unique way.

### OC-02#10 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: Health Wealth Relationships | novelty: canon | ts: 00:09:06
claim: Classify an offer by its "so that they can…" outcome: anything that ends in making money is wealth (B2B or B2C), while health and relationships are almost always B2C.
detail: Wealth B2C: a business or job opportunity, a skill for a better-paying job. Wealth B2B: making existing businesses more money, marketing, even IT services via back-end systems. Health is nearly all B2C (exception: corporate fitness). Relationships are B2C; relationship-like help sold to a business (founders communicating better) counts as wealth. Test: what is the end goal, more money, feeling loved, or being healthy.

### OC-02#11 | EXAMPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: pricing, offers | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:47
claim: Niching the same time-management course step by step raises its sellable price from about $19 to about $2,000 — roughly 100×.
detail: Credited to Dan Kennedy. (1) Generic time-management booklet, about $19, no unique avatar. (2) Same content for sales professionals, about $99. (3) For outbound B2B sales, one extra deal pays it back, about $500. (4) For outbound B2B power tools and gardening reps, nothing else like it, about $1,997. Same core product, about 100× price.
numbers: $19 → $99 → $500 → $1,997 (~100×).

### OC-02#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: content-brand, paid-ads | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:07
claim: Niching down makes selling easier and more profitable because all messaging becomes specific to one avatar and you stop competing against mass-market giants.
detail: (1) Copy and ads speak to one avatar, so selling is easier. (2) Category of one supports far higher prices. (3) Mass markets hold hard-to-dethrone incumbents; in a niche it is easier to make the case that you solve this person's problem better than a generic solution. (4) More targeted ad spend and specific outreach at higher price points. Most important below about $3M a year (really below $10M).
applies_to: Especially businesses under $3M–$10M annual revenue.

### OC-02#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: sales-process, strategy-positioning | fw: Riches in the Niches | novelty: canon | ts: 00:13:07
claim: The goal of good writing is for the reader to understand; the goal of good copy is for the prospect to feel understood.
detail: Persuading is not teaching. If you describe their situation and suffering better than they can, they assume you also articulate the solution better, so they see you as the expert, which justifies a much higher price. Niching enables this specificity.
