### POD-0075#01 | FRAMEWORK | topic: promotions-launches | also: paid-ads | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:03:09
claim: A campaign has three parts: traffic source, offer and conversion mechanism, and the conversion mechanism is arguably the most important.
detail: The mechanism covers lead to appointment, show and sale. A superior mechanism lets his clients buy traffic better, which is why they make so much money, not necessarily because of a superior offer.
numbers: Example alternative offer: 12-week transformation at $3,000.
### POD-0075#02 | DECISION_RULE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads | fw: CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:29
claim: The most you can spend per lead to break even equals your lead-to-sale conversion rate times first-sale price.
detail: A gym converting 5% of leads on a $600 offer breaks even at $30 a lead, which is not bad. Another gym that converts far better can pay up to about $250 a lead on the same offer and keeps its recurring members on the back end. Those with weaker conversion should improve it, not change the offer.
numbers: 5% x $600 = $30 per lead break-even; top gym ceiling about $250 a lead; that gym spent about $35 a lead and got 15:1 back (about $1.8M yearly revenue).

### POD-0075#03 | BENCHMARK | topic: paid-ads | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:09:55
claim: Facebook ad cost rose about fivefold in six years, and an offer survives until cost per lead hits your break-even.
detail: CPM went from about $6 to about $25-$30 for gyms; at 3 leads per 1,000 impressions, a $25 CPM is about $8.33 a lead. Even at a $125 CPM (television-like) that is roughly $42 a lead (he says about $45), still well below the $250 ceiling of the best converter. His own lead ceiling stayed around $200, so rising costs did not affect his promotions.
numbers: CPM $6 to $25-$30; $25 CPM / 3 leads = $8.33; $125 CPM = about $42-45 per lead.

### POD-0075#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: paid-ads | also: scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:34
claim: Traffic platforms rise and fall in cost every few years, while offers last much longer, so be ready to switch platforms.
detail: Early on, cheap penny clicks to static pages and 21-day trials worked. Then bigger brands arrive and raise the cost for all, and smart operators move to the next platform. He cited a talk saying Facebook had about four to five more years of cheap advertising. Offers cycle more slowly because they rest on human psychology. Failing campaigns are usually tired creative.
numbers: Offers change about every 3 to 5 years; platform golden window about 4-5 years.

### POD-0075#05 | BENCHMARK | topic: promotions-launches | also: paid-ads | fw: Promotion Refresh Order | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:05:56
claim: The six-week challenge has converted best over the last six years and is the best offer for now, though he says another offer may be found that does better.
detail: He ran the challenge at his own gym for six straight years without a break because he could outspend competitors.
numbers: Six-week challenge best-converting offer over the last six years.
### POD-0075#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: promotions-launches | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Client-Financed Acquisition | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:19
claim: Saturation fears are overblown: if many rivals run the same promotion it is probably because they can afford the traffic.
detail: In a city of a million people there may be 500 gyms and five using the challenge; many owners still just give free weeks. Staying on the original approach means being outspent by those using the modern model, like taxis against ride apps. With a client-financed model any new hook can be found because others keep testing.
