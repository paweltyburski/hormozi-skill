### POD-0583#01 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: promotions-launches | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:01:36
claim: A charity fundraiser cut its ticket supply and lifted the price from $15,000 to $25,000 despite higher demand, and had its best night ever.
detail: Hormozi attended a celebrity fundraiser where a major donor had advised the CEO: when demand rises, cut supply. The extra revenue arrived before the event began, attendees were better qualified as big donors, and every auction item was sold as one-of-a-kind and non-repeatable. Pairs of top bidders were offered bonuses so the charity captured both donations. The same items would not have fetched those prices elsewhere.
numbers: Ticket $15,000 to $25,000; about $1M extra raised before the event; about $5.4M from only about 100 people (roughly $54,000 each, derived); an item worth $10,000 elsewhere sold for $100,000 there.

### POD-0583#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:50
claim: All marketing aims at two things: raise demand and lower perceived supply, so the same product sells for more and in higher volume.
detail: Bonuses, scarcity, urgency, guarantees and naming together shift the demand curve right. This assumes a normal business, not one buying market share for another strategic advantage.

### POD-0583#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: promotions-launches | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:06:35
claim: Desire grows from unmet want, so to raise demand you must deliberately sell fewer units than you could.
detail: Satisfied desire disappears. Worked comparison for a two-day workshop: sell 10 seats at one price to everyone, versus sell two premium one-on-one days at ten times the price. The second earns more with lower cost, gives more value per client and leaves the other eight with unsatisfied desire, plus new prospects who see others loving it.
numbers: Scenario A: 10 units at $500; scenario B: 2 one-day workshops at $5,000 each.

### POD-0583#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: promotions-launches | also: pricing | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:08:08
claim: In his example, re-run a sold-out premium offer with three spots instead of two at the same price; re-running the cheap, fully satisfied offer would probably sell fewer slots the second time.
detail: Pent-up demand lets the next round fill again. Promoting before demand has built ("pulling the trigger early") shrinks each later sale until none remain. Keep supply below the demand you can generate; the longer the delay before the ask, the bigger the ask.
numbers: Example: second round opens 3 spots at the same price.

### POD-0583#10 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: verticals | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:16:44
claim: For physical products run limited releases (flavours, colours, sizes), always sell out, announce the sell-out, and do it no more than about monthly.
detail: Under-order rather than over-order so the scarcity is real. Announcing the sell-out gives fence-sitters social proof and makes next time more urgent. The effect stacks if repeated. Chanel is the cited master: one or two of each piece per store, so each item is the last or second to last.
numbers: Roughly once a month; example run of 100 boxes.

### POD-0583#11 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: sales-process | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:18:19
claim: Three ways to cap services: total client cap, weekly intake cap, and cohort cap.
detail: Total cap with a waiting list removes price resistance when a slot opens; raise capacity 10-20% periodically, then cap again; raise prices and replace weaker accounts. Weekly cap: state how many openings remain this week and what the other scheduled calls mean. Cohort cap: start clients monthly or quarterly with a fixed seat count; also gives the team operating cadence.
numbers: Capacity raise of 10-20% per step; examples: 25 clients total, 5 new clients per week, 100 clients four times a year.
applies_to: Agencies and services; best for highest tiers.

### POD-0583#12 | DECISION_RULE | topic: promotions-launches | also: offers | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:20:02
claim: For one-off workshops, events and consulting, offer fewer spots than you think you can sell so the next round is remembered as sold out.
detail: This is a compounding tactic that gains strength over time. It works best on higher-ticket upsells that need access to you.

### POD-0583#13 | TACTIC | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: offers | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:20:51
claim: Cap a free lead magnet per week and keep a notify-me list for the next batch.
detail: A free checklist limited to a small number of downloads each week makes people try, find it gone, join the alert list and then act at once next time. Scarcity also raises the chance the item is actually consumed.

### POD-0583#14 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: sales-process | fw: Scarcity | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:21:38
claim: The most honest scarcity is to pick a real capacity number and advertise how full you are.
detail: Telling prospects you are three quarters booked this week, or 81% of total capacity, moves them over the line, and implies social proof. You decide where "full" is.
numbers: Examples: 75% of weekly capacity; 81% of total capacity.

### POD-0583#18 | TACTIC | topic: promotions-launches | also: sales-process | fw: Urgency | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:25:27
claim: For rolling cohorts, tie the close to the next start date and, if true, mention a recent dropout freeing a slot.
detail: Less frequent starts make it stronger, even every other week helps. If someone buys just after a cohort starts, either give a fast personal onboarding as a bonus, or (his preference) show the benefits of waiting: more time to review materials and consult staff or family, and a longer payment plan only available because of the distant start.
