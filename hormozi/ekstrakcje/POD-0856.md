### POD-0856#01 | TACTIC | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:33
claim: Writing happens only on days with six to eight uninterrupted hours, and the calendar is cleared around heavy writing seasons.
detail: Anything scheduled later in the day nags like an open loop and prevents deep immersion, so he writes only on fully empty days. In a writing "season of no" the team knows he takes meetings on just two days, leaving about five empty days a week, one of which usually gets hijacked.
numbers: 6-8 hours uninterrupted per writing day; 2 meeting days and ~5 free days per week in a writing season.

### POD-0856#02 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:50
claim: He builds a book from the table of contents first, then gives every chapter the same repeated structure.
detail: The table of contents is the hardest and most time-consuming step and serves as the game plan. Each chapter: a story for context, a short description of the idea, plentiful examples, and his notes at the end. The hardest choice per chapter is the story that embodies the principle and the visual that ties it together. Words come first, rough doodles next, final drawings last because paragraphs get moved.

### POD-0856#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:06:29
claim: Every framework is tested on validity (is it true, in how many situations) and utility (does it change what someone does).
detail: "Sometimes things happen" is valid but useless; much sales and marketing lore is useful in one scenario but not valid across scenarios. He looks for the principle that holds everywhere by trying to break the model, and he describes his writing as about two-thirds discovery and one-third getting out what he already has. If he cannot break it, it is done.
### POD-0856#05 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: Value Equation | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:54
claim: A good framework is mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, and survives other people re-publishing it with changes.
detail: The Value Equation took years to crystallise, and copies change the icons but keep its four variables. A triangle with three arbitrary items is a weak framework since it is easy to break. If many people morph a model, it is probably not right. Test: any counterexample that does not fit means the model is wrong; "more, better, different" is a similar exhaustive split.

### POD-0856#06 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:42:35
claim: A concept is worth including only if it can be operationalised: it must tell the reader what to do.
detail: This was his main cutting filter, so the books contain almost no theory. Method: take an abstract term (branding, sales) and ask what action you would take to do it; sales is defined as a conversation that raises the likelihood someone buys. If the behaviour change cannot be stated, break the idea down further or cut it.

### POD-0856#08 | EXAMPLE | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:15:12
claim: The Leads book title was found by sequential A/B tests, with the winner of each round entering the next.
detail: Rounds: advertising beat promotion; leads beat advertising; leads narrowly beat marketing. Reasoning: leads is the output of advertising, and nobody wants to advertise, they want leads. The realistic cover image beat a cartoon version. He tested three or four back-end image variations too. The blue colour had no logic.

### POD-0856#09 | EXAMPLE | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:17:30
claim: Subtitle tests were highly sensitive to single words, and shorter did not win.
detail: He tried five or six subtitles. "Strangers" narrowly beat "more people". A one-word difference ("more strangers" versus "strangers") split 71 to 29, and "how to get..." beat the shorter "get..." by about 70 to 30. Conclusion: concision was not what buyers rewarded.
numbers: 71/29 and 70/30 splits in subtitle tests (which side won on the first split is unclear in audio; the published subtitle has no "more").

### POD-0856#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: content-brand | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:10
claim: Optimise a book's cover and title for strangers, since existing fans will buy anyway.
detail: Readers of the first book would buy a "book two" regardless; the work is aimed at the split-second decision of someone who does not know him: clear promise, output obvious. Testing a title is far cheaper than writing the book, so test before committing; he cites a bestseller title that Tim Ferriss disliked but which won, and a dating book that sold after only a cover and title change.

### POD-0856#11 | TACTIC | topic: content-brand | also: sales-process | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:57

### POD-0856#16 | DECISION_RULE | topic: content-brand | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:27:09
claim: Hormozi's approach to reader feedback: quick-fix single suggestions he agrees with, ignore ones he doesn't, and when many readers comment on the same section, he strongly considers deleting it, and most times does.
detail: When many readers have different comments on one section, he says usually it is more that something is wrong with the section than with the details. In the third book, two or three short early chapters drew comments from every reader; he deleted them, redefined a core term wherever it appeared, and the book "read downhill".
### POD-0856#17 | EXAMPLE | topic: money-models | also: sales-process | fw: Point of Greatest Deprivation | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:30:40
claim: Sell the next offer when your own solution creates a new need, not when the customer is satisfied.
detail: A marketing agency that delivers many leads finds the client no longer wants more leads, but now needs help working them; that new deprivation is the moment to offer the next service. Hormozi calls it a copy mistake to ignore timing in the prospect's journey.

### POD-0856#20 | DECISION_RULE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Core Four | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:34:37
claim: Choose one-to-one versus one-to-many by the target's size (probably), and any lever (affiliates included) still goes back to the Core Four.
detail: If you can predict an outcome from its variables you can control it. Targeting Fortune 100 firms probably points to one-to-one; mass-market weight loss probably to one-to-many. Within one-to-many choose paid ads or content. Affiliates are reached by outreach, content or ads first.

### POD-0856#26 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:54:56

### POD-0856#28 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: content-brand | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 01:01:14
claim: Put the review request in the middle of the book, once the reader has had value, and treat asking as a confidence test.
detail: Placing it at the midpoint assumes those still reading liked it. Asking raises review likelihood, but a weak book gets bad reviews. His rule: a launch shows marketing skill; sales two years later show book quality.
