### LC-11#01 | DEFINITION | topic: lead-getters | also: strategy-positioning | fw: Leverage | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:00
claim: Leverage means the difference between effort in and results out; it is not about pace — one action producing a thousand results beats making 400 calls rather than 200.
detail: A person who is a thousand times ahead of you is not working a thousand times faster, just working on different things. So a distant-seeming future may be nearer than it looks. A $100M leads machine rests on leverage.
quote: Don't mistake leverage for speed.

### LC-11#02 | FRAMEWORK | topic: lead-getters | also: hiring-talent | fw: Leverage | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:19
claim: Four scenarios of rising leverage: you get the leads; you get one lead getter; you add a lead getter every day; you get a lead getter who gets lead getters.
detail: Only you and other people can tell strangers about your stuff, and there are more of them. 1) Solopreneur doing the Rule of 100. 2) One day of outreach recruits someone who then does it daily — 365 days of leads for one day's work. 3) Recruit one more each day; soon several people each do 100 a day. 4) Recruit a recruiter, who brings cold callers, emailers, content and media-buying teams — endless leads. This is how portfolio companies get ~20,000 leads a day while Hormozi makes training videos.
numbers: ~20,000 leads/day across portfolio companies.

### LC-11#03 | FRAMEWORK | topic: lead-getters | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:14
claim: Complete picture of advertising: the Core Four you do, plus four lead getters doing the Core Four for you — customers, employees, agencies, affiliates.
detail: Customers refer via their own warm outreach or posts. Employees work inside the business. Agencies sit outside it but run the same methods. Affiliates are the most complex but among the highest-scale sources of ongoing leads for one-time work. The Core Four builds a few million to ~$10–20M; lead getters build an empire.

### LC-11#07 | DEFINITION | topic: lead-getters | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:26
claim: A referral is an engaged lead sent by a referrer; customers make the best referrers because referred customers are worth more and cost less.
detail: Referred customers have higher LTGP — they spend more, faster — and cost little or nothing to acquire. Against a 3:1 LTGP:CAC goal, little beats "costs nothing, spends more".

### LC-11#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: unit-economics | fw: Referral Rate minus Churn | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:07
claim: Every other method is linear, but referrals compound — one tells two, two tell four.
detail: Linear: $100 of ads → $500, $10K → $50K; outreach the same per 100 reach-outs. If more customers refer each month than leave, you grow forever, very profitably, with no acquisition cost — the holy grail.

### LC-11#09 | FRAMEWORK | topic: retention-ltv | also: lead-getters, unit-economics | fw: Referral Rate minus Churn | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:07
claim: Monthly referral rate minus monthly churn rate equals your compounding growth rate without advertising.
detail: Referrals > churn: grow forever without ads. Equal: you maintain and need ads to grow (common). Less: you advertise just to stay level — the "hamster wheel of death": more leads come in, the product disappoints, they leave, you need even more.
numbers: 10% referring − 5% churning = 5%/month growth.

### LC-11#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: product-delivery, scaling-constraints | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:44

detail: Until his product itself was exceptional, marketing was never enough; once people told others, life got far easier, and he won't build without it again. Something must compound against rising media costs.

### LC-11#12 | FRAMEWORK | topic: pricing | also: lead-getters, offers | fw: Goodwill (Price-Value-Cost) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:20
claim: Goodwill is value received minus price paid; big goodwill creates referrers, neutral creates silence, negative creates haters.
detail: Value − price = goodwill (consumer surplus); price − cost = profit. Paying $10 for a burrito worth $12 to you = $2 goodwill. Cutting price adds goodwill but cuts profit. The controllable lever is value: you can't control each person's perception, but you can control what gets most people value. Raising value lets you raise price and keep both profit and goodwill.

### LC-11#13 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: Goodwill (Price-Value-Cost) | novelty: canon | ts: 00:15:07
claim: Price and perceived value move each other both ways — higher price can raise perceived value, yet the same Chipotle burrito sold at $50 might feel like a rip-off to some people (he says he himself avoids the word).
detail: Reconciles the $100M Offers wine example (priciest wine rated best). Because the relationship is bidirectional, pricing is one of the largest profit levers.

### LC-11#16 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: sales-process, product-delivery | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:27
claim: Sell better customers: find what your top 20% of customers share and filter for it at the front end — better prospects get better results from the same product.
detail: Qualified buyers extract more value, so prospect quality raises effective product quality. Many sell to under-qualified buyers from fear of saying no; taking fast money blocks the big money later.

### LC-11#17 | CASE | topic: lead-getters | also: sales-process, verticals | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:17:27
claim: Gym Launch found its best clients had a signed lease, at least one employee and at least 30 members; it stopped selling anyone lacking them, and results, marketing and customer quality all rose — with no product change.
detail: Sales was told not to sell outside the criteria; marketing stated them openly. Better buyers → higher averages → better marketing claims → better buyers: a virtuous cycle.

### LC-11#18 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: lead-getters, retention-ltv | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:19:35
claim: Set better expectations: most businesses over-promise and under-deliver; raise real value so high that even a bold promise sits below what you deliver.
detail: Some $100M Offers readers made outlandish claims they couldn't fulfil, breeding customers who hate them. Lower expectations relative to delivery mean more goodwill. Making everything valuable is why business is hard — and rewarding.

### LC-11#19 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: retention-ltv, sales-process | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:20:40
claim: Set factual expectations: publish what the average customer achieved at months 1, 5, 12 and 24 — "this is what has happened", not "this will happen to you".
detail: Data can't be disputed and forces you to raise the average. Marketing on your two or three best stories inflates expectations: more persuasive ad, worse business. Harvard publishes graduation rates and graduates' average income, anchoring expectations on data.

### LC-11#20 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: offers, product-delivery | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:22:16
claim: Get more people better results in six steps, ending by making the winning actions the conditions of your guarantee.
detail: 1) Survey to find who got the best results or experience. 2) Interview them: how were they different (→ qualification) and what did they do differently (→ process)? 3) Find shared actions. 4) Require new customers to repeat them. 5) Measure the change in average results, speed and outcome. 6) Tie guarantee conditions to those actions. The guarantee sells more; the conditions get results. Customers believe more because they know what it takes, and the pitch becomes "does this sound reasonable?"

### LC-11#21 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers, strategy-positioning | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:01
claim: Decrease time delay: one of the easiest business models is doing what everyone else does in half the time.
detail: If a win takes 100 days, find how to do it in 7, 3, then 24 hours. An agency promising leads in ~60 days versus a client whose phone rings with a lead as the sales call ends — same deliverable, far more value.

### LC-11#22 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:24:53
claim: Split big outcomes into small milestones and report progress as often as possible, so customers feel rewarded more often and results feel faster.
detail: Even selling a house breaks into steps. A design agency sending daily progress instead of a weekly list gives seven wins instead of one.

### LC-11#23 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:26:25
claim: Choreograph a customer's first 28 days — especially the first 48 hours, when they judge the business — with small promises kept exactly.
detail: Early on customers are most at risk and decide whether to refer; many businesses go silent after the sale. Example: "Email within 30 minutes; Angie calls in two hours with the onboarding packet; Sandra sets up your account tomorrow; ads go live the next day" — then notify them when ads go live. After a few kept promises they expect the next and tell friends "they run a tight ship".
numbers: First 28 days; judgment within 48 hours.

### LC-11#24 | TACTIC | topic: retention-ltv | also: product-delivery | fw: BAMFAM | novelty: canon | ts: 00:28:10
claim: BAMFAM (book a meeting from a meeting): never leave a customer in limbo; they should always know the next step and its timing.
detail: Credited to Sharran Srivatsaa (heard as "Sharon Servatza"), president of a large public real-estate company. Hand over to the next step every time; not knowing what comes next breeds anxiety, cancellations and bad reviews. Don't count on being forgiven for errors — prevent them — even though accumulated goodwill helps.

### LC-11#25 | TACTIC | topic: product-delivery | also: retention-ltv | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:28:58
claim: Reduce effort and sacrifice with a continuous six-step improvement loop on the most common complaint.
detail: 1) Mine service data, surveys and reviews for top problems. 2) Design a fix. 3) Build it. 4) Test on struggling customers (not your best) or a representative sample. 5) If feedback shows it's solved, roll out; if not, back to step 2. 6) Next complaint; repeat forever. Front-end tests cut CAC; this back-end test raises LTGP.

### LC-11#26 | TACTIC | topic: money-models | also: retention-ltv | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:29:58
claim: Always tell customers what to buy next: another purchase is one of the strongest retention tools, and when their urge to buy returns they'll satisfy it with you or someone else.
detail: Each offer should lead to the next, stated clearly: "Once you complete X you'll qualify for Y, which unlocks these benefits. You're not ready yet — want us to tell you when you are?" Customers whose expectations were met believe the new ones, buy, LTGP rises, and word of mouth grows.

### LC-11#27 | DIAGNOSTIC_Q | topic: lead-getters | also: product-delivery, retention-ltv | fw: Six Ways to Increase Value | novelty: canon | ts: 00:30:50

detail: Use as a team exercise (e.g., Monday meeting). What would make their experience so valuable they send all their friends? What results, what onboarding? Which customer would you pick? Who would you need to be? Do those things now.

### LC-11#36 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: sales-process | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:36:11
claim: Ask at the moment of purchase — on the contract or checkout — framed around the buyer's benefit: doing it with a friend gets better results.
detail: Collect names and numbers. Research shows people starting new behavior with someone succeed more: "Easiest way to double your results? Bring a friend. Who?" The friend can come free or split the cost. Ask who they know, not whether they know anyone.

### LC-11#37 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: objections-closing, pricing | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:37:23
claim: Use referrals as a negotiation chip: trade a requested discount for introductions — e.g., $500 down to $400 for three friends.
detail: Changing the terms lets you ethically charge differently for the same thing. Useful for small sellers squeezed by bigger buyers: "I can't charge you less than others for the same service, but if you introduce me to people I can do X." Ask for three; fall back to two.
applies_to: Solopreneurs and small B2B sellers.

### LC-11#38 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: promotions-launches, retention-ltv | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:38:17
claim: Referral events reward points, credits, cash or bragging rights for bringing friends within a one-to-four-week window, while selling the benefits of doing it together.
detail: Pitch accountability: bring-a-friend challenges, bring your spouse. Events deepen community, and guests surrounded by enthusiastic customers get sold by them. Especially good for local businesses.
numbers: 1–4 weeks.

### LC-11#40 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: retention-ltv, offers | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:40:41
claim: Offer a valuable bonus unlocked only by referring a friend and leaving a testimonial, tied to status — often stronger than money.
detail: VIP service, faster responses, premium session times, a course, a PDF, an event. Advertise it as a VIP perk. People feel awkward taking cash for friends but not unlocking status, and doing it with a friend makes them more successful.

### LC-11#41 | DECISION_RULE | topic: lead-getters | also: offers | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:41:54
claim: Match the referral incentive to your core product so it attracts people who want what you sell — why store credit and gift cards work.
detail: Dropbox gave storage, PayPal credit; a t-shirt maker giving t-shirts attracts buyers, an IT firm giving t-shirts may not. Frame credit as a gift: "a $200 card, good here only — give it to a friend"; the friend feels obliged to use it and the referrer gets credit for the favor.

### LC-11#42 | TACTIC | topic: lead-getters | also: promotions-launches, unit-economics | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:42:47
claim: Gift-card play: give each new customer a card worth up to a third of your price, usable by a friend who signs up and expiring in 7–14 days.
detail: Size it on gross profit, keeping LTGP:CAC above 3:1. The referrer gives a big gift ("I've got a $2,000 card, don't want to waste it") instead of a discount pitch. Have them text a photo of the card to the friend; writing the friend's name on it first personalizes it and justifies collecting their contact. Extension: sell such cards at 90% off as holiday gifts (a $2,000 card for $100), limit two per person — paid to acquire customers. Adapt, don't copy.
numbers: Card ≤ 1/3 of price; 7–14 day expiry; e.g., $2,000 card sold for $100.

### LC-11#43 | PRINCIPLE | topic: lead-getters | also: offers | fw: Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals | novelty: canon | ts: 00:46:15
claim: Referring risks the customer's goodwill with a friend, so they refer only when confident the friend will be well served — maximize their benefit and status, minimize perceived risk.
detail: As with an offer: a great product (every promise kept so far), a referrer incentive, a friend bonus. Give the referrer status as a great friend. Each closes a crack until the dam breaks. Make good stuff and ask.
