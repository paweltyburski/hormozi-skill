### POD-0466#01 | BENCHMARK | topic: verticals | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:00:52
claim: An average frozen yogurt store takes about $750,000-$800,000 a year but nets 10-15%, leaving owners modest income.
detail: Average daily revenue is $2,191, of which about $328 a day is owner pay; a Menchie's owner takes home around $93,000 a year. Variable costs are high because cost of goods rises with each cup, plus spoilage, machine repairs and a prime lease.
numbers: Revenue $750,000-$800,000 per year; margin 10-15%; $2,191 per day; owner pay $328/day; ~$93,000/year.
applies_to: Retail food franchises

### POD-0466#02 | BENCHMARK | topic: finance-exit | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:38
claim: A franchise fee of about 6% of sales takes roughly 60% of a store's profit at a 10% margin.
detail: Franchisors set fees so the operator earns just enough to carry on, aiming for returns of 20-25% against about 10% in the stock market. In theory bulk purchasing across 200+ locations saves costs, but many franchisors upcharge supplies.
numbers: Fee ~6% of top line; target franchisor return 20-25% vs ~10%.

### POD-0466#03 | TACTIC | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:03:06
claim: Buy equipment from business foreclosure and liquidation listings, refreshing weekly until a suitable one appears.
detail: He bought his first gym's equipment from a failed gym for $13,000. Three failed yogurt franchises were listed, the dearest with everything included at $20,000. Someone has usually failed in the business you want, and buying from a motivated seller at 5-10 cents on the dollar can fit a $20,000 start. A franchise would not allow this.
numbers: Gym equipment $13,000; yogurt store at most $20,000; 5-10 cents on the dollar.
applies_to: Low-capital startups

### POD-0466#04 | BENCHMARK | topic: unit-economics | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:04:17
claim: Yogurt is the margin and toppings are the cost: yogurt costs about 8 cents an ounce and sells at 50-60 cents.
detail: Average cup is 8 ounces and about 75% yogurt, 25% toppings by weight. Toppings cost 10-40 cents an ounce and sell at 25-60 cents, so fruit can lose money. About 500 cups a day is implied by daily revenue.
numbers: Cup 8 oz; 75/25 yogurt to toppings; yogurt cost ~$0.08/oz; ~500 cups per day.

### POD-0466#05 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: verticals | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:32
claim: A commoditized industry can be won by beating the worst quarter of competitors.
detail: Yogurt chains are near-identical because franchisors sold locations instead of building brands; no Chick-fil-A exists in the category. Sticky floors, unattended counters and broken nozzles are typical, so basic cleanliness and service win.

### POD-0466#06 | TACTIC | topic: pricing | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:07:21
claim: Let customers choose how much they spend (by weight) so they blame themselves, not the price.
detail: With fixed small, medium and large cups the shopper sees a price and thinks the place is expensive; with self-serve by weight, they filled the cup themselves and own the amount. The same applies to usage-based pricing such as phone minutes.

### POD-0466#07 | EXAMPLE | topic: pricing | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:08:09
claim: Removing the small cup probably lifted sales 20-30% because shoppers fill whatever size they hold.
detail: His guess is that a small-cup stockout revealed it. Bigger cups raise spend by projecting an expectation.
numbers: Sales +20-30% (his guess).

### POD-0466#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: objections-closing | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:17:19
claim: Set the default so more people take the larger option; framing changes yes-rates a lot.
detail: Examples are fast-food "assume medium" upsells and organ donation, where opt-in rates of about 10-15% reached about 80% when the question was reversed. He calls it one of the most powerful and unnoticed selling tools.
numbers: Organ donor opt-in ~10-15% to ~80% (his recollection).

### POD-0466#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-process | also: money-models | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:11
claim: Direct attention first to the item with the best margin, not the one that costs you most.
detail: Shoppers are most likely to buy, and buy the most, where they look first, so lead with the profitable item to use up as much of their spending power as possible on high-profit things.
### POD-0466#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: product-delivery | also: offers | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:16
claim: More non-conflicting choices make people consume more; conflicting choices reduce buying.
detail: Buffets show it: guests sample a bit of everything. 26 jams, of which you can pick one, conflict. In his imagined store he would build a floor-to-ceiling display, many nozzles and a clunky pour so cups come out heavier.

### POD-0466#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: paid-ads | also: lead-getters | fw: Lead Getters | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:46
claim: With a very low average ticket, paid ads rarely pay, so rely on word of mouth and affiliates.
detail: Top-performing stores credit service, cleanliness, selection and above all word of mouth. Search ads were affordable years ago but are too expensive now. Without funding, referrals and affiliates are the two cheapest acquisition routes.

### POD-0466#12 | TACTIC | topic: outreach | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:13:59
claim: Offer 50% off the first order for joining the text list, because a lead's lifetime value outweighs a $2 cost.
detail: The usual "join our list?" ask fails, whereas half off makes it worth it. He accepts giving up half the profit on one order to own a reachable lead for later coupons and friend referrals.
numbers: 50% off first order; lead cost about $2.
