claim: A $1M crypto bet outside his expertise lost about half within weeks, and he treats it as pure gambling.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#02 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:11

A mentor's advice he repeats: if you know how to create wealth, create it and do not dabble in investing. All his large gains came from marketing and sales arbitrage, such as buying a customer for $10 who is worth $1,000, then pushing cash into it.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: operator-mindset | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:02:07
claim: When he feels fear of missing out on an investment, he stops and waits.
detail: He saw peers making quick, easy money and wanted the same. His rule now is that an idea everyone loves is usually a bad one, and that "tickle" of missing out is the signal to pause rather than act.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#04 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:02:07
claim: His first real estate deal, bought out of state from a friend's contact, was a scam: the seller had paid $19,000 and flipped it to him for $50,000.
detail: With about $20M in cash after a few years of Gym Launch, he bought one cheap Ohio rental to test it. ### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#06 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:57

detail: Because his investing horizon is long, he asks how long the person has been in it. He describes having assumed that since a business friend was making money he could too, and that friend lost money as well.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#07 | DECISION_RULE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:07:00

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:09:42
claim: A partner is justified only if they bring time, money or skill that you lack; otherwise one of you is not needed.
detail: His first gym partnership, arranged by an advisor, split the equity 45/45/10 between two people with the same skills and the same business, both bringing work and almost no capital. Most founders start exactly this way. He counts nine failed partnerships before he began making money, and later successes were solo or with his wife.
numbers: Advisor took 10%, partners 45% each; nine failed partnerships.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:09:42
claim: Most of the equity you will ever lose is lost on day one, and equity does not need to be equal.
detail: Founders obsess over the sale price but give away half or two thirds at the start by splitting equal thirds among friends. Compare each person's contribution of time, money and skill, and negotiate honestly now, because the more money the company makes, the more the split matters. A 95/5 split is fine if it matches contributions, and a partner who balks is better discovered on day one. You can almost always run the business without them.
numbers: Example split 95/5.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#10 | CASE | topic: finance-exit | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:11:02
claim: He sold two thirds of a gym business that was already earning for roughly what it cost to start, to wealthy partners who did nothing in the business.
detail: He ran the business full time, invested the capital and held all the fitness knowledge; the partners were older, richer, took no salary and were not present. He had saved about $70,000 in the first six months, yet each month's profit exceeded what he got for the whole two thirds. He calls it paying down ignorance debt.
numbers: About $70k saved in six months; two thirds sold at cost to start.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#11 | DECISION_RULE | topic: management-ops | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:12:03
claim: Owners who work in the business should draw a market salary on top of their equity.
detail: Ownership is one thing and working in the business is another; the salary makes up for doing the job and taking on the risk. When founders say they take home nothing because they reinvest everything, he asks for their return on capital. With no answer, the company is not reinvesting in growth, it simply is not profitable.
### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#12 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:17:41
claim: The person who brings in customers and makes the money (rainmaker) deserves a disproportionate share, and needed functions do not earn equity.
detail: If the business works without them but not without you, you are needed and they are not. An accountant is required in every business, but you hire one or use a vendor instead of giving equity. Only roles core to acquiring or delivering for customers justify meaningful equity. A clean partnership pairs one who makes the product excellent with one who tells the world about it.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#13 | DEFINITION | topic: strategy-positioning | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:19:58
claim: A bad partnership is a flawed structure with decent people; a bad partner is someone with harmful intent.
detail: The two need to be judged separately because the person and the structure of the relationship must both be aligned. His earlier partners were ethical but badly structured; the last one was deliberately harmful.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#14 | CASE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:21:02
claim: After selling his gyms, he partnered on a gym he opened and filled, then lost all the cash to a partner with a fraud history.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#16 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:24:41

detail: At a defence consulting job he learned quickly for six to nine months, then drifted, read books all day and was a poor employee. The lasting damage was to his self-confidence. His test: if you count minutes and dread the hours left, leave, because any alternative is better.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#17 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:27:08
claim: A prepared-meal business sold to his gym base made about 7% profit and created most of the support work.
detail: He found co-packers able to handle overnight volume, launched with his audience and sold about $600,000 in month one. The average meal cost $10 to the customer and $9 to produce, leaving little to cover marketing, payroll, support, refunds and broken boxes. Meals produced about 80% of support complaints but only 10% of profit, versus 90% of profit from supplements. He estimates total profit near $500,000 for a year of effort.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#18 | DECISION_RULE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: unit-economics | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:28:11
claim: Before entering an unfamiliar category, ask people who actually profit in it how they make money.
detail: He never did, and the only food-industry contacts he had (including his co-packer, who kept raising funding) were losing money. A side project that makes money can still be a loss if the same effort would have lifted the main business by even 10%.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#19 | EXAMPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: finance-exit | fw: More Better New | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:29:34
claim: Splitting focus onto small side projects cost him an estimated $10-25M at the sale of Gym Launch.
detail: Because the company was sold on a profit multiple, two or three million dollars of lost extra profit became tens of millions of lost sale value. This is the origin of his repeated "more, better" message.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#20 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:13:57
claim: Spend only where you can say how the spend returns money, and expect 3:1 to 10:1 on small-business spending.
detail: His second gym cost $250,000 (glass lobby, bigger retail unit) against $37,000 for the first in a warehouse, yet produced the same revenue, of about $20-25k a month within six months at the first. A $25,000 expense that returns $25,000 over ten years is a loss. Small companies can beat big ones by buying leads cheaply and selling a $500 product to about one in five.
numbers: First location $37k, 5,000 sq ft; second $250k, 7,000 sq ft; target return on spend 3:1, 5:1 or 10:1.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#21 | PRINCIPLE | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:15:01
claim: Don't be cute: execute fundamentals very well, because fancy strategies fumble.
detail: A private-equity CEO who tripled three companies in about three years told him to play "backyard football". Simple promises such as superior service work only when backed by systems (staff smiling, using names, handwritten cards). A product rated 4.7 versus 4.4 in the same Amazon category may sell ten times more, so small quality gains on the basics beat novelty.
numbers: 4.7 vs 4.4 stars can mean about 10x sales; PE CEO tripled companies in about 3 years, three times.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#22 | CASE | topic: hiring-talent | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:30:47
claim: An HR director's unilateral cost-of-living raise cost him about $600,000 a year of profit for no benefit.
detail: After she was denied a raise for lack of experience, she ordered a cost-of-living study and gave raises only to staff in expensive areas, herself included, announced in a mass email. He removed her. He notes that a raise should be tied to a behaviour so it can be rewarded.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#23 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:32:46

detail: He does not need every detail, but when a department answers "all good" he must know how to check below the surface. Distance from departments let the pay change happen unnoticed.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#24 | CASE | topic: management-ops | also: finance-exit | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:32:46

The in-house team happily outsourced the work to a friendly firm paid with company money. The CFO cut it, saving about $1M a year, but he puts the total loss at $1.2M.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#25 | CASE | topic: verticals | also: product-delivery | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:35:57
claim: An outsourced-development software venture designed to work gym leads cost over $1M and left him at the vendor's mercy.
detail: The bot got 19% of leads into the door versus about 10% for an average front-desk employee. A $100,000 quote ballooned past $1M; he continued because of sunk cost. After launch the developer raised fees as profits showed and left the code undocumented, so no other firm could take it over. He eventually sold it to a software company with its own developers.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#26 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:37:16
claim: He calls outsourcing the core value generation of a business a fundamental mistake; unless you have an in-house person who knows more than the vendor and can hold them accountable (who probably needs equity), you are at their mercy.
detail: The technical owner checks documentation, re-bids work, and over time moves staff in-house (even overseas, as long as they are loyal to you). Dev shops rely on non-technical founders making this mistake. The test he offers: name a large software company without a technical co-founder.
### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#27 | CASE | topic: product-delivery | also: promotions-launches | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:39:29
claim: A $270,000 vendor paid to guarantee launch capacity failed, and about 75% of launch traffic never reached the offer, a $15M loss.
detail: A lower-level vendor employee admitted shortly before launch that volumes were breaking records and that extra systems had to be spun up. Switching so close to the date seemed riskier, so they launched and lost the traffic. He thinks the vendor oversold to get a logo on its site.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#28 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:41:16
claim: Vet vendors by asking for references at your scale and use case, and treat friend referrals as if you had never met the vendor.
detail: Nobody had asked the vendor for a single customer who had done this at the size claimed. He says early warning signs were ignored, and bad news is worse after the event than before. He tells the referrer that he will ask for many assurances anyway, and walks away if they take offence.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#29 | CASE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:43:17
claim: In 2019 he cut his coaching price for gyms from $4,000 to about $3,200 a month while adding heavy one-on-one accountability, and lost about $5M a year in profit.
detail: A competitor had poached his top gyms with a partnership offer and lower price. He added status groups of the best 35 gyms grouped by revenue to help each other, a complex new operation, and cut the weekly price from $1,000 to $800 for everyone including existing clients. Customers complained instead of thanking him.
numbers: $1,000/week to $800/week (about $4k/month to $3.2k); about 20% less revenue; about $5M a year profit lost; 35 gyms.

### YT-tdLLGKoBojo#31 | PRINCIPLE | topic: pricing | also: strategy-positioning | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:45:02
claim: Every price change draws complaints, even a price decrease, and reacting to competitors is a worse driver than talking to customers.
detail: People dislike change itself. This was one of his first reactive moves; he looked at rivals' ads and copied them instead of anticipating the market. He cites Paul Graham's idea that most business problems can be solved by talking to customers, and says if you listen to customers, competitors matter little.
