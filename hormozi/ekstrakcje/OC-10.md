### OC-10#01 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: content-brand | fw: MAGIC Naming Formula | novelty: canon | ts: 00:00:58
claim: An offer may not make money because prospects do not realise it is for them, do not know what problem it solves, or do not see that it solves it the way they want.
detail: The final offer component (naming) exists so the prospect immediately recognises: this is for me, it solves my problem, and it solves it in the way I prefer. The last part matters because people want their problems solved in particular ways — the same outcome packaged differently can succeed where the original packaging failed.

### OC-10#02 | CASE | topic: offers | also: verticals, money-models | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:01:43
claim: In Hormozi's gyms, prospects who declined a membership but accepted a free nutrition consultation later bought more supplements (about $300+) than people who had bought training (about $200+).
detail: People came in asking about a gym membership, but what they really wanted was to lose weight. When someone said no to membership or training, the gym offered to help with their nutrition for free as goodwill. They would come back a day or two later for the consultation, and on average bought more supplements than clients who had bought training services. Lessons: (1) someone rejecting your core product does not mean they no longer want the problem solved — they may want it solved differently; (2) those people valued the alternative solution more, which is why they spent more. An alternative explanation is that they had more budget left, but Hormozi explains it through separate mental wallets.
numbers: Average supplement purchase ~$200+ for training clients vs ~$300+ for people who bought nothing else.
applies_to: Gyms and local services with a core service plus product upsells.

### OC-10#03 | PRINCIPLE | topic: money-models | also: pricing | fw: - | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:17
claim: People buy from separate mental "wallets" — gym membership, groceries, supplements — so selling them something in another category does not take money from your first sale.
detail: Hormozi's stance is that customers allocate separate perceived budgets to different categories. Selling a supplement or a nutrition service draws on a different wallet than a gym membership, so you are not cannibalising one purchase with another. This supports offering an alternative or additional solution when the core is declined.

### OC-10#04 | FRAMEWORK | topic: offers | also: content-brand, promotions-launches | fw: MAGIC Naming Formula | novelty: canon | ts: 00:03:17
claim: MAGIC naming formula: Make a magnetic reason why, Announce the avatar, Give them a goal, Indicate a time interval, Complete with a container word.
detail: M: make a magnetic reason why (free, discount, grand opening, back to school, season). A: announce the avatar (moms, a town's moms, gym owners). G: give them a goal (the outcome). I: indicate a time interval. C: complete with a container word (challenge, blueprint, system, makeover, fast track, intensive, giveaway, transformation). Include at least three or four of five; one word can do two jobs ("makeover" is container and goal). Not everything needs it, but it suits programs, promotions, bonuses, ad headlines and copy lead-ins, especially in niche and local markets.

### OC-10#05 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: verticals, promotions-launches | fw: MAGIC Naming Formula | novelty: canon | ts: 00:04:05
claim: Wellness and beauty names built with MAGIC: "Free Six-Week Lean by Halloween Challenge", "88% Off 12-Week Bikini Blueprint", "Free 21-Day Mommy Makeover", "60-Minute Make-Your-Friends-Jealous Model Hair System".
detail: Breakdowns: "Free six-week Lean by Halloween challenge" — reason (free, Halloween), time, goal (lean), container (challenge); no avatar. "88% off 12-week bikini blueprint" — reason (88% off), time (12 weeks), goal (bikini body), container (blueprint). "Free 21-day mommy makeover" — reason (free), time (21 days), avatar (mommy), and "makeover" works as both container and goal. "60-minute make-your-friends-jealous model hair system" — time (60 minutes), goal (make your friends jealous), container (model hair system); the avatar is implied by the wording rather than stated. Other examples: "Six-week stress release challenge" (a promotion that worked very well for one of their case-study businesses); "Free bend-over-pain-free in 42 days healing fast track" (for a pain clinic).

### OC-10#06 | EXAMPLE | topic: offers | also: verticals, promotions-launches | fw: MAGIC Naming Formula | novelty: canon | ts: 00:05:38
claim: MAGIC-built names for dental/medical and coaching: "Celebrity Smile Transformation for $2,000 Less", "Moms in Lakeway: Kids' Braces for $1,500 Off", "Back-to-School Free Braces Giveaway", "Five Clients in Five Days Blueprint", "Fill Your Gym in 30 Days Free".
detail: Doctors/dentists: "celebrity smile transformation, $2,000 off"; "moms in Lakeway, kids' braces for $1,500 less" (names the local avatar); "12 months to a perfect smile, $1,000 off for 15 families" (the cap supplies a reason why and scarcity); "Back-to-school free braces giveaway" (reason why, promotion, end goal, plus "giveaway" as the power/container word — four components); "Grand opening: free X-ray, treatment, instant relief" (reason why, benefit, the thing). Coaching: "Five clients in five days blueprint"; "Seven-figure [term unclear in audio], a 12-week intensive"; "14 days to discover your ideal product launch"; "Get your gym full in 30 days, free".

### OC-10#07 | TACTIC | topic: offers | also: product-delivery | fw: MAGIC Naming Formula | novelty: canon | ts: 00:06:26
claim: Name the overall program in days, weeks, months or quarters, but name bonuses and sub-components in seconds, minutes or hours.
detail: The core program is the big transformation (e.g. a six-month business, marriage, health or IT transformation) and is framed in longer intervals. The bonuses, features and sub-topics inside it are mini-transformations that add up to the big one, and should be framed in very short intervals — e.g. "clear your inbox in 30 minutes" inside an agency program, a "10-minute grocery calculator", "how to find properties in 60 seconds or less". Short, specific time frames make each piece far more compelling, and solving problems beyond the prospect's immediate ones also reinforces belief that the core works.

### OC-10#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: offers | also: pricing | fw: Value Equation | novelty: canon | ts: 00:08:03
claim: Stating that something takes much less time dramatically raises its value — and customers are not buying your time, they are buying getting to the goal faster.
detail: "My hair-styling system that makes everyone look like a model" is good; "a system that takes 10 seconds instead of 10 minutes" is far more valuable. A 5-minute tooth extraction is worth more than a 60-minute one. This is why Amazon Prime and Netflix's instant access took off, and why the easiest way into a market is to cut the time to result in half. Naming should communicate the speed, because it answers the prospect's biggest objections — "how long will this take?" and "will it be worth it?" — lowering perceived effort and sacrifice. Do not try to sell more of your time; the amount of time you give is irrelevant to the outcome they want.

### OC-10#09 | PRINCIPLE | topic: promotions-launches | also: verticals, paid-ads | fw: Promotion Refresh Order | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:02
claim: Local and niche promotions fatigue fast — they are easy to get working but hard to keep working, so you must refresh roughly every three weeks.
detail: In a local market you burn through the audience quickly. The double edge of local marketing: it is easy to get a promotion to work and hard to keep it working, because you effectively have to reinvent the wheel about every 21 days. Hence the need for a systematic refresh order.
numbers: Promotions in local markets need refreshing about every 21 days.
applies_to: Local businesses and small niche markets.

### OC-10#10 | FRAMEWORK | topic: promotions-launches | also: paid-ads, money-models | fw: Promotion Refresh Order | novelty: canon | ts: 00:10:02
claim: When ads fatigue, refresh in order of least to most operational drag: creative, body copy, headline/name, duration, enhancer, and finally the monetization structure.
detail: 1. Creative: images or video; offer identical. 2. Body copy: winning copy survives several creative refreshes, then needs rewriting. 3. Headline or wrapper: rename (six-week lean challenge → six-week tone challenge; free six-week weight-loss program → free 42-day detox; "holiday hangover" → "new year, new you"). 4. Duration: change only the unit (one month → 28 days or four weeks; 7 days → one week), which changes nothing in the business, or the real length (one month → six weeks), which needs updated contracts, sales team and fulfilment. 5. Enhancer: add or change a free or discounted component to tip fence-sitters. 6. Monetization structure: the series of offers and price points; hardest, alters the business core. Principle: least cost and disruption while feeling new.

### OC-10#11 | BENCHMARK | topic: promotions-launches | also: paid-ads | fw: Promotion Refresh Order | novelty: canon | ts: 00:14:06
claim: Hormozi's refresh cadence: creative about every seven days, copy every two to three weeks, headline about quarterly, duration once or twice a year, enhancer usually constant, monetization structure every two to three years.
detail: The items at the top of the refresh order are changed most often because they are cheapest to change; the deeper ones rarely. Once a monetization structure works — the series of offers given to prospects and their price points — they seldom change it.
numbers: Creative ~7 days; body copy ~2–3 weeks; headline ~quarterly; duration 1–2×/year; enhancer mostly constant; monetization structure ~every 2–3 years.

### OC-10#12 | DECISION_RULE | topic: promotions-launches | also: pricing | fw: Promotion Refresh Order | novelty: canon | ts: 00:12:21
claim: Hormozi favours free or massive discounts as enhancers and, in his opinion, mild discounts like 10–20% off do not change buyer behaviour.
detail: Hormozi is not a fan of mild discounts: he does not think 10% or 20% off makes someone buy who otherwise would not. Free offers and very large discounts do move people who are on the fence or would otherwise ignore the promotion. When refreshing the enhancer, restate the same economics in a new way: a $1 trial becomes a free start or free trial, "99% off" (or a distinctive figure like 99.2% off), or "no money down". Changing the enhancer alone also refreshes the creative angle.
numbers: 10–20% discounts, in his view, ineffective; examples: $1 trial ↔ free trial ↔ 99%/99.2% off ↔ no money down.

### OC-10#13 | TACTIC | topic: promotions-launches | also: offers | fw: Promotion Refresh Order | novelty: canon | ts: 00:11:33
claim: Changing only the unit of time in an offer name (one month → 28 days or four weeks; seven days → one week or hours) makes a promotion feel new without changing anything in the business.
detail: This is the cheap form of duration change: the program is the same length, only the metric changes, yet the offer looks fresh and can feel more unique. An actual length change (one month → six weeks) is heavier, because contracts, sales scripts and fulfilment (possibly extra weeks of delivery) must be updated. Hormozi repeats that time is a huge component of value, so how you name and bucket time can noticeably refresh an offer in prospects' minds.
