### POD-0606#01 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: hiring-talent | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:00:15
claim: Buy your time back in order: replace the task that is easiest and cheapest to replace first, then refill the freed time with work that earns more.
detail: Look at the calendar, find which of delivery, selling, promoting or admin is most replaceable in the market at the lowest price, outsource that one, and spend the hours on higher-earning work. Repeat until all time is bought back and only top-leverage work remains. Leverage means getting more out than you put in.

### POD-0606#02 | TACTIC | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:01:46
claim: To move a reluctant owner to delegate, confront them directly with the logic that the current use of time produces the current result.
detail: Hormozi compares it to someone wanting a different body while eating the same diet. Owners often hold the delusion that nobody could do the job as well. His counter: a hundred people doing that task full time would almost surely beat the owner who does it alongside other work.

### POD-0606#03 | DECISION_RULE | topic: sales-team | also: hiring-talent, operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-example | ts: 00:03:04
claim: When a first hire fails, rephrase the lesson as your own skill gap ("I do not know how to recruit, train or manage this role"), not as "this role does not work."
detail: Example: a small owner hands sales to the first salesperson, who fails because the owner has no hiring or management skills yet. The false lesson, "salespeople do not work" or "nobody sells like I do," then blocks growth until it is dropped. The gap version is solvable and repeats at every level up.

### POD-0606#04 | PRINCIPLE | topic: management-ops | also: operator-mindset | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:05:41
claim: How hard a function is to give up depends on how closely the owner ties their identity and worth to it.
detail: Ego is domain-specific: a founder may be humble in one area and proud in another. Owners driven by promotion or product often outsource finance without trouble but resist handing over product or promotion. The delegation problem is therefore mostly mental.

### POD-0606#06 | FRAMEWORK | topic: strategy-positioning | also: leads-fundamentals | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:07:14
claim: The best businesses have a built-in compounding vehicle; there are three: capital allocation, customers who bring customers, and salespeople who each produce a steady amount.
detail: Capital: each new facility returns about 50% on the money, so ask how many can be opened. Customers: if each brings 1.3 more, growth continues while quality holds. People: in a transactional business like solar sales, if each salesperson brings two roofs a month, adding salespeople compounds output. The test is whether the business will be massive in 30 years.
numbers: 50% return per new facility; 1.3 referred customers per customer; 2 roofs/month per salesperson.
### POD-0606#07 | DECISION_RULE | topic: leads-fundamentals | also: retention-ltv | fw: Referral Rate minus Churn | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:39
claim: Use the first acquisition channel to gather feedback and improve the product, and scale only once customers are bringing friends.
detail: Referrals are his own litmus test for whether the product is good enough; after that, marketing is enhanced and you can outcompete rivals who chased the next sale. If a product isn't good enough, sometimes the hard conversation is that the business may need to dial back a little rather than scale.
### POD-0606#08 | PRINCIPLE | topic: unit-economics | also: paid-ads | fw: CAC | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:11:39
claim: Promotion-led founders who scale a mediocre product hit a plateau of high revenue and low profit, because they move to colder audiences while ad costs keep rising.
detail: Marketing and sales skill alone can reach a few million to about $10M. Beyond that, acquisition cost rises with no referral stream to offset it. Great products "front-load" the effort, while a quickly built mediocre one must be promoted forever. The common hidden reason people earn less is that their product is not as good as they think.
numbers: Promotion skill alone: a few million to ~$10M.

### POD-0606#09 | DECISION_RULE | topic: product-delivery | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:10:42
claim: For him, a product was ready when anything he would add or subtract would make it worse, though he says that is not always the case; for software, aim for a consistent cadence of improvement.
detail: A book has clear ends. For software the aim is ongoing improvement, but until product-market fit shows (a share of customers bringing friends) it is still not right. He gives spending two years perfecting a book as a hypothetical example.
### POD-0606#10 | PRINCIPLE | topic: strategy-positioning | also: - | fw: - | novelty: new-detail | ts: 00:14:52
claim: Obsess over customers, not competitors; small competitors usually both have weak products, and a larger buyer sees them as one avatar to merge.
detail: Private equity that finds three companies serving the same avatar often buys and combines them. The main cost of competition is attention spent on rivals instead of customers. A rival he assumed hostile turned out never to have thought of him.

### POD-0606#12 | DEFINITION | topic: operator-mindset | also: management-ops | fw: - | novelty: new-framework | ts: 00:20:09
claim: Intelligence is the speed of learning, and learning is a new behavior under the same condition; a trait is a learnable skill.
